Pla Ra Lon (ปลาร้าหลน)

Fermented fish, simmered and strained, then cooked thick in coconut cream with pork, lemongrass and chillies — a lon, the dipping relish Thais eat with a platter of raw vegetables.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 6 as a shared relish · ~45 minutes

The Relish That Names Its Technique

หลน (lon) is one of the oldest words in the Thai kitchen: a technique of cooking in thick coconut cream, seasoned to all three tastes — sour, salty, sweet — reduced to something almost dry, and eaten as a เครื่องจิ้ม, a dipping relish, with fresh or blanched vegetables. Blogger บักอ้าย's family recipe blog lists the canon — lon tao jeow (fermented bean paste), lon pla ra, lon fermented tofu, lon salted crab — and calls it the dish he asked his mother for whenever she cooked rice. Kapook's ชายกาง, whose recipe this page uses, puts pla ra lon second only to tao jeow lon on the central Thai rice table — and beats it, he says, on smell and flavour.

The name carries the Isaan ingredient into the central Thai kitchen. ปลาร้า (pla ra) — freshwater fish fermented in salt and rice bran — is the northeast's larder, but the lon treatment is Bangkok-and-the-plain cooking. The fish must first be เกรอะ-ed (grae): simmered with lemongrass, galangal and kaffir leaves until it falls apart, then strained, bones and herbs thrown away. What survives is a liquid that keeps all the deep salt of pla ra and none of the mud. That liquid, reduced in coconut cream, is the whole dish.

And it is eaten like a nam phrik, not a curry: thick, by the spoonful, wrapped in raw vegetables. Kapook's vegetable platter is the real recipe within the recipe — white turmeric, young makok leaves, cucumber, pea aubergines, winged beans — a crudités tradition that makes the relish half dip, half salad.

Wongnai's catfish pot: 1 cup pla ra boiled in 1 cup water until dissolved, strained, then 2 cups coconut milk with lemongrass, krachai, young galangal, 10 spur chillies and kaffir — catfish fillets cooked through at the end, no tamarind, no sugar. TrueID's ปลาร้าทรงเครื่อง: 500 g catfish, 600 g coconut milk, 8 tbsp boiled pla ra water and tamarind, with bamboo shoots, long beans and pea aubergines cooked into the lon. Add catfish to Kapook's pork version and it becomes ทรงเครื่อง too.

The Method

  1. The grae (เกรอะ). Put the pla ra in a pot and cover it with tail coconut milk. Add the bias-cut lemongrass, mature galangal and torn kaffir leaves, and simmer on medium heat for about five minutes until the fish disintegrates. Strain everything out — bones, leaves, fish — and keep only the liquid. This step, Kapook's cook explains, is what takes the raw edge off the pla ra and loads the liquid with the herbs that will flavour the lon. Wongnai's version does the same thing in plain water.
  2. The paste. Pound the finely sliced lemongrass and young galangal together into a rough, moist paste. This is the only pounding the dish asks for — it is not a curry paste, just the aromatics that will bloom in the cream.
  3. The cream. Heat the head coconut milk and stir in the pounded aromatics. When it comes to the boil, add the finely sliced kaffir leaves. TrueID's version warns at this stage: keep the coconut milk smooth and unbroken — no splitting.
  4. The pork and the khao mak. Lower the heat and add the minced pork, stirring so it disperses through the cream. Then the khao mak — fermented sweet rice — which Kapook's cook credits with the round, even sweetness and the body that binds all the flavours together. It is the one ingredient none of the other sources use.
  5. The seasoning. Add half the grae liquid first — it is extremely salty — then the seasoning powder, palm sugar and tamarind. Taste and correct: the cook's rule is that every taste must be strong (ทุกรสชาติจะต้องเข้ม). Only add more grae if it still needs salt.
  6. The finish. Stir in the thick-cut spur chillies and shallots, then the remaining kaffir. Drop in whole bird's eye chillies last, and take the pot off the heat — they should sit whole on top, not cook through. Serve thick, almost dry, with the raw vegetable platter, and let everyone spoon the lon into leaves and cucumber. Leftovers keep, and the flavour only deepens overnight.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The protein. Minced pork (Kapook), catfish fillets (Wongnai, TrueID), or both together — Kapook notes that adding catfish upgrades the dish to ปลาร้าทรงเครื่อง, "with everything". TrueID's whole recipe is the ทรงเครื่อง school, with the vegetables cooked into the lon itself.

The vegetables. Kapook serves them raw on the side — the dipping platter is the point. TrueID cooks bamboo shoots, long beans and pea aubergines into the lon, turning the relish into a kind of thick herb stew. Both are called the same dish.

The sour. Kapook uses three tablespoons of concentrated tamarind; TrueID one and a half; Wongnai none at all. The fermented fish supplies the salt, the coconut the sweet, and each kitchen decides how sour the balance should be.

The sweet. Kapook sweetens with palm sugar and khao mak, the fermented sweet rice that is the recipe's signature. Wongnai and TrueID add no sugar at all — coconut milk alone carries the sweetness. Three different answers to the same question.

The grae. Kapook simmers the pla ra in tail coconut milk with lemongrass, mature galangal and kaffir; Wongnai boils it in plain water. TrueID skips the step entirely and pours in ready-boiled pla ra water. Everyone strains the bones out — how much flavour goes in before the strain is the variable.

The chilli finish. Kapook adds thick-cut spur chillies and shallots late, whole bird's eye chillies last of all, after the heat is off. Wongnai boils whole or chunked spur chillies from the start. TrueID adds its mixed chillies at the very end, after the vegetables are cooked. When the heat goes in decides whether it bites or melts.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Swapping pla ra for fish sauce. The grae step — fermented whole fish simmered with herbs and strained — is the dish's foundation. Fish sauce gives you salt and none of the depth. If you can't find pla ra, the honest answer is to cook a different relish, not dilute this one.
  • Treating it as a curry or soup. A lon is thick, nearly dry, and eaten by the spoonful with raw vegetables. Western versions that add stock or serve it over rice have made a different dish — a nam phrik's whole job is to be the dense, salty counterweight to a pile of leaves and vegetables.
  • Omitting the vegetable platter. Half the dish is the crudités: white turmeric, makok leaves, cucumber, winged beans, pea aubergines. The lon alone, eaten like a sauce, is just a very salty dip.
  • Splitting the coconut milk. TrueID's recipe is explicit about keeping the cream smooth and unbroken. High heat, curry-style frying until the oil separates, is the opposite of what this dish wants.
  • Rushing the grae. Boiling pla ra until it disintegrates and straining it is not optional fussiness — it is the step that removes the bones and the raw funk. Skimming "pla ra water" out of a jar skips the herb simmer that flavours the whole relish.
  • Sugar by default. Wongnai and TrueID make excellent lon with no added sugar at all. If a version "balances" with a spoonful of white sugar, it has missed that the sweet comes from coconut cream and, in Kapook's version, fermented rice.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Pla ra (ปลาร้า): Thai grocers sell it as whole fermented fish or minced in jars — gourami (ปลากระดี่) if you can find it. There is no Western substitute; fish sauce is not one.
  • Coconut milk: full-fat canned. Head coconut milk (หัวกะทิ) is the thick cream that separates to the top of an unshaken can — chill the tin, open it cold, and scoop the cream off. Tail milk is what remains.
  • Khao mak (ข้าวหมาก): fermented sweet rice, sold at Thai grocers, often near the desserts. If you cannot find it, add a little more palm sugar and accept the lon will be thinner and less round — say it plainly, it is not the same.
  • Lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime leaves: fresh or frozen at Thai grocers; kaffir freezes particularly well.
  • Catfish (ปลาดุก): frozen at Thai grocers if you want the ทรงเครื่อง version; otherwise any firm white fish fillet holds up to the gentle simmer.
  • The platter: cucumber, green beans, winged beans (when in season at Asian grocers), pea aubergines and fresh Thai chillies are all findable in the UK. White turmeric (ขมิ้นขาว) is the one to hunt for at Thai grocers — young ginger is not the same root but works as a crunchy stand-in.
