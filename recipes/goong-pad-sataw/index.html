Recipe · Thailand · The Stink Bean That Earns Its Heat

Goong Pad Sataw (กุ้งผัดสะตอ)

The south's loudest stir-fry: stink beans and prawns fried hard in southern red curry paste — heat first, salt next, and just enough sugar to cut both.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 2 · ~40 minutes

The Bean That Fights Back

สะตอ (sataw) is Thailand's most polarising ingredient — a tree bean so pungent that Thai cooks joke about the smell on your breath, and Kapook's recipe copy admits the "เหม็นเขียว" (green stink) outright before promising you will forget it exists "once you have tried a plate with hot rice". Southern cooks never doubted it: the bean's bitter-green snap against hot curry paste and sweet prawns is exactly the contrast the dish is built on.

The paste does the real work. The proper southern version — พริกแกงใต้ — is pounded from dried chillies, lemongrass, galangal, fresh turmeric, kaffir lime zest and shrimp paste, and it is not a mild thing. A Pantip cook, posting at the start of sataw season (and noting the early beans "are a little pricey"), gives the honest instruction: use southern paste "if you can find it" — it comes out "เข้มข้นจัดจ้าน", deeper and sharper. The flavour rule every source agrees on is foods313's: เผ็ดนำ เค็มตาม หวานตัด — heat leads, salt follows, sugar only cuts.

And the stir-fry itself has one non-negotiable: the paste must be fried until cooked through and fragrant before anything else touches the pan. Aroifin's cook also blanches the shelled beans and shocks them in cold water first, and warns to split every bean to check for the small green worm that likes the plump ones — details that separate the home version from the restaurant pot.

The southern paste, pounded from scratch (Aroifin): 40 g dried red chillies, 40 g lemongrass, 40 g shallots, 30 g garlic, 20 g fresh turmeric, 10 g galangal, 3 g kaffir lime zest, 15 g peppercorns, 5 g salt, 20 g shrimp paste — chillies and salt first, then the aromatics, shrimp paste last. The Kapook paste school: no curry paste at all — 10 cloves Thai garlic, 5 fresh and 5 dried red chillies, 3 shallots and 1 tbsp shrimp paste pounded coarse, then fried with oyster sauce, fish sauce and palm sugar (a second Kapook version pounds dried shrimp into the same base and adds minced pork). The Wongnai pot: garlic, 20 bird's eye chillies, shrimp paste and palm sugar pounded together, finished with fish sauce and a spoon of lime juice.

The Method

  1. The paste. Pound the southern curry paste, or open a jar. If you pound: dried chillies and salt first until fine, then lemongrass, shallots, garlic, turmeric, galangal and kaffir lime zest, then the shrimp paste to bind it.
  2. The beans. Shell the sataw and split each bean in half — aroifin says check inside, the plump beans nearly always hide a small green worm. Blanch the split beans about a minute in boiling water, then shock them in cold water; if you bought pre-shelled beans that have gone soft, soaking them 20 minutes in cold water brings the crunch back.
  3. The fry. Heat a little oil in a wok over medium-low and fry the paste until it is cooked through and fragrant. Every source treats this as the dish's hinge: foods313 says the paste must be fully cooked and aromatic, or the southern flavour never arrives; Pantip's cook adds a splash of warm water here so the paste does not catch and burn.
  4. The prawns. Add the prawns and stir until they are just about done — they finish with the beans.
  5. The beans, fast. Turn the heat up, add a splash of water, and throw in the sataw. Stir only long enough to warm the beans through — foods313's rule is a "flash of heat" (สะดุ้งความร้อน); cook them long and they turn soft and lose the point of the dish.
  6. The balance. Season with fish sauce and sugar — heat leads, salt follows, sugar only to cut. One stir to coat and off the heat. Pantip's cook tears in shredded kaffir lime leaves at this point if he has them. Serve immediately with hot jasmine rice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The paste. Aroifin and foods313 build the dish on พริกแกงใต้, southern red curry paste — pounded from dried chillies, turmeric, galangal, kaffir zest and shrimp paste. Pantip recommends it but makes do with red curry paste. Both Kapook recipes and Wongnai skip curry paste entirely and pound a quick garlic-chilli-shallot-shrimp-paste base instead. Same dish, two kitchens.

The beans' preparation. Aroifin blanches the shelled beans and shocks them in cold water. Pantip's cook soaks them in water "so the beans plump up" — for anyone who minds the green stink, he suggests the blanch-and-soak too. The other four recipes throw the raw beans straight into the wok.

How cooked is cooked? foods313 is strict: the beans get only a flash of heat so they stay crisp. Kapook and Wongnai simply say "stir until cooked". Ten extra seconds is the difference between the two outcomes.

The seasoning. Fish sauce plus palm sugar is the southern default (foods313, Kapook, Wongnai); Aroifin uses white sugar. Wongnai adds a spoon of lime juice at the end; Kapook's kapi-school recipe adds oyster sauce; Pantip's cook reaches for seasoning sauce and MSG along with the fish sauce and sugar.

The order. Almost everyone fries the paste first, then the prawns. foods313 does it backwards — prawns in the hot wok first, paste second — and still arrives at the same plate. Kapook's older recipe slips minced pork into the pan with the prawns, a common southern stretch when prawns are dear.

The prawns. From 6 prawns (Kapook) to 10 (foods313) to 150–200 g (Aroifin, Wongnai) for the same two-portion dish. Nobody measures twice; the prawn is the sweet counterweight, not the subject.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Swapping the beans. A version made with green beans or edamame "because stink beans are hard to find" is not this dish — the bitter-green snap of sataw against the hot paste is the entire structure. Find the real thing (frozen is fine) or make a different stir-fry.
  • Cooking the sataw through. Western stir-fry habits say cook the vegetable. Thai sources say a flash of heat — long-cooked sataw goes soft and the dish loses its reason to exist.
  • Under-frying the paste. Curry paste stirred in with the prawns at the end tastes raw and floury. It must be fried alone first, until cooked through and fragrant — every source's first real step.
  • Using generic "red curry" paste. Southern paste is its own thing — turmeric, black pepper, galangal, kaffir zest, shrimp paste, and serious heat. A mild panang-style paste makes a completely different, sweet dish.
  • Taming it. Recipes that "adjust to taste with a pinch of chilli" miss the point: เผ็ดนำ — the heat is the first flavour, by design. The dish is southern; the south does not apologise.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Sataw (stink beans): frozen at Thai and Southeast Asian grocers, usually already shelled and split — that is exactly what you want. Fresh pods appear briefly in autumn (Pantip's season-start post is an October one) at bigger Thai grocers. No good substitute; if you cannot find sataw, pick another dish.
  • Southern curry paste (น้ำพริกแกงใต้): labelled "southern Thai curry paste" at Thai grocers and online Thai supermarkets; the from-scratch paste above is a 10-ingredient pound and worth doing once.
  • Prawns: raw shell-on or peeled from any UK supermarket — the sweet ones; Thai sources use fresh tiger or white prawns but any decent raw prawn works.
  • Shrimp paste (กะปิ): Thai grocers; Pantip's cook insists on the good kind — fragrant, not too salty, not black.
  • Fresh turmeric and galangal: both at well-stocked Thai grocers, or frozen; ground turmeric is not a substitute for the paste.
  • Serve with jasmine rice; a fried egg on the side is the standard southern upgrade when you want a bigger plate.
