Recipe · Thailand · The Rice-Shop Classic That Smells Like Garlic Oil

Moo Tod Kratiem (หมูทอดกระเทียม)

Pork marinated in sam klor and Thai seasoning sauces, fried until golden, then buried under crispy fried garlic — the dish whose smell sells it before you see it.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 2–3 · ~25 minutes

The Dish That Names Its Two Ingredients

หมูทอดกระเทียม reads straight across: หมู (moo, pork) ทอด (tod, fried) กระเทียม (kratiem, garlic). There is no sauce, no curry, no vegetables doing the work — just pork, garlic, white pepper and the seasoning sauces every Thai kitchen keeps by the stove. Kapook's editors open their recipe by noting that even people who claim not to eat garlic turn their heads when a pan of it hits the oil.

It is the workhorse of the rice shop (ร้านตามสั่ง), the answer to "what should I order" at lunch, and its territory is marked: moo tod kratiem is the dry, golden, fried version piled with crispy garlic — not to be confused with moo kratiem (หมูกระเทียม), the saucy stir-fried cousin with its garlic oil pooled glossy around the meat. A Pantip thread exists purely to complain that too many rice shops blur the two.

Underneath the garlic is one of Thai cooking's oldest building blocks: สามเกลอ (sam klor, "three friends") — coriander root, garlic and white peppercorns pounded together. Wongnai and Pantip's Mai both pound their own and work it into the marinade; Aroifin's commercial recipe splits the same trio across the marinade and the fried-garlic topping. The pork is always marinated, and the marinade almost always leans on fresh milk or starch to keep the lean meat tender through the fry.

The Recipe (restaurant-style home version, from Wongnai)

The crispy shop version (Aroifin): 500 g fatty pork collar cut in 1 cm strips, marinated 30 minutes to overnight with white pepper, pork seasoning powder, palm and white sugar, a pinch of MSG, oyster sauce, light soy, fish sauce, 80 g fresh milk and 100 g crispy frying flour (แป้งทอดกรอบ) — then deep-fried in palm oil, heat raised at the end so the pork sheds its oil. The soft pan version (Pantip): 200 g pork collar, one heaped tablespoon each of oyster sauce and light soy, sugar and a pounded sam klor of ten Thai garlic cloves, one coriander root and white peppercorns — marinated 30 minutes, fried in the oil left from frying garlic, finished with a splash of dark soy. The lazy version (Kapook): any pork, fried in a pan with sugar, fish sauce, pork seasoning powder, seasoning sauce, oyster sauce and pepper, then tossed with ready-made fried garlic — add water if you want it saucy.

The Method

  1. The marinade. Toss the bite-size pork with the oyster sauce, seasoning sauce, fish sauce, dark soy, sam klor, tapioca starch and milk. Wongnai leaves it just 10 minutes; Pantip's Mai gives it 30 in the fridge; Aroifin says 30 minutes to overnight. The milk and starch are the tenderness insurance — lean loin fried naked turns hard.
  2. The oil. Heat enough oil to cover the pork. Wongnai makes the case for the deep bath: submerged pork cooks tender and the oil spatters less. Medium heat — this is a patient fry, not a sear.
  3. The fry. Fry in batches, and do not stir at first: Wongnai warns the pork sticks if you poke it before it's half done. Once the pieces are about halfway there, the minced garlic goes in — this is Wongnai's trick, adding it when the pork is nearly cooked so the garlic browns without burning.
  4. The garlic (Pantip's way). The alternative, used by Pantip, Cookpad and Aroifin: fry the garlic first on low heat until golden, lift it out, and split it — half gets tossed through the pork in the final minute, half is sprinkled over the top. Aroifin's warning applies to both roads: lift the garlic the moment it turns gold, because it keeps darkening after it leaves the oil.
  5. The finish. Raise the heat just before lifting the pork — Aroifin's anti-grease step, the sudden heat pushes the oil back out of the meat. Drain on a rack or paper, pile the fried garlic on top, and serve with hot rice, a fried egg and cucumber. Aroifin serves its version with sticky rice; Wongnai and Pantip with jasmine.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The cut. Pork loin (Wongnai), fatty collar (Pantip, Aroifin), "whatever part you like" (Kapook, Cookpad). Pantip's Mai argues for collar on tenderness alone — she claims it never comes out tough, so you can skip the milk. Loin needs the milk-and-starch help.

How the garlic enters. Wongnai adds raw minced garlic to the oil when the pork is nearly done. Pantip, Cookpad and Aroifin fry the garlic separately first, then toss and top. Kapook shortcuts to shop-bought fried garlic stirred in at the end. All agree the garlic must never be allowed to burn — it is the dish's whole smell.

Deep fry or pan fry. Wongnai and Aroifin deep-fry in a bath of oil; Kapook, Cookpad and Pantip shallow-fry in a pan. The deep fry is the rice-shop texture — crisp outside, tender inside; the pan versions come out softer and darker, almost caramelised at the edges.

The coating. Aroifin coats the pork in crispy frying flour (and flour in the fried-garlic mix); Wongnai uses tapioca starch; Pantip and Cookpad use none. Kapook sits out of this argument entirely — no coating, ready-made fried garlic, done in minutes.

Sam klor or plain garlic. Wongnai and Pantip pound the full trio — coriander root, garlic, white peppercorn. Aroifin's commercial recipe drops the coriander root and leans on seasoning powder instead. Kapook and Cookpad keep it bare-bones: garlic and pepper, no coriander root at all.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • No fried garlic on top. English-language recipes treat garlic as a marinade ingredient and stop. The pile of golden fried garlic over the finished pork is not garnish — Thai sources split it between the toss and the topping because the dish is built on garlic in three layers: paste, fry, finish.
  • Salt-and-pepper as the whole seasoning. Western versions marinate in salt and white pepper alone. Thai recipes stand the pork in oyster sauce, fish sauce, seasoning sauce and sugar — that's where the savoury-sweet depth comes from, and the dark soy is what gives the rice-shop colour.
  • The two-hour room-temperature marinade. A popular English recipe marinates raw pork on the counter for two hours. Thai sources marinate 10–30 minutes (or overnight, refrigerated) — the pork absorbs the sauces fast, and food-safety aside, hours on the counter changes nothing about a thin marinade.
  • Skipping the tenderness step. Wongnai and Aroifin both put fresh milk in the marinade — Thai pork for this dish is lean and fried hard, and the milk (with starch or crispy flour) is what keeps it from turning to leather.
  • Calling it a stir-fry. Moo tod kratiem is a fry — dry and golden. The saucy stir-fry version is moo kratiem (หมูกระเทียม), a different dish with the same two words in a different order, and Thai diners (as the Pantip thread shows) can tell which one they got.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Thai garlic: smaller, more pungent cloves than UK garlic; a "handful" of Thai garlic is roughly a large UK bulb and a half. Asian grocers sell Thai garlic loose; supermarket garlic works but use more.
  • Coriander root (รากผักชี): not in UK supermarkets — Thai grocers sell coriander with the roots on. If you can only get bunches with stems, use the thick stems pounded with the garlic; the root is earthier but the stem gets close.
  • Seasoning sauce (ซอสปรุงรส): thin, salty, savoury — Healthy Boy or Maggi Seasoning at Thai grocers. Light soy with a pinch of MSG and sugar approximates it.
  • Pork loin or collar: any UK pork steak or loin; go for pork shoulder/collar steaks if you want the fattier Pantip texture.
  • Crispy frying flour (แป้งทอดกรอบ): tempura flour or a rice-flour-based frying mix does the job for the Aroifin crispy version; tapioca starch (cornflour is a fine substitute) for the Wongnai marinade.
