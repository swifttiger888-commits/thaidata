Kuay Tiew Lord (ก๋วยเตี๋ยวหลอด)

Fresh wide rice noodle sheets steamed and wrapped around a five-spice braise of pork belly, tofu, shiitake and dried shrimp — cut into rolls, scattered with fried garlic, and eaten with a sweet-sour dark dipping sauce.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 6 · ~2 hours

The Roll That Braises Its Own Filling

ก๋วยเตี๋ยวหลอด takes its name from the shape: หลอด — the tube, the straw. A fresh, wide rice noodle sheet, the same เส้นใหญ่ that goes into pad see ew, but sold uncut and steamed rather than boiled, is rolled around its filling and eaten soft and warm. Thai noodle stalls sell the sheets fresh every morning, and every source here warns the same thing: the sheet must be fresh. A day-old sheet dries, cracks when folded, and tears when you roll it.

The classic filling is the ทรงเครื่อง — the "full dress". Pork, firm tofu, shiitake and dried shrimp, braised together until the pork is soft and the sauce has taken the colour of palm-sugar caramel. Phol Tantasathien's recipe is explicit about that colour: you caramelise the palm sugar until it darkens so the braise turns deep amber on its own — dark soy is a seasoning, not the colour.

Thai sources split the dish into three families, and all three call themselves the same name. Pim's braised version simmers pork belly a full hour and a half with five-spice. The wok version — Wongnai's, Aroifin's, Ajinomoto's — stir-fries minced pork with the same cast of tofu, shiitake and dried shrimp in twenty minutes. And Phol's bowl version never rolls anything: the same braised topping, quail eggs and all, is ladled over steamed sheets dressed with garlic oil, the way street carts in Bangkok serve it. The roll is one shape the dish takes; the sheets, the filling and the dark sauce are what make it the dish.

The dipping sauce (all sources): sugar, vinegar, dark soy, light soy, salt and a little water, heated just until the sugar dissolves — Pim grinds bird chillies in (65 g sugar, 60 g vinegar, 30 g dark soy, 15 g light soy, 35 g water), Wongnai and Ajinomoto slice fresh red chillies into theirs, and Kapook swaps in a spoon of sesame oil. Serve with fried garlic, chopped spring onion and coriander; pork crackling if you have it.

The wok version (Wongnai): fry crushed garlic, coriander root and white pepper in oil; add 200 g minced pork, then shiitake, carrot, 50 g sweet radish (ไชโป๊ว), a block of fried tofu and 20 g dried shrimp; season with sugar, seasoning powder, light soy and oyster sauce; finish with bean sprouts and spring onion, roll into the sheets and steam. The bowl version (Phol): pork belly, quail eggs, shiitake and tofu braised in a litre of stock over a palm-sugar caramel; the steamed sheets are dressed with garlic oil and served in a bowl with dark soy and fried radish-garlic. From-scratch sheets (Kapook): 75 g rice flour, 50 g tapioca starch, 25 g potato starch and ½ tsp salt whisked with 400 ml warm water, steamed in a dim-sum steamer like khao kriap pak mor, wrapped around blanched prawns.

The Method

  1. The sauce, first. Sugar, vinegar, dark soy, light soy and water in a small pot over low heat, stirred until the sugar dissolves, brought to one boil and cooled. Pim makes it before anything else so the chillies can sit in it — the sauce is half the dish, and it tastes better once the flavours settle.
  2. The five-spice. Dry-toast the five-spice powder in the wok over low heat until fragrant — twenty seconds, no more — and set it aside. Toasting wakes it up; Pim's recipe does this before the caramel so the powder is ready the moment the braise needs it.
  3. The caramel. Melt the palm sugar in the wok with a spoon of water, stirring over low heat until it dissolves and turns golden brown. Then pour in hot water — Pim underlines it: cold water makes the hot sugar seize into a hard lump that never properly dissolves. This caramel is the braise's colour and half its sweetness.
  4. The braise. In go the pork belly, sliced shiitake, dried shrimp, tofu, coriander roots, the toasted five-spice, both soys, pepper, salt and the stock with the shrimp-soaking water. Boil, skim the scum off the surface, then drop the heat and simmer one to one-and-a-half hours until the pork is soft — topping up with stock or water if it dries. Taste and correct: the finished flavour is salty-first, sweet at the end, with dried shrimp and shiitake underneath.
  5. The steam. Steam the bean sprouts and the fresh noodle sheets together, four to five minutes — Ajinomoto gives three to five, Aroifin ten to fifteen; the rule every source agrees on is that over-steamed sheets go wet and flabby (Phol: แฉะ). Plate the bean sprouts, lay the sheet on top, spoon the braise over, scatter fried garlic and spring onion and coriander, and serve with the sauce — crackling on top if you want the full street-cart treatment.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Braised or stir-fried? Pim and Phol braise — pork belly simmered an hour-plus in a five-spice caramel broth. Wongnai, Aroifin and Ajinomoto stir-fry minced pork with the same cast in a wok, twenty minutes start to finish. Same ingredients, two speeds; the braise gives you soft pork and a deep amber sauce, the wok gives you the filling the stalls cook in volume.

The protein. Pork belly (Pim, Phol), minced pork (Wongnai, Aroifin), pork plus shrimp (Ajinomoto), shrimp alone in a dim-sum style (Kapook's prawn version, which also swaps the bought sheets for batter steamed like khao kriap pak mor). Pork and tofu together are the common ground — that is what makes it ทรงเครื่อง.

Where the bean sprouts go. Rolled inside with the filling (Wongnai, Aroifin, Ajinomoto) or steamed separately and plated underneath the sheet (Pim, Phol). The first way eats crunchy; the second keeps the sprouts out of the braise and off the hot sheet until the last moment.

Steam time. Three to five minutes (Pim, Ajinomoto) against ten to fifteen (Aroifin). Everyone steams — no one boils — and everyone agrees the danger is over-steaming, which turns the sheets to mush.

The sauce. The same core everywhere — sugar, vinegar, dark soy, light soy, salt — but Kapook adds sesame oil to a prawn-version sauce, Aroifin simmers it to a syrup and pours it over the cut rolls, and Pim grinds chillies in only after the sauce has cooled. Wongnai and Ajinomoto put the chilli straight in the pot. Whether you dip or pour is a matter of cart habit, not recipe.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Boiling the rolls. The sheets are steamed, always — every Thai source steams them (or, in Phol's bowl version, holds them over hot water in a steamer basket). Boiled, they turn to paste and the rolls collapse.
  • Rolling too tight — or too loose. Ajinomoto warns that a roll packed too tight bursts open in the steamer and the filling spills out; a loose roll unrolls on the plate. Roll firmly, tuck the ends, and let the steam finish the job.
  • Sweet chilli sauce. The sauce is dark soy, vinegar, sugar and salt — savoury-sweet, not a bottled sweet chilli. Serve the rolls with anything else and the dish loses its second half.
  • Dark soy as colouring. Phol's recipe makes the point: the braise gets its deep amber from the palm-sugar caramel, not from a glut of dark soy. Colour the dish with soy and it turns one-note and salty.
  • A pork-only filling. The ทรงเครื่อง — tofu, shiitake, dried shrimp, radish — is the definition. A mince-only roll is just a spring roll in a rice sheet; and if your filling is wet, Aroifin's tip applies: it must be dry with only a sheen of fat, or the rolls weep and split.
  • Old or dried sheets. The sheets are the dish, and they must be fresh. If yours have dried and stiffened, steam them briefly to soften before rolling (Aroifin) — but don't try to make the dish with dry rice noodles as if they were the same thing.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Fresh wide rice noodle sheets (เส้นใหญ่): sold at Thai grocers fresh daily, sometimes labelled kuay tiew lord sheets or "uncut wide rice noodles". If only the boiled-noodle pack is available, it is the same sheet — just steam, don't boil. Frozen sheets work in a pinch; steam to revive. Vietnamese rice paper is Kapook's last-resort shortcut (dip, fill, roll, brush with sesame oil, steam five minutes).
  • Dried shrimp (กุ้งแห้ง): any Thai or East Asian grocer. Keep the soaking water — it goes into the braise (Pim).
  • Five-spice powder (ผงพะโล้): standard Chinese five-spice from any supermarket is the same blend.
  • Soy sauces: you need both — light soy (ซีอิ๊วขาว) and dark soy (ซีอิ๊วดำ). Thai dark soy is sweeter and thinner than Chinese dark soy; the sauce recipe wants the salty dark kind (ซีอิ๊วดำเค็ม).
  • Tofu: firm white tofu block (เต้าหู้แข็ง) for the braise; Wongnai's wok version uses ready-fried tofu (เต้าหู้ทอด), which works in either.
  • Pork stock: any light stock; water with a cube in a pinch — the braise is deeply seasoned already.
  • Steamer: a bamboo steamer or metal steamer over simmering water, lined with parchment or a plate so the rolls don't stick; they only need a few minutes.
