Kai Tod Takrai (ไก่ทอดตะไคร้)

Chicken wings in an oyster-sauce marinade, deep-fried until golden with crisp fried lemongrass and kaffir lime leaves piled on top — the garnish is the point, and you eat it.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 2 · ~55 minutes

The Garnish You Eat

The name is flat and honest: ไก่ทอด (kai tod), fried chicken, plus ตะไคร้ (takrai), lemongrass. What the name doesn't say is that the lemongrass is not background flavouring. It is pounded, fried crisp, piled on top of the chicken and eaten with it — half the dish, not a sprig to push aside. It sits in the Thai canon as กับแกล้ม, drinking food for beer gardens and late-night tables, and as a home side dish over rice.

The trick that makes the dish work is the order of the fry. Wongnai Cooking's recipe fries the lemongrass and kaffir lime leaves first, before the chicken goes in, so the oil itself takes on their scent — the chicken fries in lemongrass-perfumed oil, and the fried aromatics become the garnish. Cookpad cook Pui Bali takes the lemongrass further, pounding it into fine golden threads and dusting them with a little crispy flour before they hit the oil; her commenters couldn't work out how it was made.

Thai sources split into two schools. The garnish school — Wongnai Cooking, Cookpad and EzyThaiCooking — fries the lemongrass and lime leaves alongside the chicken, all of it crisp. The marinade school — ThaiFoodEasy and the home recipe by Wongnai cook Bambibam — pounds the lemongrass into the marinade itself and fries a second batch on top. Either way, the lemongrass appears twice: once as perfume, once as crunch. The recipe below is EzyThaiCooking's, written for two and exactly the size of a British supermarket pack of wings.

The party tray (Wongnai Cooking): 30 mid and tip wings marinated in only salt and ground pepper, tossed in 1 cup crispy flour and rested 15 minutes; 3 bundles of lemongrass cut in pieces and pounded into strands, 3 bundles of kaffir lime leaves and 20 dried chillies, each fried separately in the perfumed oil and scattered over the tray with น้ำจิ้มไก่. The threads (Cookpad, Pui Bali): ½ kg wings split at the joints, marinated in salt, pepper and chicken seasoning powder; the lemongrass pounded into fine strands, pulled apart, dusted with crispy flour and fried — chopsticks keep the strands from clumping, and the lime leaves join when the edges start to colour. The marinade school (ThaiFoodEasy): 3 tbsp finely pounded lemongrass and 3 tbsp finely shredded lime leaves inside an oyster-soy-sugar-pepper marinade, the wings flour-dusted and fried, served with plum sauce (น้ำจิ้มบ๋วย) or chicken dip. The garlic version (Wongnai UGC, Bambibam): 600 g thigh chunks with pounded garlic, coriander root and black pepper, 3 tbsp cornstarch instead of crispy flour.

The Method

  1. The marinade. Pound the lemongrass pieces in a mortar until bruised and broken, then toss with the chicken and everything else — oyster sauce, seasoning sauce, ground pepper and sugar. Marinate at least 30 minutes. The lemongrass pieces stay in the bowl; they get fried, not thrown away.
  2. The aromatics. Heat the oil in a wok over a medium heat. Lift the lemongrass and kaffir lime leaves out of the marinade and fry them until cooked through, then drain on kitchen paper. Watch the lemongrass closely — if it burns it turns bitter, and it is eaten with the chicken, not discarded. (Wongnai Cooking's tip: this first fry is also what scents the oil for the chicken.)
  3. The chicken. Fry the marinated chicken in the same oil until golden and crisp, then drain. The pieces are small — do not over-fry, or the wings dry out. Cookpad's flour trick works here too: a light dusting of crispy flour before frying keeps the crust crisp and stops the oil spitting.
  4. The serve. Arrange the fried lemongrass and lime leaves on the plate with the chicken and serve immediately with jaew and hot rice — or as it's meant at a bar table: on its own, one hand free for the beer.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The marinade. Wongnai Cooking and Cookpad keep it bare — salt and pepper, chicken powder at most — and let the aromatics carry the flavour. EzyThaiCooking and ThaiFoodEasy build a full marinade of oyster sauce, seasoning or soy sauce, sugar and pepper. Bambibam's Wongnai recipe goes the pounded-garlic, coriander-root and black-pepper route. Same dish, three different depths of chicken.

Lemongrass in or out. The garnish school (Wongnai Cooking, Cookpad, EzyThaiCooking) keeps the lemongrass out of the marinade and fries it as a crisp topping. The marinade school (ThaiFoodEasy, Bambibam) pounds it into the marinade and fries more on top — so the chicken itself tastes of lemongrass, not just the oil.

Lemongrass texture. Whole-ish pieces pounded to strands (Wongnai Cooking, EzyThaiCooking), fine shredded threads pulled apart one by one (Cookpad), or pounded nearly to paste (ThaiFoodEasy). The threads are the prettiest and the fussiest — Cookpad's comment section treats them like magic.

The dried chillies. Only Wongnai Cooking adds 20 dried chillies, fried until they puff and darken, to the topping. Every other source ends at lemongrass and lime leaves.

The dip. EzyThaiCooking and Wongnai Cooking serve jaew (น้ำจิ้มไก่), the smoky-tart pounded chilli dip. ThaiFoodEasy offers plum sauce (น้ำจิ้มบ๋วย) as the alternative. No source serves this without a dip.

The cut. Mid and tip wings (Wongnai Cooking), whole wings split at the joints (Cookpad, EzyThaiCooking), or thigh chunks for a bigger eat (Bambibam). All of them small pieces, all of them warned against over-frying.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Throwing the lemongrass away. Western instincts treat the lemongrass as spent flavouring to be fished out and binned. It is the garnish you eat — crisp, fragrant, and half the point of the dish. EzyThaiCooking's warning runs the other way: mind the fry so the lemongrass doesn't burn, because you will be eating it.
  • Burnt lemongrass. The classic failure. Over-fried lemongrass goes bitter and ruins the plate it sits on. Medium heat, drain early, watch the colour.
  • A wet batter. Thai recipes dust with dry crispy flour, never dip in egg-and-batter. A wet batter steams the aromatics instead of crisping them and turns the dish into generic fried chicken.
  • Skipping the kaffir lime leaves. The lime leaf is the lemongrass's partner — the second half of the perfume. Lemongrass alone is half a garnish.
  • Over-frying the wings. Every Thai source says the same thing: the pieces are small, so stop at golden. Two extra minutes and the wings dry out — EzyThaiCooking calls this out explicitly.
  • Bottled sweet chilli. Served with sweet chilli from a bottle, the dish tips cloying. The Thai pairing is jaew — fish sauce, lime, toasted rice powder, dried chilli — smoke and sour against the fried crust.
  • Jarred lemongrass paste. Paste cannot be pounded into strands or fried crisp. This dish needs fresh stalks, bruised in a mortar, not a spoonful from a jar.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Wings: 350 g is a standard supermarket pack — roughly 10–12 wings, exactly the size EzyThaiCooking's recipe is written for. Split whole wings at the joints, or buy mid-wings if your butcher stocks them.
  • Fresh lemongrass: Thai grocers, and Tesco, Sainsbury's and Waitrose all carry it. Two to four stalks is all this dish needs.
  • Kaffir lime leaves: Thai grocers sell them fresh or frozen; frozen is fine for frying. The larger supermarkets sometimes stock them in Thai meal kits.
  • Crispy frying flour (แป้งทอดกรอบ): Gogi or Gobek tempura flour at Thai grocers; otherwise half rice flour, half cornstarch does the job.
  • Seasoning sauce: Golden Mountain or Healthy Boy, at Thai grocers and online — the salt-sweet soy seasoning, not plain light soy.
  • Jaew: make it — toasted rice powder, lime, fish sauce, dried chilli flakes and spring onion — or buy Mae Pranom's bottled version at any Thai grocer.
