Recipe · Thailand · Isaan's Leaf-Green Curry

Kaeng Nor Mai (แกงหน่อไม้)

Bamboo shoots in bai ya nang leaf water, a pounded chilli paste, pounded rice and pla ra — green, fiery, and the curry Isaan eats with sticky rice.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · 3 servings · ~35 minutes

The Curry Isaan Grows Up On

แกงหน่อไม้ is named for its engine: nor mai, the bamboo shoot — but every Isaan kitchen adds the second half of the name, ใบย่านาง, the bai ya nang leaf, whose crushed, strained water becomes the entire broth. Kapook explains the pairing as food lore with a rule: bamboo shoots are hot in the Thai humoral system and eaten in quantity they bloat the stomach, so they must be countered with bai ya nang, which is cold. That is why, as Kapook puts it, the shoot and the leaf became inseparable.

The curry's colour is its own in-joke. Kapook's version confesses outright that the bowl "looks unappetising" — a cloudy, khaki green — and promises that the first spoon converts you anyway. The texture is where the cooks diverge: Wongnai's is thin and clear, nothing in it but shoots and herbs, while most pot-makers stir in ข้าวเบือ, raw sticky rice soaked and pounded to a slurry that thickens the broth softly — and, as Pim's kitchen blog notes, even tames the bitterness of bitter curries like gaeng wai.

The seasoning engine is pla ra, the fermented fish paste of the region — Kitty Chef's Pantip recipe states the rule plainly: the pla ra alone carries the pot, no fish sauce needed. Everything else in the bowl is vegetables and wild greens, and it is not a starter but the main event, eaten with a bamboo basket of hot sticky rice and, in Wongnai's version, grilled chicken on the side.

The mushroom pot (Pim, ครัวบ้านพิม): weights for a family batch — 20 bai ya nang leaves blended with half of 3 cups water, 5 shallots (60 g), 25–30 Karen chillies, 3 large lemongrass stalks, 170 g pre-boiled bamboo shoots, 120 g pumpkin, 160 g mixed mushrooms plus 110 g wood ear, 30 g cha-om, 30 g lemon basil, ½ cup pla ra, 2 tbsp fish sauce and 2 tbsp raw sticky rice soaked 1–2 hours for the khao buea. The thin bowl (Wongnai): no rice at all — 1 fresh shoot, 500 g bai ya nang leaves blended in 2 litres of water, 3 sliced shallots, 3 stalks sliced lemongrass, 10 red jinda chillies (half pounded, half whole), 1 cup pla ra and 1 cup lemon basil; boiled until the shoot loses its bitterness, served with sticky rice and grilled chicken. Kitty Chef's rule (Pantip via Kapook): pound the khao buea fine, pound chillies and shallots coarse, and let the pla ra alone do the seasoning — no fish sauce.

The Method

  1. The khao buea. Soak 1–2 tbsp of raw sticky rice in water 10–15 minutes, then pound it — Aroifin pounds it straight into the paste with the sliced lemongrass, chillies and shallots, all kept coarse. Pim soaks hers 1–2 hours and pounds it separately to a slurry; both agree it dissolves into the pot and thickens the broth without clouding it.
  2. The shoots. Shell the bamboo shoot down to the tender core and cut it into pieces or strips. Aroifin boils it 10 minutes; Wongnai's rule is to keep boiling — about 15 minutes — until it no longer tastes bitter, then lift it out. This parboil is not optional.
  3. The broth. Bring the bai ya nang water to the boil and add the parboiled shoots, boiling another 5–8 minutes until the pot returns to the boil. Wongnai and Pim blend the leaves with water and strain through muslin; Kapook crushes them by hand in a basin of water — same green, same mineral edge.
  4. The base. Add the boiled pla ra and boil 5 more minutes, then the paste, the pumpkin and the gourd for 1–2 minutes, and the straw mushrooms after that. Pim adds her paste and lemongrass first, then pumpkin and shoots, mushrooms only once the pumpkin is cooked.
  5. The balance. Taste before you season — Pim warns that pla ra brands differ in salt and funk, so top up with fish sauce only as needed; she adds the full 2 tbsp, Aroifin 1 tbsp plus a teaspoon of seasoning powder. Boil once more.
  6. The kaffir note. Aroifin's signature: kaffir lime leaves and cha-om go in together, boiled just 10–15 seconds so the citrus oil stays bright.
  7. The finish. Lemon basil last of all — Pim picks the leaves only, because the stems wrap themselves around your spoon — then the pot comes off the heat. Aroifin's tip for the herbs: add half while the curry still boils and the other half after the fire is out, for two layers of aroma. Eat hot with sticky rice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

To thicken or not. Wongnai's version is thin and clear — no rice anywhere in it. Kapook's basic recipe pounds soaked sticky rice into the paste. Kitty Chef, Aroifin and Pim use khao buea pounded separately, and even the soak time splits: Aroifin says 10–15 minutes, Pim 1–2 hours. The curry is genuinely made all three ways.

The garlic. Only Pim raises it: some regions pound garlic into the paste, and her friends' households in Surin and Loei do not — a regional split she files under "no drama". None of the other four pastes include garlic.

The lemongrass. Wongnai slices it into the paste; Pim cuts it short and drops it into the pot, noting that some cooks slice it small and pound it in with the paste instead; Kapook's basic recipe bruises it — and left it off the ingredient list entirely.

Kaffir lime leaves. Aroifin alone adds five leaves near the end of the cook. The other four recipes never touch them.

The seasoning. Kitty Chef is categorical: pla ra alone seasons the pot — no fish sauce ("แค่น้ำปลาร้าก็เอาอยู่"). Wongnai agrees, seasoning with nothing but pla ra. Aroifin and Pim add fish sauce on top, and Aroifin a spoon of seasoning powder besides. Kapook's basic recipe goes furthest, marking the pla ra itself optional.

The bai ya nang. Wongnai and Pim blend the leaves with water and strain through muslin; Kapook and Pim's traditional option crush them hard by hand in a big basin of water; Pim notes that in Isaan markets you can simply buy the ready-squeezed water in bags. Same broth, three levels of effort.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Adding coconut milk. Not one of the five Thai recipes uses it. The broth is bai ya nang leaf water — thin, green and mineral. Coconut milk makes it a different dish entirely.
  • Thickening with cornflour. When kaeng nor mai is thickened at all, it is with khao buea — raw rice soaked and pounded — which thickens softly and rounds the edge of the pla ra. Cornflour gives a glossy fake.
  • Skipping the parboil. The fresh shoot must be boiled until its bitterness is gone — Wongnai's test is to taste it. Raw or merely warmed bamboo shoots wreck the whole pot.
  • Simmering the herbs to death. Cha-om and lemon basil go in at the very end — Aroifin gives them 10–15 seconds, Pim turns the fire off right after they go in. A long simmer turns the greens soapy and grey.
  • Replacing pla ra with fish sauce. Fish sauce can top up salt once the pla ra has done its work, but it cannot replace it — the fermented fish is the dish's backbone, and Kitty Chef's rule is that it needs no help at all.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Bamboo shoots: tinned or vacuum-packed sliced shoots are standard in UK supermarkets and Chinese grocers and are already cooked — simmer them briefly instead of the full parboil. Fresh shoots appear at Thai grocers only in season.
  • Bai ya nang (ใบย่านาง): the one ingredient that defines the dish, and the hardest — fresh leaves rarely reach the UK. Frozen leaves at well-stocked Thai grocers or online Thai supermarkets are the route; Pim notes that even in Thailand many cooks buy the ready-squeezed water in bags.
  • Khao buea: make it from any glutinous rice — Thai sticky rice (ข้าวเหนียว) sits on every Thai grocer's shelf. Soak, pound, done.
  • Pla ra: bottled and shelf-stable at Thai grocers; Aroifin boils it before it goes in, which tames the raw edge — do the same.
  • Karen chillies are not exported; any fresh red bird's eye chillies stand in. Cha-om (acacia) is frozen at Thai grocers — the curry still works without it, though the garlicky-green note is missed.
