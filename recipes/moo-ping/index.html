Moo Ping (หมูปิ้ง)

The morning-market skewers: thin pork in a coriander-root and coconut-milk marinade, grilled over charcoal and basted glossy, eaten with sticky rice and the jaew chilli dip.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 4 · ~35 minutes + 3 hours marinating

The Breakfast Skewers

Before eight in the morning, every Thai town has the same scene: a charcoal grill at the roadside, thin strips of pork threaded onto bamboo, smoke and coconut in the air, and commuters buying them in pairs with a bag of sticky rice. หมูปิ้ง is breakfast — protein on a stick, sweet-savoury from the marinade, carried away in a plastic bag.

The marinade is small but specific. Coriander root pounded with garlic and white pepper is the base note — the root is the part western cooks throw away and Thai cooks build on. Coconut milk softens and sweetens; white soy and palm sugar round it; a drop of sweet dark soy gives the caramel colour. The final trick is the baste: coconut milk flicked over the skewers as they grill, so the surface glazes instead of drying.

The side is น้ำจิ้มแจ่ว — jaew: chilli flakes, fish sauce, tamarind, lime and spring onion. Sour, salty, hot — the sharp cut against the sweet pork. Street stalls in tourist areas sometimes hand over sweet chilli instead; the jaew is the real thing.

The Method

  1. Slice thin. Two centimetres wide, four to five long, one to three millimetres thick — thin enough for the marinade to reach everywhere and the fire to cook fast.
  2. Pound. Garlic, coriander roots and white pepper to a paste.
  3. Marinate. The paste with the green-cap sauce, white soy, sweet dark soy, palm sugar, sugar and coconut milk, massaged into the pork. At least 3 hours; overnight is better.
  4. Skewer. Soaked bamboo sticks, pork threaded flat and even.
  5. Grill and baste. Over charcoal, coconut milk flicked over the skewers as they cook — the glaze is the point.
  6. Jaew. Chilli flakes, fish sauce, palm sugar, tamarind water, lime juice and spring onion, stirred together.
  7. Serve. Hot skewers, sticky rice, jaew for dipping. The breakfast of champions.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The cut. Rump (aroifin's shop version) against pork neck (Wongnai's) — the neck is marbled and juicier, the rump slices cleaner. Both grill beautifully.

MSG in the jaew. The aroifin source lists it openly — street jaew has it, and the source says so. Home versions skip it. We've transcribed the source amount and flagged it.

The dip. Jaew is the Isaan standard; tourist-market stalls often serve sweet chilli instead. One is the dish's partner, the other is a shortcut.

Marinade time. Three hours minimum in the shop recipes; home cooks range from thirty minutes to overnight. The longer sit wins.

The baste. Coconut milk (Wongnai, aroifin) against plain oil at some stalls. Coconut is the flavourful choice — the baste is also the last seasoning.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Confusing it with satay. Moo ping has no peanut sauce, no turmeric, no curry powder. Different marinade, different side, different dish.
  • Sweet chilli by default. The jaew — tamarind, lime, chilli, fish sauce — is the real partner. Sweet chilli is the tourist-market substitution.
  • Thick chunks. The strips must be millimetres thin. Cubes on sticks grill dry outside and raw inside.
  • No coriander root. It is the marinade's backbone. Ground coriander seed is not a substitute — find the roots.
  • BBQ-sauce marinade. A smoky-sweet western bottle sauce replaces the whole flavour architecture with sugar and smoke.
  • No baste. Unbasted skewers dry out over the coals. The coconut glaze is what keeps them glossy and juicy.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Pork: shoulder or rump from any butcher, sliced thin. Neck (ask the butcher for collar) is the juiciest version.
  • Coriander roots: Thai grocers sell coriander with the roots attached — they're the part worth buying. Rinse them well; they carry the soil.
  • Green-cap seasoning sauce: Ros Dee brand, any Thai grocer. If it's missing, white soy plus a pinch of sugar gets near it — but the real bottle is two pounds.
  • Sweet dark soy: the thick, syrupy one (ตราช่อเย็น or similar) — it's for colour and gloss, not salt.
  • Chilli flakes for the jaew: Thai grocers' roasted flakes, coarser and smokier than supermarket crushed chillies.
  • Sticky rice: glutinous rice from Thai grocers — see the mango sticky rice page for steaming.
  • Charcoal if you can; a very hot grill pan otherwise. The smoke is part of the dish.
