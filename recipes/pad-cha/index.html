Pad Cha (ผัดฉ่า)

Clams through a screaming wok under a storm of fingerroot, green peppercorns, kaffir lime and fresh chillies — the seafood shop's loudest dish.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 2–3 · ~17 minutes

The Stir-Fry Built on Aromatics

ผัดฉ่า is the ahan tam sang dish with the biggest herb count per spoonful: กระชาย fingerroot, พริกไทยอ่อน green peppercorns on the stem, ใบมะกรูด kaffir lime leaves and two kinds of fresh chilli, all thrown through the wok in the final seconds. The TrueID roundup carries twenty-five versions of it — clams, prawns, squid, chicken, frog — because the dish is really the herb technique; the protein is whoever the shop has that day.

The base starts coarse: garlic, shallots and red Jinda chillies pounded together and fried until the wok smells, then the clams go in to open in the steam. Seasoning stays minimal — fish sauce, a little sugar, pepper, a splash of water — because the herbs are the flavour, not the sauce.

The Aroifin seafood version makes the point about order: the aromatics join after the protein is nearly done, so the fingerroot and peppercorns arrive at the table still bright. Overcooked, the herbs fade; timed right, the dish is a nose-hit of kaffir before the chilli lands.

The Recipe (serves 2–3 — the TrueID clam version)

Swaps (TrueID's 25 variants): prawns, squid or chicken for the clams. The Lemon8 home version fries a spoon of curry paste in the oil before the aromatics and adds holy basil at the end.

The Method

  1. The base. Pound the garlic, shallots and Jinda chillies coarse; slice the fingerroot.
  2. Fry and steam. Wok hard and hot, paste in until it smells, then the clams until they open.
  3. Season. Fish sauce, sugar, pepper and the water.
  4. Herbs last. Fingerroot, green peppercorns, kaffir and spur chilli — one or two tosses, straight to the plate. Hot rice on the side.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The protein. TrueID runs twenty-five versions — clams, prawns, squid, chicken, frog. The herbs are the dish; the protein is interchangeable.

Curry paste or none. The Lemon8 home version fries a spoon of red curry paste in the oil first for a deeper base; the TrueID clam version is paste-free, pure herbs. Both are pad cha.

Holy basil. Some home versions finish with holy basil; the classic herbal set is fingerroot, green peppercorns and kaffir. The basil pushes it towards pad krapow territory.

Seasoning. TrueID keeps it to fish sauce, sugar and pepper; the home versions reach for seasoning powder and oyster sauce. The shop version trusts the herbs.

Chilli count. Two kinds of fresh chilli in TrueID's version; the Lemon8 version adds a third heat through the curry paste. The dish's default is hot.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Dried herbs instead of fresh. Dried fingerroot and kaffir are faint echoes — the dish is a fresh-herb event.
  • Calling it pad krapow. Pad cha has no basil, no minced meat, no fried egg. Different dish, different herbs.
  • Overcooking the aromatics. The fingerroot and peppercorns go in last, one toss — stewed, they fade to nothing.
  • Sweet sauce. The seasoning is fish sauce, a little sugar, pepper. A sweet glaze buries the herbs.
  • Ginger instead of fingerroot. Ginger is hot and plain; fingerroot is floral and piney. The swap changes the dish's signature.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Fingerroot (กระชาย): Thai grocers — the thin knobby roots; slice thin, skin on.
  • Green peppercorns on the stem: Thai grocers, fresh or frozen.
  • Clams: standard fishmongers/supermarkets — wash and purge well.
  • Red Jinda chillies: Thai grocers; red bird's-eyes if you want it hotter.
  • Serve with jasmine rice and a cold beer — the seafood-shop pairing.
