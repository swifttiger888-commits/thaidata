Mango Sticky Rice (ข้าวเหนียวมะม่วง)

Steamed glutinous rice folded hot with salted coconut cream, ripe mango, salted cream sauce, fried mung beans. The salt is the dish.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 26 September 2026 · Serves 2 · ~4 hours with soaking

The Dessert That Stopped a Festival

In April 2022, the Thai rapper MILLI ate mango sticky rice on the Coachella main stage — and Thailand's vendors had to double their stock overnight. The dish didn't need the publicity, though: Bangkok's K. Panich has sold it for 90 years, and the coconut-folding tradition behind it goes back to King Rama II's era. A palace record from King Rama V's court pairs the same coconut rice with ripe อกร่อง mango.

Chef Chumpol of R-HAAN (2 Michelin stars) sets the standard: the rice "should not be too sticky, not soggy, not hard — soft with a slight bite," carrying a sweet-salty balance; the mango sweet and fragrant with a faint sour finish, "not sour-lead." Everything else is a negotiation.

The Method

  1. Rinse hard. One rinse, then cover with water and swish a piece of alum through for 10 seconds, rinse, repeat three times until the water runs clear. Soak at least 3 hours. (If you skip the alum, rinse thoroughly anyway — the point is clear water.)
  2. The coconut mix. Cream, sugar and salt over low heat until dissolved, knotted pandan leaves in, off the heat before it boils. Leave to cool — it's added warm, never hot enough to cook.
  3. Steam, don't boil. Rice drained and spread in muslin over boiling water, about 60 minutes, until cooked through. Boiling makes it waterlogged; steaming keeps the grains separate.
  4. Fold hot. Hot rice, warm coconut mix, stirred through. Cover, rest 10 minutes, stir once more, rest another 30–40 minutes. The rice absorbs the coconut and turns glossy — this rest is where the dish happens.
  5. The sauce. The remaining cream with the salt and rice flour, simmered stirring until just thickened. It goes over the top, lightly salted.
  6. Plate. Glossy rice mounded, ripe mango sliced beside it, cream sauce over, fried mung beans on top. The crunch against the soft rice is the finish.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The mango. อกร่อง — the court favourite, fragrant, slightly fibrous, now rare and pricey — vs น้ำดอกไม้, the big uniform modern standard. Both are correct; one is history.

The rice crop. Old-crop glutinous rice absorbs the coconut better and goes less soggy; new-crop is more fragrant. Vendors argue; both work.

Fresh vs boxed coconut. Chef Chumpol insists on fresh cream; home recipes run boxed happily. Fresh wins on richness, boxed on convenience.

The sauce. Plain salted cream, or rice-flour-thickened (R-HAAN's way) — some skip the sauce entirely.

Colour. The white original vs the butterfly-pea blue, pandan green and black-rice versions now standard at market stalls.

The Documented Relatives

Cambodia — Bey Dom Neib: glutinous rice with palm sugar, sometimes black beans in the rice, a wetter coconut pour.

The Philippines — Puto Maya: glutinous rice cooked with fresh ginger juice and coconut, wrapped in banana leaf, and — documented, not a joke — served with hot chocolate poured over.

Thailand: the version above — white rice, salted coconut fold, the mango the star. Sweet-salty balance is the marker.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Non-glutinous rice. Jasmine or pudding rice will not do it. It must be glutinous rice, steamed.
  • No salt. The salt is the dish — the sweet-salty balance is what makes it Thai, not a western rice pudding.
  • Soggy rice. Over-soaked, boiled instead of steamed, or drowned in coconut — the rice must hold its shape and stay glossy.
  • Unripe mango. Green sour mango belongs to a different street snack. This dessert needs ripe, fragrant, sweet mango.
  • Skipping the rest. The 10-minute cover and the 30–40 minute rest are where the rice absorbs the coconut. Fold-and-serve is undercooked.
  • Sweet cream sauce. The sauce on top is salted, not sweetened — it cuts the rice, it doesn't double it.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Glutinous rice: Asian grocers, labelled "Thai glutinous rice" or "sweet rice" — long-grain Thai, not the Japanese short-grain kind.
  • Mangoes: น้ำดอกไม้ appears in Thai grocers in season; otherwise Alphonso, Kent or Keitt — ripe, fragrant, no stringiness. The mango decides the dessert, so buy the best one.
  • Coconut: full-fat coconut milk tins work; use the thick settled part as the "cream" — or fresh coconut from an Asian grocer if you want to see what the fuss is about.
  • Pandan leaves: frozen in Asian grocers, keep in the freezer. Vanilla is not a substitute — just leave it out if needed.
  • Fried mung beans: buy ready-fried (ถั่วทอง) in Asian grocers, or split yellow mung beans briefly fried at home.
  • Alum: the traditional rinse is optional — a thorough plain rinse works. Use only food-grade alum if you use it at all.
  • Steaming: a proper steamer or a colander over a pot with a lid — the muslin is the trick for even cooking.
