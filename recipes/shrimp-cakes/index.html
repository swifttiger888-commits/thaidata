Shrimp Cakes (ทอดมันกุ้ง)

Shrimp pounded with garlic, coriander root and white pepper, kneaded until it springs back, crumbed and fried golden. Crisp shell, bouncy heart — the bounce is the dish.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · ~16 pieces · ~45 minutes

The Shrimp Cakes That Bounce

ทอดมันกุ้ง is the prawn version of the tod man family — and the same law applies: the cake must bounce. A Thai cook tests the paste by feel; if it doesn't spring back under the fingers, it goes back in the bowl. That spring is not a texture preference, it's the definition of the dish. A shrimp cake that crumbles into mashed shrimp is just a fried mistake.

The spring comes from two things. One: pounding, not mincing — a cleaver or a pestle beats the protein into strands that grip each other. Two: kneading — the paste is worked like dough until it turns sticky and elastic. The seasoning paste is small and specific: white peppercorns pounded first, then coriander root and Thai garlic — the same trinity that anchors so much of the Thai repertoire.

And then the crumb. Unlike its fish cousin, the shrimp cake goes out wearing coarse breadcrumbs — both published sources agree on this — so the outside shatters while the inside stays springy. Served hot with ajad cucumber pickle or plum sauce, and gone before the plate cools.

The Method

  1. Pound the paste. Peppercorns first — they take longest — then coriander roots and garlic, to a fine paste.
  2. Pound the shrimp. The larger portion into the paste until it turns sticky and elastic; fold in the chopped shrimp, pork fat, sugar, seasoning powder and oyster sauce.
  3. Knead. Work the mixture hard with your hands until it genuinely bounces back. Test it with a finger — if it doesn't spring, keep going.
  4. Shape and crumb. Small flat patties, both sides pressed into the coarse breadcrumbs.
  5. Fry. Medium heat, deep oil, until deep golden. Drain on paper.
  6. Serve. Hot, with ajad cucumber pickle or plum sauce — and eat them while they bounce.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Pure shrimp vs pork fat. The TrueID version is กุ้งล้วน — shrimp only, held together with crispy-fry flour. The ครัวบ้านพิม shop version adds minced hard pork fat for richness and a looser, juicier crumb. Both are published; the pure version tastes more of shrimp, the fat version fries more tenderly.

Flour or none. The pure-shrimp recipe uses four tablespoons of crispy-fry flour as binder; the paste version needs none — the pounding does the binding. Substituting one for the other changes the texture, not the identity.

The crumb. Both sources coat in breadcrumbs. The fish-cake cousin goes in bare; the shrimp cake wears crumbs. That's the documented difference, not a choice.

Seasoning powder. Shop recipes use pork seasoning powder (children-brand, the standard Thai one); home versions use fish sauce. The powder is the commercial shortcut — take it or leave it.

Sweetness. The source's author admits she hates sweet in savoury food and halves the sugar; the full teaspoon rounds the flavour. Your call, both published.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Minced, not pounded. A food processor whips air in and breaks the protein strands. Pounding builds them. The bounce is the test.
  • Skip the kneading. The paste must be worked until elastic. Unkneaded cakes crumble in the oil.
  • Sweet chilli as the default dip. The Thai table serves ajad or plum sauce. Sweet chilli is the takeaway-counter substitution.
  • No coriander root. It's the paste's backbone — ground seed is not a substitute.
  • Frying cool. Medium heat, steady — too low and the crumb drinks oil; too high and the outside burns before the shrimp sets.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Shrimp: raw shell-on prawns from any fishmonger or supermarket counter — shelled weight is what counts. Frozen raw prawns work if fully defrosted and dried first.
  • Pork fat: ask the butcher for hard back fat; a little goes in, it's the texture that matters, not the flavour.
  • Coriander roots: Thai grocers sell coriander with the roots on — rinse well, they carry the soil. Five roots is roughly one supermarket bunch.
  • Pork seasoning powder: Ros Dee or similar at Thai grocers; swap in a teaspoon of fish sauce plus a pinch of sugar if it's missing.
  • Coarse breadcrumbs: panko does the job — the coarse cut is what shatters crisp.
  • Plum sauce: the Chinese-aisle jar is the right one; or make the ajad from the fish-cakes page.
