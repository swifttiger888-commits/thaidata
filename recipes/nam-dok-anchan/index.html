Recipe · Thailand · The Fence-Line Flower

Nam Dok Anchan (น้ำดอกอัญชัน)

Butterfly pea flowers boiled until the water runs deep blue, sweetened, iced — and turned purple the moment lime goes in.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 29 September 2026 · 2 glasses · ~15 minutes

The Flower The Fence Gives Away

ดอกอัญชัน is the blue flower every Thai fence grows and nobody plants on purpose — which is exactly Kapook's pitch: people think of it as the eyebrow dye grandmothers used, but break off a handful and it becomes the day's prettiest drink in fifteen minutes. The recipe is barely a recipe: boil, sweeten, strain, ice.

Then there is the trick the drink is famous for. Wongnai's home recipe notes it matter-of-factly: the moment lime juice goes in, the blue water turns purple — the same glass of anchan, two colours, no dye. It is chemistry, not decoration, and every Thai source treats the lime squeeze as part of the drink, not an optional garnish.

Beyond the home glass sit two other schools. TrueID's concentrate is made for colouring desserts, and it is strict about it: the stems and pistils come off every flower because they make the water bitter, petals only, boiling water poured over and left to steep. And Nestlé Professional builds the café version backwards — lime and sugar syrup in the glass first, the blue anchan water poured in last so it floats over the acid layer, served immediately while cold.

The Recipe (home version, from Kapook)

The lime version (Wongnai): 30 dried flowers, 2 L water, 5 limes, 2 tbsp sugar — the lime goes in at the end and the drink turns purple. The café batch (Coffman): 50 g fresh or dried flowers, 1 L water, 150 g sugar, 20 g lime powder, 1/2 tsp salt — boiled until the petals turn pale. The concentrate (TrueID): 60 g flowers to 100 g water, petals only, steeped 5–10 minutes — for colouring desserts, not drinking.

The Method

  1. The wash. Flowers rinsed clean — no stems needed for the home glass.
  2. The boil. Flowers into the water for 2–3 minutes until it turns blue; sugar in, boiled until dissolved.
  3. The strain. Off the heat, flowers strained out — they've given all they have.
  4. The cool. Left until warm, tasted, adjusted.
  5. The glass. Over ice — and for the lime turn, a squeeze at the glass flips the blue to purple.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Boil or steep. Kapook boils 2–3 minutes, Wongnai 5, Coffman until the petals turn pale. TrueID doesn't boil at all — boiling water poured over the petals, steeped 5–10 minutes off the heat. Longer and hotter means deeper colour, and more bitterness if the flowers break down.

Fresh or dried. Kapook's fence version uses fresh flowers; Wongnai counts 30 dried ones; Coffman accepts either, 50 g. Dried flowers colour harder per gram — the selling recipes prefer them.

Lime or not. Wongnai squeezes fresh limes in and watches the purple turn; Coffman batches it with lime powder and half a teaspoon of salt; Nestlé layers the blue over the lime so the glass shows both colours. Kapook and TrueID serve it plain. The pure version is the base; the lime version is the showpiece.

The sugar scale. Kapook's half cup for two cups of water is a syrup; Coffman runs 150 g per litre; Wongnai's 2 tablespoons over two litres is barely sweetened. Every recipe says "to taste" — the disagreement is where each starts.

The bitter parts. TrueID insists stems and pistils come off, because they taste bitter. The other sources wash the flowers whole and never mention it — a detail only the colouring school cares about.

Salt. Only Coffman adds it — half a teaspoon, the batch-brew trick that rounds the sweetness. The home glass skips it.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Butterfly pea powder and teabags. The drink is whole petals boiled into the water — powder brews murky and thin, and a teabag gives dust instead of colour.
  • Serving it blue and stopping there. Half the drink is the lime turn. Every Thai lime recipe treats the blue-to-purple change as the point, not an optional flourish.
  • Boiling it to green-brown. The home version stops when the water turns blue. A long violent boil overcooks the petals into a muddy colour and a bitter edge.
  • Making it a latte. Milk turns the drink grey and belongs to the café's butterfly pea latte trend, not to any Thai source — the Thai drink is clear.
  • Selling it as a miracle detox. The Thai sources present it as a drink and a natural food colouring. The health-claim copy you see in the West is marketing, not in the recipes.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Dried butterfly pea flowers: Thai grocers and online — Wongnai's 30-flower recipe works unchanged.
  • Fresh flowers are rarely sold in the UK; dried is the practical form and colours more strongly.
  • Limes: any supermarket. Lime powder (citric-based) only matters if you're batching like Coffman.
  • Sugar: plain granulated matches every source.
  • The concentrate (TrueID) keeps in the fridge and doubles as natural food colouring for bakes, rice and iced drinks.
