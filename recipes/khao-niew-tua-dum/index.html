Khao Niew Tua Dum (ข้าวเหนียวถั่วดำ)

Glutinous rice steamed, then kneaded warm with sweetened coconut cream, under a ladle of black beans simmered in coconut milk and palm sugar — two pots cooked apart, finished with a dribble of salted cream. Thailand's morning-market dessert, served warm.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 8–10 small bowls · ~1¼ hours plus soaking

Two Pots That Meet At The Table

ข้าวเหนียว (khao niew, glutinous rice) and ถั่วดำ (tua dum, black beans) name the dish in full, and that pairing is exactly how it's made: two things cooked apart and combined only in the bowl. The rice half is ข้าวเหนียวมูน (khao niew mun) — steamed glutinous rice kneaded with sweetened coconut cream, the same rice that carries mango in khao niew mamuang. Here its partner is ถั่วดำแกงบวด, black beans simmered in coconut milk and palm sugar. The Phol Food Mafia cooking school's Kanom Thai recipe puts it plainly: mun rice eaten with black beans in coconut milk. Kapook adds that the bean pot doesn't need the rice at all — it's a dessert on its own, or poured over sago.

Six Thai sources we found agree on one structural rule, stated from opposite ends: two sweet things must be tuned to each other. Phol's recipe cuts the sugar in the mun rice — deliberately less than ordinary mango-sticky-rice mun, because the bean pot is already sweet. ครัวบ้านพิม's recipe makes the mirror move — cut the sugar in the bean pot to 80 g when it will sit on sweet mun rice. Each pot, by itself, is too sweet or too plain; the dish is the balance between them.

The ingredients stay stubbornly Thai. Four of the six sources name ข้าวเหนียวเขี้ยวงู (khao niew heiao ngu, "snake-fang" glutinous rice) as the rice. The beans are black turtle beans, soaked long and boiled soft, then simmered in the coconut milk until the sweetness works into the bean — ครัวบ้านพิม says about ten minutes, so bean and cream become one thing. Salt sits in every pot, rice and beans alike: the Thai sweet is always sweet-salty. And one Pantip cook's ritual no one else shares — toast the dry beans in the pan first, just until fragrant, before they go into the water.

The home pot (Pantip): two cups of dry beans toasted in a dry pan until fragrant, soaked 3 hours, boiled about an hour with a pinch of salt, rinsed until the water runs clear — then simmered in 4 cups thin coconut milk (หางกะทิ) with pandan and 220 g real coconut sugar, finished with 2 cups head cream (หัวกะทิ) on a gentler heat; three cups of rice steamed in the rice cooker and mun with ¾ cup head cream, ¾ cup sugar and ¾ tsp salt. The small pot (Cookpad, Areerat): 250 g beans soaked 6 hours, boiled, then simmered in 3½ cups coconut cream with ½ cup palm sugar and ½ cup sugar; 250 g rice soaked 4 hours, mun with 1 cup coconut cream, 100 g sugar and ½ tsp salt, under the salted, starch-thickened topping. The no-soak pot (ครัวบ้านพิม): beans dropped into already-boiling water (three times their volume), 40 minutes, rinsed four times; 4 cups coconut milk with 1 cup head cream held back, ½ cup sugar, 100 g palm sugar, ½ tsp salt, five pandan leaves — and two eggs beaten in at the very end, off the heat. The quick pot (Kapook): one cup of beans, 2–3 cups coconut milk, one cup palm sugar, ½ tsp salt — soak 3 hours, boil 40 minutes, simmer in the milk with the sugar last, stirring gently so the cream never splits.

The Method

  1. The beans. Wash and soak the black beans at least 3 hours. Drain, cover with three times their volume of water, boil, then simmer about an hour until a bean pressed between your fingers yields — Phol warns old and new beans take different times, and cooked beans weigh about double their dry weight. Drain and rinse until the water runs clear; every source does this rinse before the beans meet coconut milk.
  2. The rice. Scrub the glutinous rice with the alum in water for five minutes — it whitens the grain — rinse until clear, then soak three hours. Drain, wrap in muslin and steam over a medium fire about 30 minutes, until cooked through.
  3. The mun. Heat 250 g coconut milk with the sugar and salt just until dissolved — Pantip's warning: boil it hard and the cream splits (แตกมัน), and the pot is ruined. Pour the cream over the hot steamed rice in a lidded bowl, mix, cover 10 minutes, stir to turn the rice, cover 10 more. The rice drinks the cream and turns glossy; that is mun.
  4. The bean pot. Coconut milk, water, palm sugar, salt in a pot; stir until the sugar dissolves, bring to the boil, then add the drained beans and simmer 5 minutes so the sweetness works into the bean, not just the liquid.
  5. The topping. Coconut cream and salt over low heat; whisk the two starches with a little water, pour in, stir until it just thickens, off the heat. Cookpad's are the starches; the salt is the point — the finish is salty against the sweet.
  6. The table. Warm is the serving temperature — zarina99's recipe says to eat it อุ่น ๆ (warm), and the market stalls that sell it keep the pots warm all morning. Rice in the bowl first, bean pot ladled over, salted cream dribbled on top.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Which pot loses the sugar. Phol cuts the sugar in the mun rice because the beans are sweet; ครัวบ้านพิม cuts the sugar in the bean pot (to about 80 g) because the rice is sweet. Both agree the two pots must be tuned to each other — they just tune from opposite sides.

Toasting the beans. The Pantip recipe toasts the dry beans in the pan before soaking, for fragrance. No other source does. It costs one dry pan and five minutes.

Pandan. Pantip and ครัวบ้านพิม simmer the bean pot with pandan leaves (two or three, tied, removed later). Phol, Cookpad and Kapook skip it entirely. The classic Thai cream pot usually has pandan; here only half the sources bother.

Eggs. ครัวบ้านพิม beats two eggs into the bean pot at the very end, off the heat, so they set in soft strands through the cream. She is the only source who does. Nobody else mentions eggs.

Split coconut milk. Pantip and ครัวบ้านพิม build the pot from thin coconut milk (หางกะทิ) and finish with head cream (หัวกะทิ), the old way of controlling richness. Phol, Cookpad and Kapook use single-strength coconut milk throughout. The result is the same class of pot; the texture is creamier the split way.

The alum scrub. Phol scrubs the rice with alum for five minutes to whiten it; zarina99 soaks it in warm alum water for six hours. Cookpad and Pantip just wash the rice. Purely cosmetic — the rice tastes the same without it.

Soaking times. Beans: 3 hours (Phol, Kapook), 6 hours (Cookpad, zarina99) or none — ครัวบ้านพิม drops them into already-boiling water and still gets them soft in 40 minutes. Rice: 3 hours (Phol), 4 hours (Cookpad), 6 hours for old-crop rice (zarina99). Old beans cook slower than new; that explains more of the spread than the cooks do.

The topping. Cookpad thickens the salted coconut cream with tapioca and arrowroot starches so it sits on the rice like a lid. Most other sources pour plain salted cream, and Phol and Kapook skip the topping altogether. The salt, at least, everyone keeps.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Serving it cold. This dessert is eaten warm — zarina99 says อุ่น ๆ, and every market stall keeps both pots warm through the morning. Chilled, glutinous rice sets into a brick; the fridge is where sticky rice goes to die.
  • The wrong rice. Jasmine or long-grain rice cannot hold the cream — it drinks nothing and turns to mush. It must be glutinous rice, ideally heiao ngu. If the grain doesn't say glutinous or sweet rice, it isn't this dish.
  • Confusing ถั่วดำ with black rice. The beans are black turtle beans. Black glutinous rice is a different dessert entirely (ข้าวเหนียวดำ). "Black bean" in the name means beans — a whole pot of them.
  • Spicing it like a winter soup. Cinnamon, vanilla, nutmeg — the Thai pot is coconut milk, palm sugar, salt and at most pandan. The caramel note is the palm sugar; the spice cabinet stays shut.
  • Mango-sticky-rice sugar levels. Mun rice under mango is sweet; mun rice under black beans is deliberately less so, because the bean pot carries the sugar. Phol's rule. Sweeten it like mango rice and the two pots collide.
  • Boiling the coconut milk hard. Thai sources repeat the warning across recipes — boil or stir too hard and the cream splits (กะทิแตกมัน), oil separates, and the dish is lost. Low heat, constant gentle stirring, from Pantip and Kapook alike.
  • Sweet cream on top. The classic finish is salted coconut cream — Cookpad's topping carries ¾ teaspoon of salt and two starches. Sweet on sweet on sweet is not how the Thais end a sweet.
  • Keeping the bean water. Every source rinses the cooked beans until the water runs clear — two, three, four rinses — before the beans meet coconut milk. Boiled-in-black-water beans make a grey, muddy pot.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Glutinous rice: sold as "glutinous rice" or "Thai sweet rice" at Thai and East Asian grocers; heiao ngu specifically is the classic Thai variety and worth asking for. Regular long-grain rice is not a substitute — the dish's whole technique is the rice drinking the cream.
  • Black beans: dried black turtle beans — the same bean Thai cooks use — are on Waitrose and Tesco shelves and in every health-food shop. 400 g dry gives roughly the 800 g cooked this recipe wants. Canned black beans, well drained, are a shortcut UK cooks may take, but no Thai source starts there — say so if you do.
  • Coconut milk: tinned coconut milk for the pot and tinned coconut cream for the topping do the job; no need to press fresh coconut like the Thai sources. Use full-fat — the dessert is built on the fat.
  • Palm sugar (น้ำตาลปี๊บ): at Thai grocers, in soft discs or tubs. If you must substitute, soft light brown sugar gets the caramel note partway; white sugar alone misses the point of the dish.
  • Pandan leaves: frozen at Thai grocers and much of the flavour of the Pantip / ครัวบ้านพิม pots. Skip it rather than use essence, which is mostly green perfume.
  • Alum (สารส้ม): skip it. It is a cosmetic whitening scrub for the rice and not a UK kitchen item; the dish tastes and behaves identically without it.
  • Arrowroot starch (แป้งท้าวยายม่อม): cornflour replaces it one-for-one in the topping.
  • Steaming: a bamboo steamer, or a muslin-lined colander over a pot of boiling water, does the job. Pantip's rice-cooker method works if your cooker has a steam line — water to the line, rice in a muslin parcel above it.
