Recipe · Southern Thailand · The Fried Fish That Tastes of Turmeric

Pla Tod Kamin (ปลาทอดขมิ้น)

Whole fish rubbed in pounded turmeric, garlic and pepper, deep-fried until the skin crackles, then buried under golden fried garlic — the south's everyday fried fish, and the reason a southern plate always glows yellow.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 2 · ~40 minutes

The Fish That Carries the South's Colour

Ask what makes a fried fish southern, and the answer is in the name: ขมิ้น (kamin), fresh turmeric. The Institute of Nutrition at Mahidol University puts it plainly — southern cooks put turmeric in almost everything, and their fried fish is defined by it: a pale fish comes out of the marinade and the wok a deep golden yellow, with the warm, earthy smell no pepper can fake. The dish is so much the region's signature that a southern set meal is built around it: a piece of turmeric fish next to a fiery curry, the mild crisp fish calming the burn between mouthfuls.

It is also a working dish. Kapook's recipe comes from a Pantip member's fishing story — grey mullet (ปลากระบอก) netted at the dark of the moon, when the fish is fat with roe, and eaten the same day with gaeng som. That recipe's author admits the dish's one real drawback: it is oil-hungry, and some households skip it because the turmeric-garlic paste scorches before the fish cooks. The southern fix, shared by nearly every source, is what makes the dish: the marinade's garlic is fried separately, slowly, into a golden crunch that goes back on top of the fish at the end.

The fish itself is negotiable — mullet, sand whiting, tilapia, sea bass, snakehead. Sanook lists ten acceptable fish. The turmeric is not.

The Mahidol marinade: the institute's sand-whiting version adds a spoon of shrimp paste (กะปิ) to the pound — turmeric, pepper, garlic, shrimp paste and salt — for four whiting, marinated 20 minutes and fried in palm oil. The Dinner Studio pot: 500 g fish, 1 tbsp fresh turmeric (or 1 tsp powder), 5 cloves garlic, 1 tsp each pepper and salt, marinated 30 minutes, fried 5–7 minutes a side, served with seafood dipping sauce. The Kapook pot: 3 turmeric rhizomes, 5 whole heads of garlic, 1 tbsp pepper, ½ tbsp salt for one mullet, marinated a full hour — its author fries the whole paste separately into the crispy topping.

The Method

  1. The fish. Scale and gut the fish, then score it deeply on both sides — the cuts let the marinade in and help the skin crisp. Pat it dry; wet fish steams in the oil instead of frying.
  2. The paste. Pound the turmeric, half the garlic, the peppercorns and salt to a coarse paste. Wongnai warns the turmeric fights back — it is resinous and stains the mortar yellow for days, which is also exactly why it colours the fish. Rub the paste over the fish, inside the cavity and into the cuts, and leave it 15–30 minutes.
  3. The garlic. This is the step that makes the dish. Fry the remaining garlic slowly in oil, the way you would fry garlic for a topping — Kapook's cook fries it over a low flame until the cloves float, meaning the moisture is gone, then drains and cools them so they crisp. If you like, a little turmeric paste goes into the same oil for golden crumbs.
  4. The fry. Wipe the excess marinade off the fish — Wongnai is blunt that paste left on burns before the fish cooks. Then deep-fry over a medium flame: hot enough to crisp the skin, gentle enough to cook the flesh through. Kapook's timing for a mullet: four minutes a side, then slightly lower heat for three more.
  5. The finish. Drain the fish on paper, scatter the crisp garlic over it and serve immediately — with rice, seafood dipping sauce (น้ำจิ้มซีฟู้ด), or the Sanook pairing of blanched vegetables and nam phrik kapi. Kapook's cook eats the garlic crumbs first, by the spoonful, and warns that the fish disappears faster than anyone expects.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The marinade. Wongnai keeps it minimal — garlic, turmeric and salt. Sanook, Kapook and Dinner Studio add white pepper. Mahidol adds shrimp paste (กะปิ) as well. All five agree on the core: fresh turmeric pounded with garlic, never a dry rub of curry powder.

How long to marinate. Wongnai and Sanook say 15 minutes; Mahidol 20; Dinner Studio 30, and up to overnight if you can; Kapook's fisherman leaves it a full hour. Nobody marinates less than fifteen minutes, and everyone agrees longer is better rather than worse.

What happens to the paste at frying time. Wongnai wipes it off so it does not scorch — and, if you like the flavour, fries the scraped-off paste separately until crisp. Kapook builds the whole dish on that idea: the paste is fried slowly into golden turmeric-garlic crumbs and returned as the topping. Sanook and Mahidol instead set aside half the garlic from the start, fry it into plain crispy garlic, and leave the marinade on the fish. Three roads to the same place: the crunch must come from fried garlic, not from the fish.

The fish. Kapook's mullet, Mahidol's sand whiting, Dinner Studio's sea bass or snakehead — Sanook simply lists ten acceptable species, from tilapia and mackerel to climbing perch and snakehead. The dish takes whichever fish the market has; the south's preference is for small sea fish fried crisp enough to eat bones and all, which Mahidol notes is also how you get the calcium.

The oil. Mahidol, unusually for a recipe site, weighs palm oil against health — palm oil gives the crispest skin but is heavy in saturated fat, so it insists on blotting the fish well after frying. The other sources simply say plenty of oil, medium flame.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Curry powder as the marinade. The yellow comes from fresh turmeric pounded with garlic — every Thai source, from the home cooks to Mahidol's nutrition institute, builds the paste that way. A shake of curry powder colours the fish and gives you none of the dish.
  • Leaving off the fried garlic. The crisp fried garlic is not a garnish; it is the texture the dish is known for, and Kapook's recipe exists to teach the slow-fry that makes it. Plain turmeric fish without the garlic crunch is a half-made dish.
  • Battering or flouring the fish. No Thai source coats the fish in anything but the pounded paste. The crispness is supposed to come from hot oil on well-dried skin, plus the garlic on top — a flour coating is a different dish wearing the name.
  • Low-and-slow frying. A timid flame steams the skin soft and the paste burns onto the pan. Thai sources fry over medium to hot oil — Kapook heats the oil, Wongnai wants it properly hot — and blot the fish after, rather than fry it cool.
  • Treating turmeric as optional colour. Mahidol's whole write-up is about turmeric as the defining ingredient — the flavour, the colour, the reason the dish is southern. A western version that reduces it to a pinch of spice in the flour has missed the point of the name.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Fish: any whole white fish works — sea bass, sea bream, tilapia or small mackerel, all easy to find at UK fish counters. Gutted and scaled is fine; ask the fishmonger to leave the head on for the full look.
  • Fresh turmeric: sold in Asian grocers and increasingly in UK supermarkets' produce sections; a 2 cm piece per fish. Ground turmeric works as a fallback (about 1 tsp per fish, per Sanook and Dinner Studio) but stains less and tastes flatter.
  • Garlic: standard UK bulbs; the recipe wants cloves from a couple of whole heads, and the fried-topping step uses plenty of oil, so don't skimp.
  • Shrimp paste (กะปิ), for the Mahidol marinade variant: jars at Thai grocers.
  • Oil: any neutral oil with a high smoke point; Mahidol's palm-oil point stands — blot the fish well after frying whatever oil you use.
