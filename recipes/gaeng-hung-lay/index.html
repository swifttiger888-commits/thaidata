Recipe · Thailand · The North's Curry That Came From Burma

Gaeng Hung Lay (แกงฮั่งเล)

Pork belly and shoulder, kneaded with a pounded spice paste and left to drink it in, then simmered until it collapses — finished with fresh ginger, tamarind and peanuts, the curry Chiang Mai makes for guests.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Serves 4–5 · ~2 hours (plus marinating)

The Curry That Crossed a Border

แกงฮั่งเล — Wongnai spells it ฮังเล, the Lanna cookbooks write แกงฮินเล or แกงฮันเล — is the north's great pork curry, and the Thai sources agree on where it came from. Kapook tells the story plainly: the Burmese dish เว่ตาฮีน — pork curry, from ฮีน, curry, and the word for meat — came south with Burmese traders into the Lanna kingdom, and later settled as spice-powder communities in Mae Hong Son and Tak's Mae Sot district, where ผงฮังเล, the ready-mixed hang-lay masala powder, is still made and sold. Wongnai's recipe column gives the same etymology in one line: in Burmese, ฮี่น is curry and เล่ is meat.

The dish the sources describe is remarkably consistent. Pork — belly, with shoulder or ribs or both, depending on the kitchen — is kneaded with a pounded paste of dried chillies, galangal, lemongrass, shallots, garlic and coarse shrimp paste, and left to marinate for an hour. Then it is fried lightly, covered with water, and simmered long. The finish never varies: tamarind water for the sour, sugar for the deep sweet, fresh ginger sliced in near the end, and roasted peanuts on top. Ginger is the dish's signature — it goes in whole, at the finish, where it keeps its bite against the collapsed meat.

Kapook adds the detail the other sources skip: there are two hung lays. แกงฮังเลม่าน, the Burmese-style pot, is the plain curry. แกงฮังเลเชียงแสน, from Chiang Saen, folds in local vegetables — long beans, aubergines, pea aubergines, chaphlu leaves, pickled bamboo shoots. Both are the same curry; one of them just has a garden in it.

The powder question. Kapook calls hang-lay powder essential — two sachets, kneaded into the pork with the paste. Wongnai's editorial recipe and the Knorr-sponsored version use no powder at all, letting the paste and the pineapple do the spicing. Aroifin splits the difference: paste, two tablespoons of powder and the pineapple. If you can find proper Thai hang-lay powder (ผงฮังเล) at a grocer, use it; if not, the paste-only pot is a fully documented version of the same curry. The ready-made route. Lemon8's recipe skips the mortar entirely: 80–170 g of shop-bought hang-lay curry paste with a kilo of belly and 400 g of ribs — the same dish for a weeknight.

The Method

  1. The paste. Pound the dried chillies with the salt until fine, then work in the garlic, shallots, galangal, lemongrass and turmeric, pounding until it is a paste. Add the coarse shrimp paste last and pound it through. Kapook and Wongnai both keep the shrimp paste coarse-grained (กะปิหยาบ) — it is not meant to dissolve.
  2. The marinade. Mix the paste with the crushed pineapple and the hang-lay powder if using, then knead it into the diced pork with your hands. Leave it for an hour — every source marinates, and every source says the curry tastes like what happens in that hour.
  3. The fry. Put the marinated pork in a pot with a splash of water and fry, stirring, until the meat firms and the paste turns dark and fragrant. This is the step that sets the spice — skip it and the curry tastes raw.
  4. The simmer. Add water to cover and simmer gently, topping up a little at a time. Here the sources part ways on patience: Wongnai and the Knorr version call the pork done in about an hour; Kapook simmers one hour, adds the sugar, then simmers two hours more. The short pot is a fine weeknight curry; the long pot is the falling-apart festival one.
  5. The seasoning. Stir in the tamarind water, fish sauce, dark soy and brown sugar. Then add the julienned ginger and the whole garlic cloves — the ginger goes in now, near the end, so it keeps its bite against the soft pork. Kapook also adds pickled garlic and a splash of its brine here, if you have it.
  6. The peanuts. Scatter in the roasted peanuts and let the curry bubble for a final fifteen minutes. Serve hot with steamed rice or, as Kapook prefers, to dip with sticky rice. Left overnight, it only deepens.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The powder. Kapook treats hang-lay powder (ผงฮังเล) as the one ingredient the dish cannot do without. Wongnai's editorial recipe and the Knorr version never mention it — paste, pineapple and time do the work. Aroifin uses both. This is the biggest split among the sources, and it is a practical one: the powder is the Burmese masala heritage, and its absence is why the paste-only pots lean brighter and chilli-forward.

Simmer time. Wongnai and the Knorr recipe simmer for about an hour, total. Kapook simmers one hour, then two more after the sugar goes in — three hours for pork that collapses. Aroifin lands in the middle at seventy minutes. Nobody disputes the technique; they dispute how long a hung lay should have been waiting for you.

The sweet. Wongnai says plain sugar. Kapook says 80–100 g of brown sugar — a serious, dark sweetness. Aroifin uses cane-sugar powder (น้ำอ้อยป่น). All agree the curry must end sweet against the tamarind; the colour of the sugar is where they differ.

Pineapple versus pickled garlic. Wongnai and Aroifin put crushed pineapple into the marinade. Kapook's pot has no pineapple, but finishes with pickled garlic and its brine (น้ำกระเทียมดอง) alongside the tamarind. Two different doors into the same sweet-sour room.

The vegetables. Only Kapook mentions the Chiang Saen version, hung lay chiang saen, with long beans, aubergines, pea aubergines, chaphlu leaves and pickled bamboo shoots folded in. The other sources publish the plain Burmese-style pot. If your hung lay has vegetables in it, you are eating the Chiang Saen one.

The pork. Wongnai and Aroifin use collar and belly, half a kilo total. Kapook cooks two kilos of belly mixed with ribs — the feast size. Lemon8 uses a kilo of belly with 400 g of ribs and ready-made paste. All agree belly is non-negotiable; the collar, ribs and trotters are the kitchen's choice.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Calling it massaman. Hung lay shares the ginger, peanuts and slow pork, but it has no coconut milk, no cinnamon and no cardamom — the Thai sources never send you near a tin. It is the coconut-free, tamarind-soured cousin, not the same curry.
  • Skipping the marinade. Every Thai source kneads the paste into the pork and waits an hour. Western quick versions tip paste into a pan of browning pork and simmer. The hour is not optional resting time — it is where the paste gets into the meat.
  • Ginger in the paste. Galangal goes in the paste; fresh ginger goes in near the end, julienned and still firm. Grinding the ginger into the paste loses the one texture the dish is known for — the bite of ginger against the soft pork.
  • The coconut-milk and potato habit. Westernised "Burmese pork curries" add coconut cream and potatoes. The Thai sources use water, a splash at a time, and the body of the curry is the rendered pork itself.
  • Skipping the sugar. Hung lay is a sweet curry — Kapook's 80–100 g of brown sugar is not a rounding error, it is the dish. Undersweeten it and the tamarind turns it into a sour stew with nothing to lean on.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Pork: belly and shoulder from any UK butcher or supermarket; ask for the rind on — it gives the long-simmered version its silk.
  • Hang-lay powder (ผงฮังเล): the Lobo brand and northern Thai imports appear at Thai grocers and online Thai supermarkets; Mae Ploy and Lobo also make ready-mixed hang-lay curry paste for the Lemon8 shortcut. If you find neither, the paste-only Wongnai recipe above is complete.
  • Dried long chillies: Thai grocers sell them as พริกแห้งเม็ดใหญ่; Mexican guajillo or Kashmiri chillies are the standard stand-ins.
  • Coarse shrimp paste (กะปิ): the Thai brands in tubs, not the Malaysian belacan blocks — the coarse, paler kapi is what the recipes mean by กะปิหยาบ.
  • Pickled garlic and tamarind pulp are both standard Thai-grocer items; tamarind pulp beats ready-made tamarind paste for the clean sour the recipe wants.
