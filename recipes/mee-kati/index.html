Recipe · Thailand · Coconut Twice: In The Noodles And On Top

Mee Kati (หมี่กะทิ)

Thin rice vermicelli tossed in coconut milk until every strand turns pink, then a sweet-sour coconut gravy of minced pork, prawns and tofu poured over the top — central Thailand's two-pan noodle dish.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 3–4 · ~45 minutes

Two Pans, One Name

หมี่กะทิ splits neatly into its parts: หมี่ (mee) is the thread-thin rice vermicelli, กะทิ (kati) the coconut milk — and the dish delivers both, twice. One pan holds the เส้นหมี่สีชมพู, noodles tossed in coconut milk until every strand drinks it and turns pink. The other holds the น้ำราด: the same coconut milk, seasoned with fermented soybean paste, palm sugar and tamarind, carrying minced pork, minced prawns and diced tofu. The two meet only at the table, when the gravy goes over the noodles.

It is a central Thai dish with a long pedigree. Wongnai's version carries the title หมี่กะทิชาววัง — "palace-style" — inherited, its author says, from earlier generations; Pim of ครัวบ้านพิม traces her formula to a cookbook by อ.จรรยา สุบรรณ์ written more than thirty years ago; the Bloggang cook from Belgium files hers squarely under หมี่กะทิภาคกลาง, central Thai mee kati.

The street version, Pim complains, is just noodles dyed pink with food colouring and nothing on them — which is why she learned to make her own. At home the pink comes honestly: yen ta fo sauce, a cube of red fermented tofu, or in the oldest formulas, tomato ketchup. And the toppings come loaded.

The palace pot (Wongnai): 2 cups kati, a full cup each of pork and prawn mince, ¼ cup white tao jiew, ¾ cup firm tofu, 2 tbsp fish sauce, 3 tbsp palm sugar and 5 tbsp tamarind, noodles tossed in kati with 4 tbsp yen ta fo sauce. The cookbook pot (Krua / แสงแดด): tao jiew, eight shallots and ¼ cup red fermented tofu pounded into a paste; 150 g coarse pork and ¼ cup small prawns in head-and-tail kati, 4 tbsp fish sauce, 5 tbsp tamarind — then half the finished gravy is folded into the noodles themselves. The thirty-year-old pot (Pim, from อ.จรรยา สุบรรณ์): fresh kati squeezed from 250 g grated coconut, seasoned with pounded white tao jiew, its brine and palm sugar — no fish sauce at all — and noodles turned pink with 4 tbsp concentrated ketchup. The Isaan namesake (Aroifin): a different dish — red curry paste fried in 500 ml kati until it splits, 500 g pork, tamarind, beaten egg and peanuts, ladled over plain white noodles.

The Method

  1. The omelette. Beat the eggs and fry them into thin sheets, then roll and slice into fine shreds (ไข่ฝอย). Every version tops the noodles with these.
  2. The gravy base. Bring half the coconut milk to a boil and add the shallots. The older sources — Pim, Pantip, Bloggang — wait for the coconut milk to break (แตกมัน) first. Then add the pork and prawn mince, first loosened in a ladle of the hot coconut milk so they fall in as strands, not lumps, and fry until cooked.
  3. The seasoning. Add the tao jiew, palm sugar, tamarind water and chilli powder, then the tofu. Simmer until the gravy is slightly reduced. Tukata001's rule of thumb: it should taste like เต้าเจี้ยวหลน — the old fermented-soybean dip — sweet, sour and salty. Where the salt comes from is a point of dispute (below).
  4. The pink coconut milk. In the second pan, bring the remaining coconut milk to a boil and stir in the yen ta fo sauce and the ketchup — the two together give the pink that the old ketchup-only formulas could only manage pale.
  5. The noodles. Soak the sen mee in plain water only until soft — five to ten minutes, never long, or they go mushy — then drain well. Pim blanches them briefly first; Kapook skips straight from soaking to the pan.
  6. The toss. The step that separates the dish from a pad thai: add the noodles to the pink coconut milk and toss by lifting them high with two spatulas and letting them fall — Pim calls it ส่าย, Bloggang calls it สรง, both refuse the word ผัด. Hard stir-frying shatters the threads. When the noodles are evenly pink and the coconut milk is absorbed, add the bean sprouts and garlic chives, toss once, off the heat.
  7. The table. Noodles on the plate, egg shreds and sliced red chilli on top, the gravy in a small bowl. Pour it over at the table — just enough to moisten — and finish with crushed peanuts, extra chilli, a squeeze of lime, and more bean sprouts and chives on the side.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The pink. Yen ta fo sauce (Wongnai, Pantip, Bloggang), red fermented tofu — whole, or just its brine (DekSomboon, Phol) — or tomato ketchup alone (Pim's thirty-year-old formula). Kapook's recipe, the one used here, doubles up: ketchup and yen ta fo. All agree only on what the pink is not: plain food colouring, which is the street-cart shortcut.

To toss or to stir. Pim, Bloggang and the Cookpad cook all forbid hard stirring — the noodles are lifted and shaken until they drink the coconut milk, because stir-frying turns them to mush. Wongnai and DekSomboon write ผัด (stir-fry) and mean it, but with the same warning against overworking the threads.

The salt. Krua, Wongnai and DekSomboon season with fish sauce. Pim's old formula uses no fish sauce at all — the brine from the tao jiew does the salting, and she keeps it back on purpose. Bloggang pours in a spoon of the same brine. Pantip points out the saltiness is the tao jiew's job in the first place.

The split. Pim, Pantip and Bloggang hold out for กะทิแตกมัน — the coconut milk breaking its oil — before the meat goes in. Kapook, Wongnai and DekSomboon go straight from boiling coconut milk to shallots and seasoning, skipping the wait entirely.

Where the noodles cook. Most versions toss the noodles in a separate pan of pink coconut milk. Krua's cookbook version cooks the noodles in half the finished gravy itself, then pours the reserved half on top — one sauce doing both jobs.

The Isaan namesake. Aroifin's หมี่กะทิ สไตล์อีสาน shares the name and nothing else: red curry paste fried in coconut milk, half a kilo of pork, beaten egg and peanuts, served over plain white noodles with fresh herbs. Same two words, a different dish — and a reminder that the pink belongs to the central Thai version.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Miso or doenjang for the tao jiew. Western recipes quietly substitute Japanese or Korean bean pastes. Pim is explicit that white tao jiew is its own thing — sold by weight at Thai markets, brine and all — and it is what makes the gravy taste like tao jiew lon. Miso makes a different sauce.
  • Stir-frying like a pad thai. The whole technique is the lift-and-shake — Pim's ส่าย, Bloggang's สรง — specifically because hard stir-frying breaks the thread-thin noodles. Tossing them hard in a wok produces pink mush.
  • Over-soaking the sen mee. Every Thai source warns: soak briefly, five to ten minutes, or the noodles turn to paste in the pan. Western recipes that soak "until very soft" are the most common way to ruin the dish.
  • Serving it as a soup. The Lao dish of a similar name comes in a bowl of broth; the central Thai mee kati is dry noodles on a plate with the gravy poured over at the table. A western "coconut noodle soup" version has swapped countries.
  • Skipping the pink. The colour is the dish's signature, and the Thai home kitchen earns it with yen ta fo sauce, red fermented tofu or ketchup — only the street carts resort to dye. Plain white "coconut noodles" is a version nobody in Thailand makes on purpose.
  • Egg noodles. The dish is defined by sen mee, the thread-thin rice vermicelli. Any other noodle — egg, wheat, rice-stick — changes both texture and the way the coconut milk is absorbed.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Sen mee (เส้นหมี่): look for the thread-thinnest rice vermicelli — Thai brands like Vivi (ไวไว) in the red packet are the standard, and Chinese-supermarket vermicelli works in a pinch. Bloggang's cook swears the red-packet Vivi is softer than the blue.
  • Tao jiew (เต้าเจี้ยว): jars of "yellow bean sauce" or "soybean paste" — Healthy Boy and DekSomboon both reach Thai and Chinese grocers. Not black bean sauce, not miso; the white/yellow kind is the one.
  • Yen ta fo sauce (ซอสเย็นตาโฟ): Thai brands at Thai grocers and online Thai supermarkets; if you cannot find it, a cube of red fermented tofu (เต้าหู้ยี้แดง) with its brine — from any Chinese supermarket — does the pink job, and the brine alone tints the noodles in Phol's recipe.
  • Coconut milk: full-fat canned; one 400 ml can is the two cups the recipe wants. Pim's fresh version starts from 250 g grated coconut if you want to squeeze it properly.
  • The side vegetables: bean sprouts and garlic chives from any Asian grocer; banana blossom (หัวปลี) and pennywort (ใบบัวบก) from Thai shops when in season — more sprouts and chives are an acceptable stand-in.
