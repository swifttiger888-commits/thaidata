Pad Thai (ผัดไทย)

The dish the world knows — and the one it cooks worst. Tamarind, palm sugar and fish sauce in balance, sen chan noodles, dried shrimp and preserved radish. No ketchup, ever.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 26 September 2026 · Serves 2 · ~30 minutes

The Dish Invented in Wartime

Pad thai is younger than you think — a World War II creation. With rice scarce, Prime Minister Phibun Songkhram's government campaigned to get the country eating noodles instead, and rebranded Chinese-style fried noodles as a national dish. The "Thai" touches were deliberate: pork — a Chinese ingredient — was swapped for dried shrimp, yellow tofu, lime, bean sprouts and banana blossom. A 2004 Thai rom-com even borrowed the name.

The dish's defenders take it seriously. On British TV's The F Word, Chef Chang of London's Blue Elephant told Gordon Ramsay to his face: "This is not pad thai at all. Pad thai must be sweet, sour, salty." — a clip Michelin's own article notes has 8.1 million views. Bib Gourmand chef Andy Yang of Phad Thai Fai Ta Lu goes further: "one of the hardest dishes in the world," five techniques in one plate — infusion, caramelisation, smoke, al-dente texture, glazing.

The south has its own century-old cousin: pad thai Chaiya, from Chaiya in Surat Thani — hotter, with a sauce built on dried chilli, shallot and shrimp paste fried in cracked coconut cream, simmered into the noodles over low heat. Chef Ice of Sorn, the world's first three-Michelin-star Thai restaurant, gave Michelin the full recipe. It's documented below.

The Method

  1. The sauce first. Tamarind concentrate, palm sugar, fish sauce and chilli simmered until the sugar dissolves and it thickens slightly. Taste it — sour, sweet, salty in balance. Chef Om of Baan Phad Thai: the sauce holds all three flavours of the dish.
  2. Soak, don't boil. Sen chan noodles in room-temperature water 10–15 minutes until pliable, then drain. Over-soaking is the classic route to mush.
  3. Build the base. Tofu fried golden and out. Shallots, then dried shrimp and preserved radish, until fragrant. These three are the umami core western recipes drop.
  4. Prawns in, just until pink, out. Never rubber.
  5. The egg. Everything pushed to the side, eggs in, broken lightly, almost set.
  6. Noodles + sauce. In together — stir so the sauce soaks in and caramelises into a glaze. Hot enough to glaze, never hot enough to burn. This is the step that separates pad thai from sweet noodle stir-fry.
  7. Finish fast. Prawns and tofu back, sprouts and chives in, high heat for seconds — barely wilted.
  8. Plate. Crushed peanuts on top; lime, raw sprouts, chives and extra chilli on the side — the eater squeezes the lime over at the table.

The Chaiya Variant (Sorn, 3 Michelin stars)

The southern cousin, eaten in Chaiya, Surat Thani for over a century — hotter, richer, and saucy in a different way:

The sauce: 150 g dried chillies and 1 kg shallots pounded with flower salt, then 100 g shrimp paste; fried in 2 kg coconut cream that has been cooked until the oil cracks (the south calls it "ขี้โล้น" oil); seasoned with 1 kg coconut sugar, 250 g tamarind water (lime can substitute) and simmered until thick.

The stir-fry: oil and shallots, prawns and fresh sen chan noodles, a splash of stock, then the sauce — on low heat, stirred until the sauce absorbs into the noodles and they turn glossy. Egg at the pan edge, noodles folded over it, bean sprouts and chives in on high heat.

The sides: raw green mango strips, banana blossom, bean sprouts, chives, lone chillies (พริกลูกโดด), lime.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Classic tamarind sauce vs Chaiya's coconut-chilli-paste sauce. Different regions, both authentic, both called pad thai.

Banana blossom (หัวปลี) is a traditional side in the south and old Bangkok; most modern shops skip it.

Chilli in the sauce (some home recipes) vs chilli only on the side (the classic).

Pan. Chef Andy is relaxed: a teflon pan is fine if it's hot — the street-stall steel wok isn't the point, the heat control is.

The original had no tamarind. The first Phibun-era recipe used fermented orange juice for sourness; tamarind became standard later.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Ketchup. Appears in zero Thai sources. The sour is tamarind — ketchup makes a sweet noodle fry, not pad thai.
  • Wrong noodles. Sen chan is a specific thin rice noodle from Chanthaburi. "Rice sticks" boiled to mush, or spaghetti, are different dishes — Michelin's article mocks the pasta-fusion versions.
  • No dried shrimp or preserved radish. These two are the dish's depth. Without them it's noodles in sugar.
  • Vegetable swaps. Carrots and salad veg are western additions; the dish takes bean sprouts, chives and banana blossom.
  • No glazing step. The sauce must caramelise onto the noodles. Raw-tasting noodles mean the stir-fry was rushed at low heat.
  • Egg scrambled through. The egg is folded into the noodles as it sets, not scrambled into curds.
  • Sweet-forward. Gordon Ramsay heard it straight: pad thai must be sweet, sour, salty — a balance, not a dessert.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Sen chan noodles: Asian grocers, labelled "rice stick noodles — Chanthaburi style" (flat, ~5 mm). If only "rice sticks" exist, get the thinnest and under-soak rather than over-soak.
  • Tamarind: block or paste from Asian grocers — soak the block in hot water and strain. The concentrate is what the recipe calls for.
  • Sweet preserved radish (ไชโป๊วหวาน): Thai grocers, sold shredded in packets. It's the difference between good and great.
  • Yellow tofu: Asian grocers; firm tofu works but the yellow kind has the right eggy-springy bite.
  • Dried shrimp, palm sugar, fish sauce: any Asian grocer. Garlic chives: Asian grocers — regular chives are a poor second.
  • Banana blossom: optional, Asian grocers, tinned if you must.
