Bamee Kiao (บะหมี่เกี๊ยว)

Egg noodles and hand-folded wontons in a clear pork-bone broth, crowned with red barbecue pork — the noodle bowl that anchors every Thai street-side stall, from breakfast to last orders.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Serves 4 · ~2¼ hours

A Bowl With Three Crafts In It

บะหมี่เกี๊ยว is a dish assembled, not just cooked: there is the บะหมี่ — fresh yellow egg noodles; the เกี๊ยว — wontons, folded by hand around a pork filling; and the หมูแดง — char siu, the red barbecue pork that gives the bowl its sweetness and its colour. The fourth element, the broth, is the quiet one: pork bones simmered with coriander root, garlic and peppercorns, the same aromatic trinity (สามเกลอ) that seasons the filling.

Thai sources treat it as a Chinese-Thai dish — KRUA.CO's dry version is explicitly built on Cantonese wonton wrappers and Cantonese egg noodles — but the bowl as sold in Thailand is its own thing: generous, sweet-edged, and finished with fried garlic rather than the Cantonese scallion oil. The Wongnai cook ปีศาจหมู notes it can be served three ways from one pot: as soup, dry (haeng), or tom yum.

The stall version is also a family version. Every source here makes it at home, and the honest message in all of them is that the parts are simple; what takes time is the broth and the char siu. Do those well and the assembly is ten minutes.

The dry bowl (KRUA.CO): 6 Cantonese wonton wrappers, 2×45 g Cantonese egg noodle cakes, 40 g choy sum, 70 g sliced char siu, half a cup of char siu sauce, garlic oil for tossing, and fried garlic, crispy pork fat and spring onion on top — served with the pork-bone broth on the side and the full condiment tray (pickled chilli vinegar, fish sauce, sugar, chilli powder). The big-family pot (Pim): 1 kg pork neck for the char siu, 250 g fatty minced pork for 45–50 wontons, and a 7-litre broth from pork knuckle bones with white radish, pickled garlic and rock sugar. The three-way pot (Wongnai UGC): pork, prawn or crab fillings, folded golden-cup style, served as soup, dry, or tom yum with chilli jam and crushed peanuts.

The Method

  1. The filling. Pound the coriander roots, garlic and white peppercorns together — this is the สามเกลอ trinity — then work the paste into the minced pork and prawns with the soy sauce and cornflour. Knead until the meat turns sticky and holds together; Pim adds iced water at this point to keep the filling springy, and Kapook's version folds in minced prawn for sweetness.
  2. The fold. A teaspoon of filling in the centre of each wrapper. The easy fold is a triangle, pressed shut at the edges; the stall fold is the ถ้วยทอง (golden cup) — pull the two corners up and pinch them together over the filling. Keep finished wontons under a damp cloth so the skins do not dry, and cook them the same day.
  3. The broth. Pork bones in cold water, bring to the boil, skim the scum — the Wongnai UGC cook warns not to stir once the bones are in, or the broth clouds. Add coriander roots, garlic and peppercorns, drop to a gentle simmer and leave it an hour or two. Season lightly: salt and a touch of sugar or light soy, and never so much that the broth loses its clear colour.
  4. The char siu. Marinate the pork — Kapook's classic marinade is cinnamon, star anise, garlic, honey, tomato sauce, oyster sauce, light soy and orange colouring, a good three to four hours — then simmer it in the marinade until cooked and finish in a hot oven for ten to fifteen minutes until the surface dries and glazes. Wongnai's quick route boils the marinated pork, then roasts ten minutes at 200°C. Slice thinly on the diagonal.
  5. The blanch. A big pot of boiling water, and an order to it: choy sum first (seconds only), then the noodles, unravelled with chopsticks so they cook evenly, then the wontons — they are done when they float. Toss the noodles in a little garlic oil as soon as they drain so they never clump.
  6. The bowl. Noodles and choy sum in the bowl, wontons and sliced char siu arranged on top, hot broth ladled over, then fried garlic, spring onion and ground white pepper. Put the condiment tray on the table: pickled chilli vinegar, fish sauce, sugar and chilli powder — that is where each eater finishes the bowl to taste.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The wonton filling. Wongnai's Arunaa, Pim and KRUA.CO use pork alone — Pim's is fatty minced pork with sesame oil and iced water, KRUA.CO's adds pork belly, pork fat and spring onion, Cantonese style. Kapook goes pork and prawn (200 g pork to 100 g prawn), and the Wongnai UGC cook offers pork, whole prawn or crab. Everyone keeps the same seasoning base: coriander root, garlic, white pepper, light soy.

The char siu. Three roads to the same red pork. Kapook: the full classic — cinnamon, star anise, honey, tomato sauce, marinated three to four hours, simmered, then oven-finished. Wongnai's Arunaa: a quick home version — salt, pepper, sugar, oyster sauce, chilli sauce and red colouring, marinated one to two hours, boiled, then ten minutes at 200°C. Pim: a ready-made char siu marinade thinned with evaporated milk, marinated 24–48 hours. The Wongnai UGC cook skips char siu entirely and serves the bowl without it.

The broth. Bones, coriander root, garlic and peppercorns is the common base. Pim scales it up to a stall-sized 7 litres and adds white radish and pickled garlic; KRUA.CO and Pim both add rock sugar (น้ำตาลกรวด) for the soft sweetness; the Wongnai UGC cook prefers it nearly bare — salt, a little sugar, and only a splash of soy, to keep the broth clear and pale.

Wet or dry. KRUA.CO is the only source that serves the bowl exclusively dry — noodles and wontons tossed in garlic oil with char siu and sauce on top, the broth alongside as a drink. The rest ladle the soup over the noodles. The Wongnai UGC cook simply makes one pot and serves it all three ways: soup, dry, or tom yum with chilli jam, crushed peanuts, chilli powder, lime, sugar and fish sauce.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Chicken stock from a cube. The broth is the dish's quiet engine: pork bones simmered with coriander root, garlic and peppercorns. A cube or a chicken stock shortcut gives the bowl none of the pork sweetness the Thai sources simmer for one to two hours to get.
  • Egg noodles that are not egg noodles. The bamee must be fresh yellow egg noodles — alkaline, springy, yellow. Spaghetti, rice noodles or instant ramen change the texture the whole bowl is built around.
  • Overboiled wontons. Wontons are done the moment they float. Boiling on turns the filling tough and the skins to paste — several Thai sources also rinse the blanched noodles and wontons in clean water to shed excess starch before tossing them in garlic oil.
  • No char siu. หมูแดง is not optional garnish; its sweet, slightly smoky slice is the counterpoint to the savoury broth and the pepper of the filling. Western versions that swap in plain roast pork miss the point — the redness and the glaze are part of the dish.
  • Forgetting the condiment tray. Pickled chilli vinegar, fish sauce, sugar, chilli powder — the eater is expected to adjust. Serve the bowl without them and you have served half the dish.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Fresh egg noodles (บะหมี่ไข่): fresh yellow noodle cakes in the fridge or freezer of East and Southeast Asian supermarkets; dried wonton noodles also work if fresh ones are scarce.
  • Wonton wrappers (แผ่นเกี๊ยว): square or round packs in the freezer aisle of any Chinese supermarket; thaw fully and keep under a damp cloth while folding.
  • Char siu (หมูแดง): buy ready-made from a Cantonese roast-meat counter — most UK Chinese supermarkets have one — or make it with a jarred char siu sauce (Lee Kum Kee or similar) following the simmer-then-roast method above.
  • Pork bones for the broth: ask any butcher for pork bones or trotter; chicken bones can be added but pork should lead.
  • Choy sum (ผักกวางตุ้ง): fresh at Chinese grocers; pak choi is the usual stand-in.
  • Pickled chilli vinegar (น้ำส้มพริกดอง): steep sliced fresh chillies in white vinegar with a pinch of salt for a few hours — the stall tray is easy to copy.
