Recipe · Northern Thailand · Pork, Curry Paste And A Singed Banana Leaf

Aab Mu (แอ๊บหมู)

Minced pork worked with a lemongrass, galangal and turmeric paste, wrapped in banana leaf and grilled over low coals until the leaf singes and the parcel drips clear — the north's answer to the sausage roll.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 4 · ~60 minutes

The Parcel That Names Itself

แอ๊บ (aab) is the verb: to wrap and roast in banana leaf. The north has a whole family of them — แอ๊บปลา (fish), แอ๊บกุ้ง (prawns), แอ๊บไก่ (chicken) — and the pork one is the plainest and most loved. The Lemon8 cook who breaks the dish down for beginners draws the line: แอ๊บหมู is minced pork, straight; the more obscure แอ๊บอ่องออ is the offal-and-blood version. Start here.

The banana leaf does the cooking. It steams the pork from inside while the flame singes it from outside, so the paste's lemongrass and turmeric come out through smoke. Wongnai's Joey, whose Chiang Mai recipe grills a whole kilo of it, answers the inevitable question in his comments: it's like sai ua sausage — but no coconut milk. A reader answers the second question: it eats like hor mok, only grilled instead of steamed, and wrapped instead of cupped.

And the eating is the point. Krua.co's air-fryer version finishes with a northern verdict — ลำแต้ลำว่า, delicious beyond argument — and every source serves it the same way: torn open hot, with steamed sticky rice, and if you have it, nam phrik num and a pile of blanched vegetables.

The Chiang Mai pot (Wongnai): a full kilo of fatty mince, two eggs, and a richer paste — dried chillies, garlic, shallots, lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime zest, roasted coriander seed and a half-spoon of good shrimp paste — loosened with a coffee cup of water, parcelled at 100 g and grilled over charcoal. The air-fryer pot (Krua.co's แอ๊บจิ้นหมู): 400 g mince, four eggs, coriander roots in the paste, 120 g parcels, 200°C for 20 minutes with a flip at ten. The weeknight pot (Lemon8): store-bought northern curry paste, a spoon of toasted rice to bind, pan-grilled flat parcels — or foil in a 180°C oven when there is no leaf to be had.

The Method

  1. The paste. Pound the soaked chillies, lemongrass, galangal, turmeric, shallots and garlic until fine. Nothing is fried, nothing is bloomed — the paste goes into the pork raw, and the grill cooks it inside the leaf.
  2. The mix. Knead the paste through the minced pork, then work in the egg, spring onion, coriander and kaffir lime leaves, and season with fish sauce. Krua.co and Lemon8 both rest the mix fifteen minutes so the herbs soak in; Lemon8 notes it wraps more cleanly chilled.
  3. The leaf. Singe the banana leaves over a flame until they soften, wipe them, and use them doubled — Wongnai's rule is two layers per parcel, about 100 g of mix, folded flat and pinned with a bamboo toothpick. Flat parcels cook through; fat ones scorch outside and stay pink in.
  4. The grill. Low coals, medium-low gas, or a 170°C oven — about 30 minutes, turning. Wongnai's doneness test: the juices dripping from the parcel run clear and the leaf is just starting to dry. His warning: don't grill until dry, or the pork turns hard.
  5. The serve. Open the parcel hot and eat with steamed sticky rice. The northern table adds nam phrik num and blanched vegetables — and the smell of singed banana leaf does the rest.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The eggs. One per 500 g of pork (Cheewajit), two per kilo (Wongnai), or a gluttonous four per 400 g in Krua.co's air-fryer version. The egg binds the mix and keeps the parcel tender; northern home cooks add as many as the richness of the pork demands.

Shrimp paste in the paste. Wongnai's Chiang Mai paste works in half a spoon of กะปิ, plus kaffir lime zest and roasted coriander seed. Cheewajit's cookbook paste is pure herbs — chilli, lemongrass, galangal, turmeric, shallot, garlic — and Krua.co's lands in between, dropping the shrimp paste but adding coriander roots. The herb core is the constant.

How it's seasoned. Fish sauce alone (Cheewajit), or fish sauce plus a spoon of ผงปรุงรส seasoning powder (Wongnai, Krua.co). The powder is the shortcut that makes the northern funk friendlier; the cookbook pot stays old-school.

Toasted rice. Lemon8's home version adds a spoon of ข้าวคั่ว — toasted rice powder — to bind the mix and add a nutty edge. None of the three traditional sources use it; it is an Isan habit leaking north, harmless but not orthodox.

The cooking. Low charcoal for 30 minutes (Cheewajit, Wongnai), a 170°C oven (Cheewajit), a 200°C air fryer with a mid-cook flip (Krua.co), or a covered pan and even foil (Lemon8). Every source agrees on the principle: low and slow, never a hard sear.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Calling it hor mok, or steaming it like one. Hor mok is a coconut-milk custard steamed in a cup. Aab mu is dry-ground pork with no coconut milk, wrapped and grilled — the singed leaf is the flavour, and a steamer produces none of it.
  • Coconut milk anywhere in the recipe. Wongnai's Joey is asked point-blank in the comments whether aab is like sai ua sausage, and answers: yes — but no coconut milk. Add cream and the parcel turns to paste.
  • Lean mince. The north uses pork with fat, and Lemon8's cook is explicit: 20–30% fat, or a spoon of lard, or the parcel comes out dry. Supermarket 5%-fat mince is the quickest way to ruin it.
  • Baking paper, or foil straight through. Foil is Lemon8's last-resort fallback for the ovenless — the leaf's smoke is not decorative. If you must, grill uncovered for the final minutes to fake a little of it.
  • Green or red curry paste from a jar. The aab paste is dried chilli, lemongrass, galangal, turmeric, shallot and garlic — turmeric-forward, no coriander seed, no cumin, nothing green. A shop-bought central-Thai paste takes the dish to a different region.
  • Overcooking to "food-safe". Pork mince needs cooking through, but Wongnai's tip stands: the moment the drips run clear, take the parcel off. Keep grilling and the meat sets hard — a northern cook's first complaint about western grills.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Banana leaves: frozen packs at Thai and Chinese grocers (defrost and singe them just the same); fresh at well-stocked Thai grocers. Foil works as Lemon8's fallback, but buy the leaf — it is half the dish.
  • Minced pork: 15–20% fat from any supermarket; if you can only get lean, knead in a spoon of lard or neutral oil.
  • Fresh turmeric: at Indian and Thai grocers; in a pinch, half a teaspoon of ground turmeric per 500 g of pork gets the colour and most of the earth.
  • Galangal and kaffir lime leaves: fresh or frozen at Thai grocers — don't substitute ginger or lime zest; the leaf slices are structural.
  • Dried bird's eye chillies: small bags at Thai grocers, or whole dried chillies from a world-foods aisle; soak them soft before pounding.
  • Sticky rice: Thai glutinous rice, soaked and steamed — or serve the parcels with jasmine rice and no one in the north is watching.
  • No charcoal? A gas grill on low, a 170°C oven, or a 200°C air fryer with a flip halfway (Krua.co's own method) all produce a proper parcel.
