Pla Meuk Yang (ปลาหมึกย่าง)

Skewered squid browning over charcoal, yellowed with a turmeric dip and crisped in lime water, sliced over a pounded nam jim seafood — the smell of every Thai night market.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · 4 servings · ~45 minutes

The Squid on Every Cart

ปลาหมึกย่าง is the street food with no fixed address: a cart, a brazier, skewers of squid turning over low charcoal in every night market, temple fair and school gate in the country. Wongnai answers the question every first-timer asks — why is the squid yellow? Because of the turmeric in the marinade, which colours it and gives the aroma the stall's regulars recognise from a distance.

The treatment is a short three-step build. The squid is scrubbed in salt water to pull off the slime and the fishiness, then dipped in turmeric and dark soy for fifteen minutes, then given the step that makes or breaks the texture: a soak in น้ำปูนใส, lime water, for another fifteen minutes to crisp the flesh before it meets the coals. TrueID's street-vendor exposé does it differently — no lime at all: the squid is massaged with coarse salt until the flesh firms up under your hands, rinsed, and soaked in ice water before it's skewered and packed on ice.

And the grill is only half the dish. The other half is the น้ำจิ้มซีฟู้ด, the pounded seafood dip — coriander root, garlic, chillies, palm sugar, fish sauce and lime. Kapook's round-up of nine dipping sauces for grilled squid shows it is a genre of its own: the classic pounded one, a peanut-brittle version, a syrup-and-holy-basil version. The squid is the carrier; the sauce is the argument.

The Method

  1. The wash. Scrub the squid in salt water — Wongnai's ratio is 2 handfuls of salt per 1 kg of squid — rubbing until the foam and slime lessen, then rinse. This kills the fishiness and strips the slippery coating.
  2. The yellow. Mix water with the turmeric and dark soy, pour over the squid to cover and marinate for 15 minutes. Both Wongnai and the TrueID vendor recipes build the colour this way.
  3. The crisp. Soak the squid in lime water for 15 minutes, then rinse clean — Wongnai's tip, and the step that keeps the flesh crunchy instead of soft. No lime water? The vendor's ice-water bath does the same job.
  4. The sauce. Pound the coriander roots, garlic and sliced jinda chillies together, then season with salt, palm sugar, fish sauce and lime juice, loosening with a little boiled water.
  5. The grill. Grill over low heat — Wongnai says charcoal cooks it better than an electric grill — turning until just cooked. TrueID's warning: do not overcook, or the squid turns tough.
  6. The serve. Slice into bite-size pieces and serve over the sauce — or with the sauce in a little cup beside it, stall-style.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The crispness step. Wongnai soaks in น้ำปูนใส (lime water) for 15 minutes; TrueID's street recipe soaks in water mixed with ปูนขาว (slaked lime) for 15–20 minutes; TrueID's vendor exposé uses no lime at all — just the coarse-salt massage until the flesh firms, then an ice-water bath. Three routes to the same crunch.

The yellow. Wongnai marinates the squid in turmeric and dark soy before grilling; the TrueID vendor and street recipes dip it in turmeric water (or food colouring — the street recipe admits it) at the skewer stage. Turmeric gives aroma as well as colour; the colouring is purely cosmetic.

The sauce build. Wongnai pounds coriander root, garlic and red jinda chillies and loosens the dip with boiled water; TrueID's no-MSG version blends green bird's eye chillies with a much wetter ratio — 5½ tablespoons each of fish sauce and lime juice. Pounded stays chunky; blended goes smooth.

The squid. Wongnai specifies ปลาหมึกกระดอง, cuttlefish. The vendor page grills whatever came off the boat: ปลาหมึกยักษ์ (giant squid) necks sliced, and ปลาหมึกสาย tentacles. The recipe is the same; the cephalopod negotiates.

Heat. Wongnai says low heat throughout. TrueID's street recipe doesn't fix a temperature but fixes the failure mode: overcooked squid goes เหนียว — tough. Both agree the grill is short and patient.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Cooking it into rubber. Western recipes braise, boil or grill squid until it is bulletproof. Thai method is low heat and just-cooked — TrueID warns explicitly that overcooking makes it tough.
  • Skipping the crispness step. The lime-water soak (or the vendor's salt-and-ice treatment) is what gives market squid its snap. Marinate-and-grill without it and the texture is flat.
  • Sweet chilli sauce from a bottle. Pla meuk yang is paired with a pounded nam jim seafood — coriander root, garlic, chillies, palm sugar, fish sauce, lime. Bottled sweet chilli is a different dish wearing the same name.
  • Long marinades. The Thai treatment is a 15-minute dip — turmeric, dark soy, done. An overnight soy-garlic marinade changes the flavour and makes the flesh weep on the grill.
  • Yellow for yellow's sake. Some versions dye the squid without turmeric. The Thai sources use turmeric — colour and aroma — and the street recipes that admit to food colouring say so openly.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Squid or cuttlefish: UK fishmongers and supermarket fish counters sell both; frozen whole squid and cuttlefish at Thai grocers work the same.
  • Food-grade lime (ปูนแดง / ปูนขาว, calcium hydroxide): Thai grocers carry it for betel and cooking. If you can't find it, use the vendor's method instead — salt massage and an ice-water bath.
  • Palm sugar, fish sauce, jinda and bird's eye chillies: standard at any Thai grocer; most UK supermarkets now stock fish sauce and palm sugar too.
  • Coriander root: the roots attached to bunched coriander at Asian grocers — they carry the sauce's flavour; the leaves alone won't do it.
  • Charcoal: any BBQ charcoal works; keep the heat low and turn often, and the squid comes off before it tightens.
