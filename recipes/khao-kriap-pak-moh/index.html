Recipe · Thailand · The Dumpling Steamed On The Pot's Mouth

Khao Kriap Pak Moh (ข้าวเกรียบปากหม้อ)

A batter-thin rice skin steamed on a cloth stretched over the pot, folded in one move around a sweet-savoury pork, radish and peanut filling — the other half of every market dumpling stall in Thailand.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Serves 8 · ~55 minutes

The Dumpling That Names Its Steamer

ข้าวเกรียบปากหม้อ takes its name from where it is cooked: ปากหม้อ is the mouth of the pot, and the dish is made by stretching a white cloth tight across that mouth and steaming wafer-thin rounds of rice batter on it — ข้าวเกรียบ is the "rice crisp" the translucent skin is named after, even though the finished skin is soft, not crisp. The vendor's rhythm never changes: ladle, spread, cover, and the moment the skin turns glass-clear, a ball of filling goes in the middle and the skin is folded up and over with one scrape of the spatula.

It is the permanent stall-mate of sakoo sai moo, and the two usually share a filling. Kapook's recipe runs the same sweet-savoury pork — palm sugar, ground peanuts, radish — through both dishes, and notes that this is why the pair is always sold together: one filling, two wrappers, one stall. TrueID pitches the dish the other way round, as a money-maker — the same page teaches sakoo sai moo first, then this, as the two halves of a proper snack business.

The skin is the skill. Wongnai's vendor advice is all about the cloth: use white muslin, stretch it as tight as it will go, because the batter will not spread on a slack cloth — and sieve the batter twice so no lumps of raw flour spot the skins. The filling, every source agrees, must be cold before it touches the skin, or the steam from a warm filling turns the wrapper to glue in your hands.

The market-scale batch (TrueID): 250 g minced pork, 200 g roasted peanuts, 200 g sweet radish, palm sugar, light soy and a half teaspoon of salt — plus 300 g coconut cream thickened with rice flour, poured over the top. The classic thin skin (Kapook / Wongnai): 1 cup rice flour with just 2 tablespoons tapioca flour and 1 tablespoon arrowroot to 3 cups water — the old thin, translucent style. The coloured skins (Wongnai): swap part of the water for pandan water for green skins and butterfly pea water for blue, the way market stalls lay out white, green and blue dumplings side by side. The vegetarian stall (Krua): firm tofu in place of the pork, same radish, peanuts and seasoning.

The Method

  1. The filling. Pound peppercorns, coriander root and garlic to a paste, fry it in oil until it smells, then add the pork and break it up as it cooks — TrueID cooks the sugar into the pan early so it melts into the meat rather than sitting on it. Add the shallots and sweet radish, season with palm sugar and light soy, and fry until the filling is dry. Peanuts go in last, off the heat. Spread it out and let it cool completely — Wongnai is explicit that a warm filling steams the skins before they are wrapped.
  2. The batter. Whisk the rice flour, tapioca flour and arrowroot flour with the water until smooth. Aroifin rests the batter for 20 to 30 minutes so the flours absorb the water fully — rested batter makes a skin that steams even and does not tear — and Wongnai sieves it twice against lumps. Stir before every ladle, because the mixed flours settle.
  3. The cloth. Stretch a clean white cloth over the mouth of a wide pot of boiling water and tie it drum-tight. Both Wongnai and Kapook name this as the step that decides everything: on a slack cloth the batter puddles, the skins steam thick, and nothing peels off clean.
  4. The skins. Ladle a thin round of batter onto the cloth and spread it into a circle, cover the pot, and steam until the skin turns translucent — a matter of seconds. Aroifin's tip is a medium-high flame: hot steam sets the skin fast, and a fast-set skin is thin, clear and peels off the cloth in one piece.
  5. The fold. Drop a small ball of filling in the centre of each cooked skin, then fold the skin up and over it with a spatula — one scrape, one parcel, the vendor's move. Lift onto a tray brushed with garlic oil.
  6. The serve. Brush the tops with garlic oil and shower with fried garlic. Serve warm with lettuce leaves, fresh bird's eye chillies and coriander — wrap a dumpling in lettuce with a sliver of chilli, the way the stalls hand them over. TrueID's stall version adds a ladle of warm, lightly salted coconut cream over the top.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The coconut cream topping. TrueID finishes the dumplings with น้ำราดกะทิ — coconut cream thickened with a spoon of rice flour and a pinch of salt, warmed and poured over. Kapook, Wongnai and Aroifin use nothing but garlic oil and fried garlic. Both are genuine stall styles: the plain version is the classic Bangkok market one, the coconut-cream version a richer regional take — TrueID frames it as the version that sells.

The skin ratio. Kapook and Wongnai make the old thin skin: a cup of rice flour stiffened with only a spoon or two each of tapioca and arrowroot flours. Aroifin goes a full cup of tapioca flour to two cups of rice — nearly a third of the batter — for a thicker, springier skin that holds a bigger lump of filling, and says outright that less water gives you the thick-skin style. Same dish, two generations of skin.

Coloured skins. Wongnai and TrueID colour part of the batter with pandan water (green) and butterfly pea water (blue); Kapook and Aroifin make only white. The coloured versions are pure market style — the stalls lay out the three colours together, and the flavour of the skin is unchanged.

The filling's radish. All five sources use sweet pickled radish (ไชโป๊หวาน) here — unlike sakoo sai moo, where the sources split between sweet and salted. On this dish the sources agree, which is the rare thing worth noting itself.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Spring roll wrappers. The western shortcut is a shop-bought wheat wrapper, fried or steamed. The dish is a rice-flour skin steamed on cloth — change the wrapper and you have a different snack wearing the name.
  • Frying. Khao kriap pak moh is never fried. The name's "kriap" (crisp) is a leftover from the word's root — the finished skin is steamed, soft and translucent, and a fried version is a western invention.
  • Skipping the arrowroot. Arrowroot flour (แป้งเท้ายายม่อม) is what lets the skin peel off the cloth in one piece and stay supple as it cools. Every Thai source includes it; wheat-flour substitutes skip it, and the skins stick and crack.
  • A thick dumpling skin. The classic skin is batter-thin — ladled and spread, not rolled and cut. The thick wrappers western recipes reach for bury the filling and miss the whole point of the dish: you should see the pork through the wrapper.
  • A dipping sauce. Like sakoo sai moo, this dumpling is not dipped. It is dressed with garlic oil and fried garlic and eaten in lettuce with fresh chilli. A sweet chilli dip is the surest western tell.
  • Warm filling, wrapped. The filling must be cold when it meets the skin. Wrap it warm and the steam it gives off turns the wrapper to paste — the one instruction every Thai source repeats.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Rice flour: Thai rice flour (แป้งข้าวเจ้า) from Thai or East Asian grocers; it is finely milled, not the coarser supermarket "rice flour" sold for gluten-free baking.
  • Tapioca flour and arrowroot flour: both at Thai grocers, health food shops and larger supermarkets (tapioca is often labelled tapioca starch).
  • Sweet pickled radish (ไชโป๊หวาน, chai po wan): jars at Thai grocers, shredded and in a brown-sugar brine.
  • The cloth: a large piece of clean white muslin or cheesecloth, tied drum-tight over the pot — a flat-weave cotton is better than a thick towel.
  • Steaming: a wide pot with a lid that clears the cloth's surface; a bamboo steamer is not used here — the pot mouth is the steamer.
  • For TrueID's coconut cream finish: one small tin of coconut cream, warmed with a spoon of rice flour and a pinch of salt.
