Kung Sarong (กุ้งโสร่ง)

The royal court snack in one bite: a sweet prawn marinated in the three friends — coriander root, garlic, white pepper — wrapped in fine mee sua threads and fried until the sarong turns to gold.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Serves 4 as a starter · ~45 minutes

The Prawn That Wears A Sarong

The name does all the work. โสร่ง (sarong) is the wrapped garment of everyday Southeast Asia — and here the garment is made of noodles: fine mee sua threads wound round a marinated prawn, fried until they set into a crisp golden sheath. Strip the name and the dish loses its poetry; the Thai name is the recipe's description.

The dish belongs to the old royal snack table (อาหารว่างชาววัง). Kapook traces it back to the original สร่ง — seasoned pork (หมูสร่ง) or chicken wound in the same noodle sarong — and the prawn version is the later, lighter refinement: the same craft, but with the clean sweetness and bounce of fresh prawn in place of rich pork. Wongnai's recipe, made with chef Gino, frames it as court food any home cook can pull off — the ingredients are ordinary; the work is in the wrapping.

The craft, in fact, is the dish. The seasoning is deliberately lean — coriander root, garlic, white pepper, salt — because the prawn must taste of prawn and the noodle of crispness. The kitchen technique sits in three small steps the sources all insist on: score the prawn's belly so it fries straight, wrap the threads snug but not strangling, and rest the wrap until it is dry before it meets the oil. Get those three right and the sarong crackles; miss them and it is just another battered prawn.

Tamarind dip (Kapook's classic, น้ำจิ้มมะขาม): 40 ml wet tamarind paste, 125 ml water, 75 g palm sugar, 25 g white sugar, 1 tsp salt, 2 tsp fish sauce — simmer together, stirring, until the sugars dissolve and the dip thickens slightly; cool before serving. Plum-calamansi dip (Wongnai): 200 g plum sauce, 100 g sweet chicken dipping sauce, 50 g chilli sauce, 2 tsp salt, 2 tbsp lime juice, the juice of 2 calamansi, 2 tsp grated calamansi zest, 1 tsp chilli flakes — stir, taste for sour-sweet-salty-hot, finish with the zest and toasted sesame. Spring-roll dip and Sriracha: Kapook's second recipe cooks sugar, vinegar and salt with pounded garlic and chilli, then shredded carrot and radish; Golden Mountain simply serves the prawns with Sriracha.

The Method

  1. The prawn. Shell the prawns but keep the tail — it is the handle, and the bit that pokes out of the sarong. Devein, then score the belly lightly with the knife and bend the prawn straight. Kapook and Wongnai both do this so the prawn fries long and straight instead of curling into a ball.
  2. The three friends. Pound the coriander roots, garlic and white pepper into a paste — the สามเกลอ, the trio behind most old Thai marinades. Rub over the prawns with the salt, soy sauce and sugar, and rest 5–15 minutes. Wongnai covers and chills for 10–15; Golden Mountain is happy with 5. No long cures: the prawn should keep its own sweetness.
  3. The sarong. Soak the mee sua only until pliable, then drain and dry it properly. Kapook's trick: dip the strands in beaten egg before wrapping, which helps them grip and fry crisper. Wind each prawn from head to tail, two layers, until the body is covered — snug, but not strangling.
  4. The rest. Leave the wrapped prawns until the noodle threads are dry to the touch. Wet threads steam in the oil instead of crisping; every source either says this or implies it.
  5. The fry. Deep-fry in medium to medium-high oil until the sarong is golden, about 3–5 minutes depending on the size of the prawns. Phol Food Mafia pins the temperature at 160°C; Seagull, for the air-fryer road, runs 180°C for ten minutes, flips, and gives five more.
  6. The serve. Drain on a rack, not paper alone, and serve at once — the crisp has a short life. Plate with the dip, coriander and a few shreds of chilli; Wongnai adds crispy-fried kale, and the Thai table adds hot jasmine rice if the starter is being stretched into a meal.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The noodle. Kapook, Wongnai and Seagull wrap in เส้นหมี่ซั่ว, the thin wheat threads (Phuket mee sua is the named favourite). Golden Mountain uses plain rice vermicelli (เส้นหมี่). Phol Food Mafia goes furthest: half-cooked capellini pasta, deliberately boiled for only half its packet time so it stays dry enough to crisp. All three roads lead to the same golden cage, but the threads are different.

Making the wrap stick. Kapook dips the soaked strands in beaten egg — grip, colour and extra crunch. Phol Food Mafia dusts the prawns with plain flour instead. Wongnai and Golden Mountain wrap bare, trusting dry threads and snug winding. The disagreement is honest: every cook fights the same problem, and picks a different glue.

The dip. The widest split in the dish. Kapook gives three: tamarind dip, spring-roll dip, and the plum-calamansi mix. Wongnai stops at the plum-calamansi mix. Golden Mountain reaches for Sriracha. Phol Food Mafia serves ajad — the vinegar-pickle relish of cucumber, shallot and chilli. There is no canonical sauce; there is a canonical rule (sour-sweet-hot against salty-crisp) and each kitchen dresses it differently.

The fry. Most sources say medium to medium-high oil and a golden colour. Phol Food Mafia specifies 160°C. Seagull skips the wok entirely for an air fryer at 180°C. The shared bottom line: hot oil, dry wrap, small batches.

The spelling. Kapook titles the dish กุ้งสร่ง — the older สร่ง form, without the เ — while Wongnai, Seagull and the rest write กุ้งโสร่ง. Both names are in circulation; โสร่ง, the sarong garment itself, is the word the dish was named after.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Thick noodles, wonton skins, batter. The sarong must be fine mee sua threads — the crisp lacework is the dish's signature. Wrap a prawn in egg noodles or a wonton skin and you have made a different, heavier thing; the court version is built on the thinnest thread available.
  • Fully cooked noodles. Boiling the threads to readiness before wrapping — the western pasta instinct — fills them with water, and water is the enemy of crisp. Phol Food Mafia says it outright: half-cook, don't rinse, or the fry takes forever and stays soft. Soak, don't boil.
  • Wet wraps straight into the fryer. Noodle threads straight from the water steam instead of fry. The Thai recipes rest the wrapped prawns until the surface dries — skip that and the sarong comes out pale and leathery.
  • Low oil, crowded pan. A timid fryer makes greasy prawns. The sources all fry hot and in batches so the threads puff and set the moment they touch the oil.
  • Heavy marinades. Oyster sauce, hoisin, five-spice — the kind of armour western prawn starters wear — bury the point. The Thai seasoning is coriander root, garlic and white pepper, because the prawn is the flavour; the marinade is a whisper, not a coat.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Mee sua (หมี่ซั่ว): thin wheat vermicelli at Thai and Chinese grocers — Phuket brands are the ones Thai recipes name. Plain rice vermicelli (เส้นหมี่) works, and in a real pinch Phol Food Mafia's capellini hack does too.
  • Coriander root (รากผักชี): buy coriander with the roots still attached at Asian grocers; the stems below the leaves stand in when roots aren't sold.
  • Prawns: raw shell-on or peeled king or tiger prawns from any UK fish counter or freezer — thaw fully and dry well, especially the tail, or the oil will spit.
  • Calamansi (น้ำส้มซ่า): scarce in UK shops; lime is the working substitute in the plum dip.
  • Palm sugar (น้ำตาลปี๊บ): blocks at Thai grocers; light brown sugar carries the tamarind dip if palm sugar isn't to hand.
  • Duck eggs: Kapook lists them alongside chicken eggs for the noodle dip — chicken eggs are the practical UK default and the result is the same.
