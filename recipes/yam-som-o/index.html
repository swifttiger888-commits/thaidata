Yam Som O (ยำส้มโอ)

Pomelo segments tossed with roasted coconut, dried shrimp and fried shallots in a palm-sugar, tamarind and coconut-cream dressing — sweet first, sour after.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 29 September 2026 · Serves 2–3 · ~35 minutes

The Salad Built for a Pomelo That Isn't Sweet

Pholfoodmafia puts the origin in one line: Thai wisdom says that when the pomelo in the house is not sweet enough, you stop eating it and start cooking it — the texture of the fruit becomes the star of a yam. The result is the most luxurious of the Thai salads: strands of pomelo holding a sweet-sour dressing between their cells, against a crunch of roasted coconut, dried shrimp, peanuts and fried shallots.

The dressing is where the recipes split into two schools. The old school simmers it — palm sugar, fish sauce and tamarind water melted together over heat, with BlogGang and Kapook stirring in coconut cream that has been bubbled without splitting. The quick school stirs it cold — Maggi's version is nam prik pao, lime juice and palm sugar in a bowl, no heat at all.

The recipe here is BlogGang's ยำส้มโอโบราณ, a family version of the dish as served at the old เมธาวลัย ศรแดง on Ratchadamnoen: river prawns boiled in the shell for their colour, shredded chicken breast, and the instruction every good version shares — tear the pomelo gently, toss it gently, and taste for sweet first, sour after, never salty. Maggi adds the shopping rule: pick a pomelo with dry, firm flesh — ขาวใหญ่, ขาวน้ำผึ้ง or ขาวแป้น — so the segments hold their shape instead of collapsing into the dressing.

The chef's version (Pholfoodmafia): 2 cups pomelo, 8 blanched diced prawns, 4 tbsp roasted coconut — dressing pounded from roasted chillies, shallots and garlic with smoked catfish, then simmered with 3 tbsp palm sugar, fish sauce and tamarind. The quick version (Maggi): 200 g pomelo, 300 g blanched prawns, cashews instead of peanuts — a cold nam-prik-pao-and-lime dressing, no simmering. The roasted-paste version (Aroifin): 300 g pomelo with a pounded paste of roasted dried chilli, dried shrimp and roasted shallot and garlic, plus makrut leaf shreds.

The Method

  1. The tear. Pomelo segments into strands, pulled apart gently — BlogGang warns that bruised flesh ruins the salad's texture. Set aside.
  2. The protein. Chicken breast boiled and shredded; river prawns blanched in the shell — peeling after keeps their colour vivid.
  3. The cream. Coconut cream simmered until it bubbles without splitting, then left to cool.
  4. The dressing. Palm sugar, water, fish sauce, tamarind water and chilli jam melted together over low heat.
  5. The toss. Pomelo strands, peanuts, dried shrimp, fried shallots, fried garlic, roasted coconut, roasted chillies and lime juice in a bowl; the dressing poured in gradually, never all at once.
  6. The taste. Toss gently — hard mixing crushes the pomelo — and check the balance: sweet first, sour after, never salty. Plate.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Simmered or stirred dressing. Four sources simmer the palm sugar, fish sauce and tamarind into a dressing; Maggi stirs it cold — chilli jam, lime and palm sugar, no heat. The simmered dressing is deeper and keeps; the cold one is a five-minute lunch.

Coconut cream. Only BlogGang and Kapook bubble coconut cream and fold it into the dressing. The other three keep the dressing clear. The cream makes the salad richer and slightly blurry; the clear dressing shows the pomelo's colour.

Where the sourness comes from. Everyone but Maggi uses tamarind water; Pholfoodmafia and Aroifin stop there, while BlogGang, Kapook and Maggi also squeeze in lime. Tamarind-sour is round; lime-sour is sharp — the old-school plates like both.

The umami base. Pholfoodmafia pounds smoked catfish into the dressing; Aroifin pounds roasted dried chilli, dried shrimp and roasted shallot and garlic into a paste; BlogGang and Kapook rely on chilli jam straight from the jar. Same idea, three intensities.

Chicken. BlogGang and Kapook add shredded chicken breast alongside the prawns — the royal version's second protein. The others are prawn-only. Kapook notes minced pork or blanched squid work just as well.

The crunch set. Cashews belong to Maggi alone; makrut leaf shreds to Pholfoodmafia and Aroifin alone. Peanuts, roasted coconut and dried shrimp are the trio nobody drops.

Sweetness. Pholfoodmafia runs 3 tbsp of palm sugar to 2 tbsp fish sauce — noticeably sweeter than BlogGang's and Aroifin's equal 2-and-2. The chef's version leans dessert; the home versions lean savoury.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Grapefruit instead of pomelo. Grapefruit is bitter and gushes juice; the pomelo's dry, sweet, firm flesh is the whole reason the salad holds together. Different fruit, different dish.
  • Lettuce-salad dressing logic. Olive oil, honey, vinaigrettes — a yam dressing is palm sugar, fish sauce and tamarind. There is no oil in it.
  • Skipping the crunch trio. Roasted coconut, dried shrimp and peanuts are not optional toppings; they are the texture of the dish.
  • Tossing hard. Every Thai source warns about this in the recipe itself. Vigorous mixing turns pomelo strands to pulp.
  • Sweet chilli sauce from a bottle. The dressing's smoke and sourness come from nam prik pao, palm sugar and tamarind simmered together — bottled sweet chilli tastes like a takeaway.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Pomelo: supermarkets stock it in winter and spring; Thai and Chinese grocers carry it year-round. Look for heavy fruit with dry, firm flesh — Maggi's rule of thumb.
  • Prawns: raw shell-on prawns from any fish counter; Thai grocers have river prawns if you want the real thing.
  • Dried shrimp: Chinese and Thai grocers — the small amber ones. Crisp-fry them briefly for the เคยคั่ว effect, or buy them already fried.
  • Nam prik pao, palm sugar, tamarind paste: all standard Thai-grocer jars and blocks.
  • Fried shallots and fried garlic: fry your own — thin slices, low oil, patience — or buy the ready-fried tubs (หอมเจียว) at Thai grocers.
  • Makrut lime leaves, if you add the Pholfoodmafia shreds: fresh or frozen at Thai grocers.
