Recipe · Thailand · The Fish-Sauce Glaze Built On Crisp Skin

Pla Kapong Tod Nam Pla (ปลากะพงทอดน้ำปลา)

A whole sea bass fried until the skin shatters, then dressed with a bubbling glaze of fish sauce and palm sugar — the dish that proves the humblest Thai pantry can dress a restaurant fish.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 2–3 · ~35 minutes

The Glaze That Needs The Skin

ปลากะพงทอดน้ำปลา is a two-part trick, and the parts only make sense together. The fish — a whole sea bass, butterflied open, or sometimes cut into pieces — is fried bone-dry and brittle, skin first. Then a glaze of fish sauce, palm sugar and water is boiled in a pan until it thickens and turns mellow, and poured over the top. The crisp skin drinks the glaze without going soft, and every source here insists the two must meet at the table: aroifin's cook pours the sauce at the last moment, or serves it in a cup on the side, "to keep the fish crisp".

It is the dish Thai cooks order to check a seafood restaurant's seriousness — which is exactly why home-cook sources like TrueID's are titled "อร่อยเหมือนร้าน" (delicious like the restaurant) and Kapook's promises "ภัตตาคาร" (fine-dining) quality "at a light price". The secret is not the fish. It is frying discipline: bone-dry fish, very hot oil, no flipping until the skin has set, and a final blast of high heat so the fish comes out of the oil dry.

Almost every source serves the fish with a second sauce — a sour-spicy น้ำยำ, usually built on green mango, lime, shallots and bird's eye chillies — so the plate lands both sweet-salty and sharp. Wongnai's home cook says it simply: she got a sea bass, made the fish, made a mango yam to go with it, and dinner was done.

The Recipe (home version, from TrueID / Aroifin)

ปลากะพงขาวSea bass, whole, butterflied1 fish (800 g–1 kg)
แป้งทอดกรอบCrispy frying flour2–4 tbsp
น้ำปลาFish sauce (good quality)2–3 tbsp
น้ำตาลปี๊บPalm sugar2–3 tbsp
น้ำเปล่าWater3 tbsp
น้ำมันสำหรับทอดOil for deep-frying1 litre (to cover)

The optional green-mango yam (TrueID, Wongnai, Pantip): 2½ tbsp lime juice, 2½ tbsp fish sauce, 1 tbsp palm sugar, 50 g sour green mango, 20 g shallots, a handful of bird's eye chillies, 50 g cashew nuts — shake the dressing until the sugar melts, then toss everything together. The brand pot (Deksomboon): the same dish at restaurant scale — 70 g fish sauce, 130 g white sugar, 250 g water boiled 3 minutes, fish floured twice (wet–flour–wet–flour) and fried 7–10 minutes, the sauce served in a bowl on the side. The Kapook marinade school: coat the fish in oyster sauce and seasoning sauce, dust with corn flour, then fry — the marinade, not the glaze, seasons the flesh.

The Method

  1. The butterfly. Clean the fish, then split it from the top down the backbone with the belly left attached, so it lies flat (แล่แผ่กลาง). Pat it absolutely dry — aroifin warns a damp fish spits oil and the flour will not cling.
  2. The flour. Dust a thin, even coat of crispy frying flour over both sides. TrueID's cook skips this step and fries the bare fish; both schools work, but the floured skin comes out thicker and louder.
  3. The first side. Heat enough oil to cover the fish — aroifin says 170–180°C, tested with a pinch of flour that floats up in a foam — and lower the fish in skin side down. Do not move it until the skin has set and coloured, 6–7 minutes on TrueID's clock. Kapook adds: fry medium, then crank the heat at the end so the fish stays crisp without drinking oil, and do not flip it back and forth — that is how the flesh turns to mush.
  4. The second side. Flip once and fry the other side until golden, 3–4 minutes. Lift out, hold the fish up to drain, and rest it on paper.
  5. The glaze. Palm sugar, fish sauce and water in a small pan over medium-low heat. Stir until the sugar melts, then let it simmer until it thickens slightly and smells mellow (TrueID: "ผัดจนน้ำงวดเข้าที่ และน้ำปลาหอมดี" — reduced and fragrant). Use real fish sauce; aroifin says the good stuff is กลมกล่อม — round, not sharply salty.
  6. The dressing. Pour the glaze over the fish at the table, or set it in a small bowl alongside (Deksomboon's and aroifin's way, if you want the skin to stay shatter-crisp). Serve with hot jasmine rice and the green-mango yam if you made it.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Flour or bare skin. TrueID's cook fries the fish naked — just dry and very hot oil. Aroifin, Deksomboon and Pantip dust with crispy frying flour; Deksomboon doubles down with a wet-flour-wet-flour coat. Kapook takes a third road: marinate in oyster sauce and seasoning sauce, then corn flour. Same dish, three different skins.

The glaze ratios. The home recipes agree on roughly 2:2:3 (fish sauce : palm sugar : water). Deksomboon's brand kitchen is the outlier — 70 g fish sauce to 130 g white sugar, almost twice as sweet, and white sugar instead of palm. Kapook skips water entirely, melting palm sugar and fish sauce in a little oil into a caramel.

Pour it or pass it. TrueID and Pantip pour the glaze over the fish. Aroifin and Deksomboon serve it in a bowl on the side — the same sauce, but the fish stays crisp longer. Aroifin states the principle: pour it if you are eating now, pass it if you are not.

Whole fish or pieces. Kapook, TrueID, Aroifin and Wongnai butterfly a whole 800 g–1 kg fish. Pantip's camping-cook version uses 500 g of fish pieces, floured and pan-fried in 250 ml of oil — the same dish scaled down for a home stove, since not every kitchen owns a litre of oil.

The second sauce. Wongnai, TrueID and Pantip all pair the fish with a green-mango yam (ยำมะม่วง). Kapook's first recipe serves a tomato-shallot น้ำยำ, and its Gourmet & Cuisine version goes full banquet — seafood dip, pomelo yam, mango yam, and two aubergine dishes as sides.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Batter. A beer-battered or panko-crusted "Thai fried fish" is a different dish. This one is dusted lightly, or not at all — the star is the fish's own skin, fried brittle.
  • Pan-fried fillets. The dish is built on a whole butterflied fish, skin down in deep oil. A skinless fillet seared in a splash of oil cannot give the crackle the glaze is designed to sit on.
  • Teriyaki-style glazes. Soy sauce, honey and ginger are not the sauce. The glaze is fish sauce and palm sugar — savoury-fermented and caramel-brown, not soy-dark. Swap the fish sauce out and the dish loses its name.
  • Olive oil, low heat. Olive oil cannot reach the 170–180°C the fish needs without smoking, and lukewarm oil steams the skin instead of crisping it — the fish comes out pale, greasy and limp. Thai sources use palm or vegetable oil, and finish hot.
  • Skipping the rest. Draining the fish on paper and letting it sit a minute before glazing is not optional garnish advice — it is the difference between crisp skin and a soggy one. Same for the final blast of high heat.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Sea bass: ปลากะพงขาว is Asian sea bass (barramundi) — frozen whole at Thai grocers. European sea bass from any UK fish counter works just as well: same firm white flesh, thin skin that crisps beautifully. Ask the fishmonger to butterfly it (split down the back, belly on).
  • Crispy frying flour (แป้งทอดกรอบ): Thai tempura-style mix (Gogi and similar) at Thai grocers and online Thai supermarkets; fine cornflour does the job in a pinch.
  • Fish sauce: use a real Thai brand — Squid, Megachef, Tiparos. The glaze has three ingredients, so the fish sauce cannot hide.
  • Palm sugar: solid discs or jars of the soft kind at Thai grocers; soft light brown sugar is the closest common substitute.
  • Oil: rapeseed, sunflower or groundnut — anything neutral with a high smoke point. One litre for a deep pan, or follow Pantip and pan-fry pieces in 250 ml.
  • Green mango: unripe mango from Asian grocers (or a very firm supermarket one) for the yam; plain lime dressing is fine if you cannot find one.
