Chor Muang (ช่อม่วง)

Butterfly-pea dough crimped petal by petal into a tiny flower, hiding a sweet-salty pork and peanut filling — the royal court's dumpling, made to be looked at before it is eaten.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Makes 40 dumplings · ~1 hour 15 minutes

A Bouquet You Eat

The name says what the plate should look like: ช่อ (chor) is a bouquet — a cluster of flowers — and ม่วง (muang) is purple. A proper plate of chor muang is a dozen tiny violet blooms arranged like a posy, each one a dumpling of rice flour dough dyed with butterfly pea flowers and pinched, layer by layer, into petals with a pair of special tweezers.

Thai sources agree on where it began: the royal court of the early Rattanakosin era, presumed to date to the reign of King Rama II (r. 1809–1824). Sriwangying — the Bangkok cooking school whose teachers trained at the palace artisan school — describes it as a sweet originally made as an offering inside the palace walls before it spread to the outside world; Aroifin calls it the court snack with the reputation "รูปสวย รวยรส" — beautiful to look at, rich in flavour. Both note the practical consequence of all that craft: the dumplings are made fresh, keep poorly, and are usually ordered from shops that supply Thai sweets for weddings and other auspicious events.

The colour is chemistry, not dye. Butterfly pea flowers steep a clear blue; a squeeze of lime turns the same water purple — the anthocyanins in the flower respond to the acid. The dough itself is the other palace trick: rice flour, tapioca, arrowroot and a little glutinous flour, stirred over heat until it sets, so the petals hold their crimp through the steam and the finished skin goes faintly translucent. Inside sits the filling Thai cooks call หวาน ๆ เค็ม ๆ — sweet and salty together: meat fried dry with coriander root, garlic and pepper, sweet pickled radish, palm sugar and roasted peanuts.

The Recipe (savoury home version, from Naturalpalm / ผึ้ง)

To serve: fried garlic (กระเทียมเจียว), lettuce (ผักกาดหอม), coriander and bird's-eye chillies (พริกขี้หนูสวน). The palace chicken pot (Sriwangying): ½ kg chicken breast steamed 15–20 minutes (keep the cooking water), minced, then fried with 5 tbsp of the root paste, 250 g onion, 6 tbsp sugar, 1 tbsp salt and ½ cup ground peanuts — if the filling dries out, the saved chicken water goes back in; dough is three flours only (1 cup rice, 1 tbsp arrowroot, 2 tbsp tapioca) and the petals are crimped in three offset rings. The sweet-filling school (Aroifin's main recipe): a true ไส้หวาน, the sweet filling of the original court sweet. Aroifin's ไส้เค็ม variant: 250 g minced pork or chicken with 200 g sweet pickled radish, ½ cup shallots, 4 coriander roots, palm sugar, white soy sauce, pepper and a squeeze of lime.

The Method

  1. The paste. Pound coriander root, garlic and white peppercorns into a paste. Fry it in hot oil over medium heat until the smell rises — this trio of aromatics is the base of the filling and the thing you should smell in every bite.
  2. The filling. Add the minced pork and fry until cooked, breaking it up fine. Add the shallots, then the sweet pickled radish and coconut sugar; when the sugar melts into the meat, season with soy sauce and salt. Fry until the pan is dry — a wet filling bursts the wrapper — then fold in the peanuts and take it off the heat to cool. Roll into balls about 1.5 cm across.
  3. The purple. Steep butterfly pea flowers in hot water, squeeze the colour out, and stir in the lime juice until the water turns from blue to purple. Strain through muslin.
  4. The dough. Mix the four flours, then gradually stir in the purple water and strain through a sieve. Pour into a pan and stir over low heat until the mixture dries and clumps into a dough; rest it a minute or two, then knead on a surface dusted with แป้งนวล (a half-and-half mix of rice flour and tapioca) until smooth and no longer sticky.
  5. The wrap. Flatten a piece of dough into a round disc, set a ball of filling in the middle and close the dough around it into a neat ball. Work fast — the dough dries and stiffens in the open air, which is also why the finished dumplings should sit covered or in a box until they steam.
  6. The petals. This is the step that makes it chor muang. With the crimping tweezers — the แหนบจับจีบ, sold at Thai sweets-supply shops in two mouth shapes, leaf and straight — pinch rings of petals from the base of the ball upward, offsetting each ring between the ones below. Dust the tweezers in flour every few pinches so they don't tear the dough; pinch decisively, because the crimp marks are what you will see after steaming.
  7. The steam. Lay the flowers on oiled banana leaf in the steamer, dot each with a little coconut cream for the fragrance, and steam 10–15 minutes — the dough turns translucent when done.
  8. The finish. Brush the hot dumplings with garlic oil so the skins stay soft, scatter over fried garlic, and serve on lettuce with coriander and sliced bird's-eye chillies. Eat them the day they are made; this is not a keeper.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The meat. Sriwangying steams a whole chicken breast for 15–20 minutes, minces it and saves the cooking water to re-moisten the filling. Naturalpalm and Aroifin's savoury variant fry raw minced pork straight in the pan. The steamed chicken is the palace way — gentler, paler; the fried pork is the market way — deeper, drier.

The flours. Sriwangying's court dough uses three flours and no glutinous rice flour at all. Naturalpalm and Aroifin use four, the glutinous flour giving the skin its slight stretch. A Pantip home cook's dough (1 cup rice flour, a spoon each of glutinous and tapioca, half-spoons of arrowroot, plus oil) hardens when the dumplings cool — the thread's whole complaint — and the answers point at more glutinous flour and more oil as the fix. The court recipes accept the trade-off: chor muang is eaten hot, minutes off the steam.

The sugar. Six tablespoons in Sriwangying's chicken filling; two or three of palm sugar in the pork fillings. Both sit on the same tightrope — sweet enough to taste the sugar through the meat, not so sweet the filling goes jammy and weeps through the dough.

Sweet or savoury. Aroifin leads with a genuine sweet filling, the descendant of the original court sweet, and files pork-and-radish under "savoury variant". Every other source cooks the sweet-salty pork filling as the main event. The dish that survived is the savoury one; the sweet one lives on mostly in recipe archives.

Before the steam. Sriwangying sprays the shaped flowers with water and steams them 8–10 minutes. Naturalpalm and Aroifin dot the tops with coconut cream and steam 10–15 minutes on a lower flame. The spray keeps the skins supple; the cream adds aroma and a sheen — many home cooks do a little of both.

The finish. Sriwangying brushes the hot dumplings with plain oil, Naturalpalm with the oil that fried the garlic, Aroifin with garlic oil and a scatter of crisp garlic on top. All three serve the same plate: lettuce, coriander, bird's-eye chillies.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Food colouring. The purple is butterfly pea flowers plus lime — the lime is what turns the blue infusion purple. A drop of violet food dye makes a purple dumpling but not a chor muang; the flower is part of the dish's identity, and Sriwangying notes fresh flowers colour better than dried.
  • Wheat wrappers. The skin is gluten-free by design — rice flour, tapioca, arrowroot, a touch of glutinous rice flour, cooked on the stove before shaping. Wonton wrappers give you a purple wonton, and the petals will never crimp the same way.
  • A wet filling. The filling must be fried until the pan is dry; a moist filling steams its way through the dough and the flowers collapse. Sriwangying even keeps the chicken's cooking water back, adding it only if the filling has gone too dry.
  • Skipping the crimp. A smooth purple dumpling is not the dish. The tweezer work — petals in offset rings, bottom to top — is the entire point of ช่อม่วง, the purple bouquet. If you skip it you have made the filling in a wrapper, and left the dish in the palace.
  • Skipping the garlic finish. The garlic-oil brush and the fried garlic on top are not optional garnish; the oil is what keeps the rice-flour skin soft once it leaves the steamer, and the garlic carries the savoury note over the sweet filling.
  • A dipping sauce. Chor muang is served on lettuce with coriander and chillies — nothing to dip. The filling is already balanced sweet-salty; a sauce would shout over the court dish's quiet harmony.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Dried butterfly pea flowers (ดอกอัญชัน): sold as "blue tea" in Thai grocers, health-food shops and online — a tablespoon of flowers to ¾ cup of hot water, steeped, is all you need.
  • Arrowroot flour (แป้งท้าวยายม่อม): health-food aisles and larger supermarkets; rice flour, glutinous rice flour and tapioca starch are staples of any Thai or East Asian grocer.
  • Sweet pickled radish (ไชโป๊วหวาน): tinned or packed, from Thai and Chinese grocers — it is the filling's quiet sweet-sour engine, so don't substitute fresh radish.
  • Chor muang tweezers (แหนบจับจีบ): order online from Thai kitchenware sellers; until they arrive, fine-nosed tweezers or fork tines pinched across the dough make a serviceable flower.
  • Banana leaf: frozen at Thai grocers; a square of oiled baking paper in the steamer works in a pinch.
  • Sriwangying's court garnish of bilimbi (ตะลิงปลิง) is essentially unfindable fresh in the UK — the lettuce, coriander, chilli and fried-garlic plate is the standard here.
