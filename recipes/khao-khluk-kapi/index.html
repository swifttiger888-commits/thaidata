Khao Khluk Kapi (ข้าวคลุกกะปิ)

Rice fried with shrimp paste until it smells like the sea, ringed by sweet pork, fried shrimp, sausage, egg, mango and lime — the street plate that eats like a feast.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 2 · ~40 minutes

One Rice, Ten Sides

ข้าวคลุกกะปิ means "rice mixed with shrimp paste" — and the mixing is the whole technique. The Wongnai and Kapook recipes are near-identical, both descended from the Pantip kitchen that published the classic: กะปิ, fermented shrimp paste, fried in hot oil with a splash of water until it smells, then the cooked rice tossed through on high heat until every grain turns the colour of the paste. That's the dish's centre; everything else is the ring around it.

The ring is the point. Fried dried shrimp, fried kun chiang sausage, a shredded omelette, sliced shallots, long beans, fresh chillies, shredded green mango, cucumber and lime — each side a different texture against the salty rice. And the centrepiece side is หมูหวาน, sweet pork: belly simmered in oyster sauce and fish sauce until tender, with the palm sugar added only at the end — the Kapook source is explicit that sugar early in the simmer would toughen the pork. Added last, it melts into a glossy lacquer.

Pim's modern version swaps the fried egg sheet for ไข่ข้น, soft scrambled eggs — same plate, creamier egg. The sources agree on everything that matters: the rice must taste of shrimp paste, and the plate must be full.

The Recipe (serves 2 — the Wongnai/ครัวตุ๊กตา version)

กะปิShrimp paste2 tbsp
หอมแดงซอยShallots, sliced½ cup (+100 g for pork)
กุ้งแห้งทอดFried dried shrimp½ cup
กุนเชียงทอดFried kun chiang sausage½ cup
พริกทอดFried chillies2
ไข่เจียวหั่นฝอยEgg, fried and shredded1
ถั่วฝักยาวซอยLong beans, chopped½ cup
พริกซอยFresh chillies, sliced½ cup
มะม่วงดิบGreen mango, shredded½ cup
แตงกวา / มะนาวCucumber / lime1 each

For the sweet pork (หมูหวาน): 500 g pork belly in 1 cm slices, 100 g sliced shallots, 2 tbsp oil, 3 tbsp oyster sauce, 3 tbsp fish sauce, ~1 tbsp dark soy, 1 cup water, 75 g palm sugar.

The Method

  1. Sweet pork first. Fry the shallots in oil until fragrant, add the pork belly, season with oyster sauce, fish sauce and dark soy, cover with water and simmer about 10 minutes until tender. Then the palm sugar, reduced until thick and glossy.
  2. Prep the ring. Fry the egg into a thin sheet and shred it; fry the dried shrimp, kun chiang and chillies crisp; shred the green mango, slice the cucumber and long beans.
  3. Fry the rice. Oil hot, shrimp paste in, fry until it smells, a splash of water to loosen, then the rice — high heat, toss until every grain is coated and the paste has married in.
  4. Plate the feast. Rice in the middle, sweet pork and every side arranged around it, lime to squeeze over. Mix as you eat.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The egg. Kapook and Wongnai fry a thin egg sheet and shred it; Pim's version pours soft scrambled eggs (ไข่ข้น) over the rice instead. Both are egg — one crisp-edged, one custard-soft.

How many sides. Kapook's full set runs nine or ten sides; Aroifin's "เครื่องแน่น" version adds rolled egg and doubles down on extras; simpler stalls serve sweet pork, egg and cucumber only. The rice stays the same.

Green mango. The sources all include shredded green mango — the sour counterweight. Where green mango is hard to find, home cooks substitute a squeeze of extra lime.

Sugar timing in the pork. Kapook states the rule outright: palm sugar only after the pork is tender. Earlier sugar caramelises onto still-tough meat. Wongnai follows the same order.

Shrimp paste in the rice only. Every source fries the paste into the rice, never into the sides — the sides stay clean against the pungent rice.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Calling it fried rice. The rice is mixed (คลุก) with fried shrimp paste, not stir-fried with egg and veg. Different dish, different texture.
  • Raw shrimp paste. The paste must be fried in oil first — raw paste in the rice tastes of the jar.
  • One-bowl everything. The sides sit around the rice, not in it. The plate is the format.
  • Ripe mango. The mango is green and shredded — sour, not dessert-sweet.
  • Skimping the sides. Sweet pork alone is a rice bowl; the fried shrimp, sausage, egg and mango are what make it khao khluk kapi.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Shrimp paste (กะปิ): Thai grocers — "kapi" in tubs. It's pungent; fry it well.
  • Kun chiang (กุนเชียง): sweet Chinese pork sausage, vacuum-packed at Thai/Chinese grocers.
  • Green mango: hard unripe mango at Asian grocers — the supermarket ripe ones won't shred.
  • Dried shrimp: standard in Thai grocers; fry crisp.
  • Palm sugar: Thai grocers; brown sugar works but the caramel note changes.
