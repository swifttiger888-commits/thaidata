Khai Look Keuy (ไข่ลูกเขย)

Boiled eggs deep-fried to a golden wrinkle, halved and drowned in sweet-sour tamarind sauce under a drift of fried shallots — the dish with the warning in its name.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 4 · ~30 minutes

The Dish That Warns the Son-in-Law

ไข่ลูกเขย — "son-in-law eggs" — carries the old story in its name: a mother-in-law deep-fries the eggs as a warning to a son-in-law who has mistreated her daughter. Fried, not boiled — the difference is the dish. The frying turns the whites golden and wrinkled, a skin that grabs the sauce, and the deep-fried eggs become something no plain boiled egg can be.

The sauce is three ingredients, and the Wongnai recipe keeps it exactly that: tamarind water, palm sugar, fish sauce — simmered until thick. The TrueID home version takes a different road entirely, sweetening with oyster sauce, white soy and sugar instead of palm sugar and tamarind. Same name, two sauces; the tamarind one is the classic.

The finish is fried shallots — the Wongnai home recipe fries the shallots in the same oil, before the eggs, so the oil carries the onion — plus coriander and fried dried chillies. Served over or beside rice, the dish is a side that often eats like a main.

Chicken eggs work too — the TrueID source uses either, and the Wongnai home recipe boils chicken eggs for 7 minutes instead of duck eggs' 8.

The Method

  1. Boil and shock. Duck eggs 8 minutes at a rolling boil, straight into cold water, peel carefully — the whites must stay whole.
  2. Fry to a wrinkle. Deep-fry the peeled eggs in hot oil until golden and lightly wrinkled all over. Drain.
  3. The sauce. Tamarind water, palm sugar and fish sauce together over heat, stirring until the sugar dissolves, then simmer until it thickens enough to cling.
  4. Plate. Halve the eggs, pour the sauce over, finish with fried shallots, coriander and fried dried chillies. Serve with rice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The sauce. The classic is tamarind, palm sugar and fish sauce (Wongnai). TrueID's ครัวคุณพลอย version sweetens with oyster sauce, white soy and sugar — no tamarind at all. The tamarind version is the one the name belongs to.

Duck or chicken. Wongnai's recipe is duck eggs; the home recipes use chicken eggs; TrueID says either. Duck yolks are richer and the eggs fry with a deeper gold.

Boil time. Eight minutes for duck eggs, seven for chicken — the sources split by egg, not by taste.

How sour. The Wongnai home recipe runs 11 tablespoons of tamarind water to five eggs — properly sour. The classic version's quarter-cup is the balanced middle.

Shallots in the oil. The home recipe fries the shallots first and reuses the oil for the eggs; the classic version adds shallots only as a topping. The onion-scented oil is the home-kitchen refinement.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Skipping the fry. Boiled eggs in tamarind sauce are a different, sadder dish. The fried wrinkle is the point.
  • Ketchup sauce. The classic sauce is tamarind — sour, not tomato-sweet. Ketchup makes devilled-egg sauce.
  • Whole eggs. The eggs are halved after frying so the sauce meets the yolk.
  • No fried shallots. The shallots are not garnish — they're half the dish's texture.
  • Calling it "Thai Scotch eggs". No breadcrumbs, no sausage — the fried egg is bare, and the sauce is tamarind.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Duck eggs: farm shops and larger supermarkets; chicken eggs substitute fine.
  • Tamarind: blocks or paste at Thai grocers — soak the block in warm water and strain.
  • Palm sugar: Thai grocers; soft brown sugar is the nearest swap.
  • Fry the shallots first, then the eggs in the same oil — the onion carries.
  • Serve with jasmine rice; the sauce is meant to run into it.
