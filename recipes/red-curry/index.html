Red Curry (แกงเผ็ด)

Dried chillies, not powder. Cracked coconut cream, not jar paste. Pork and aubergines, finished with basil and torn kaffir lime — the curry the UK knows as "red" and Thailand simply calls "spicy".

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 3–4 · ~45 minutes

The Curry Named for Being Spicy

แกงเผ็ด means "spicy curry" — and it earns the name. The redness isn't tomato or powder: it comes from a paste of soaked dried chillies pounded from scratch. In Thailand this is the everyday curry, the one home cooks make on a Tuesday with pork or chicken, and the one that turns into kaeng phet pet yang — roast-duck red curry — when the occasion matters.

The heart of the dish is the technique. Coconut cream is simmered in the pan until it cracks — the oil splits out and the surface turns glossy red — and only then does the paste go in. Skip that step and the curry never develops its depth, no matter how good the paste is.

And the dish has an older name. Thai food historians record the original as kaeng daeng — "red curry" — built on duck, pineapple and pineapple juice, with a paste enriched with mace, nutmeg and cumin. The modern version dropped the fruit and kept the heat. Both are documented below.

The Original: Kaeng Daeng, Duck and Pineapple (แกงแดง — recorded by อาจารย์ศรีสมร คงพันธุ์)

The older, richer character. To the same paste add 1 tsp mace (เปราะ), ¼ nutmeg (ลูกจันทน์) and 2 tsp cumin (ยี่หร่า), all dry-roasted before pounding. The curry itself takes 400 g roast duck, 100 g pineapple, 10 cherry tomatoes, 50 g pea aubergines and ½ cup pineapple juice — the fruit's sharp-sweet edge is what the modern version lost.

The recorded method cracks the head cream the same way: 1 cup head coconut cream cracked with 3 tbsp rice bran oil, the paste fried in it, the duck warmed through, then 3½ cups of thin coconut milk with the pineapple and juice simmered to order. Thai basil and kaffir lime leaves finish it, exactly as today.

If you can only try one restaurant version in Thailand, the roast-duck one is the one — it is the same dish in its Sunday clothes.

The Method

  1. Make the paste. Soak the dried chillies about 10 minutes until pliable. Pound the coriander seeds and white peppercorns to powder first, then the coriander roots, lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime zest, shallots, garlic, shrimp paste, salt and the drained chillies — pound to a smooth paste. Keeps a week in the fridge.
  2. Crack the cream. Heat the thick head of coconut milk over medium heat and let it simmer until the oil visibly splits out — the surface turns glossy with red-orange oil. This is แตกมัน, and it is the depth of the dish.
  3. Fry the paste. Into the cracked cream, stirring, until it darkens and smells cooked. (The lighter route — a spoon of oil, the paste fried first, then the coconut milk — also appears in the sources; the crack is the classic.)
  4. Pork in. Stir-fry in the paste until sealed on the outside.
  5. Thin milk in. The rest of the coconut milk plus about 100 ml of water. Gentle boil, then simmer until the pork is tender — roughly 15 minutes.
  6. Aubergines. Apple aubergines first, pea aubergines a few minutes later. Cook just until tender; they should hold their shape.
  7. Season. Fish sauce (or salt) and palm sugar. Taste for the order: chilli heat leads, salt behind it, sweetness only rounding the edges.
  8. Finish. Off the heat, stir in the Thai basil, the sliced spur chillies and the torn kaffir lime leaves. Serve hot with jasmine rice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The paste's richness. The everyday market paste is chilli, coriander seed, lemongrass, galangal, shallot, garlic and shrimp paste. The recorded kaeng daeng paste adds mace, nutmeg and cumin. Neither is wrong — one is Tuesday, the other is festival day.

Crack vs fry. Maggi's Thai recipe cracks the cream first; the Wongnai home cook fries the paste in a spoon of oil first. Both work; the crack builds more depth.

Salt vs fish sauce. Home cooks genuinely split. Fish sauce adds savoury depth; salt keeps the chilli colour brighter. The recipe above gives both, honestly.

Sugar. From none in the strictest versions to three teaspoons in the sweetest. Palm sugar is the Thai choice — it rounds without a syrupy edge.

The pineapple question. The recorded kaeng daeng had pineapple and pineapple juice; modern kaeng phet has neither. Which one is "the real recipe" depends on which century you ask.

The protein. Pork and chicken are the everyday choices; roast duck is the classic; bamboo shoots and turkey berries swap in by region and by season.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Sweet coconut gloop. If the first thing you taste is sugar, it isn't แกงเผ็ด. Heat leads; sweet only rounds.
  • Jar paste, no crack. The crack-the-cream step is where the depth comes from. Skipping it leaves the curry flat no matter what paste you bought.
  • Curry powder. That is kaeng kari, a different dish. Red curry gets its colour from dried chillies, not powder.
  • Broccoli and carrot medleys. The vegetable of the dish is the aubergine — apple and pea. A bag of stir-fry mix replaces it with crunch and water.
  • No kaffir lime, no basil. The finish is half the aroma. Torn kaffir lime leaves and Thai basil at the end, or it smells like nothing.
  • Cream only. Simmering everything in thick cream makes it heavy and muddled. The thin-milk phase matters.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Dried chillies: the long mild red ones from Thai grocers, plus a few small hot ones for balance. Soak them properly or they won't pound smooth.
  • Kaffir lime leaves and zest: fresh or frozen in Thai grocers; dried leaves work in a pinch. There is no real substitute for the leaf's scent.
  • Thai aubergines: apple aubergines and pea aubergines from Thai grocers. If you can't find them, make the duck version instead of substituting courgette — the sources never do.
  • Shrimp paste (กะปิ): any Thai grocer. It's a teaspoon, but it's the backbone. Leaving it out makes a different curry, not a vegetarian one.
  • Coconut milk: full-fat tins only — "light" coconut milk won't crack. The cream has to be thick enough to split.
  • Roast duck for the kaeng daeng: any Chinese takeaway's roast duck, chopped, bones and all — it simmers into the curry.
  • Palm sugar: Thai grocers or the world-food aisle. Soft brown sugar works, but the flavour is closer with the real thing.
