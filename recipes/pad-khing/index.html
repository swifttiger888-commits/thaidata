Pad Khing (ผัดขิง)

Chicken through a hot wok with pounded garlic and a half-cup of ginger — the fragrant, warming stir-fry every rice shop knows.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 3 · ~20 minutes

The Stir-Fry That Leads With Ginger

ผัดขิง is the dish where ginger is the main ingredient, not a seasoning. The Wongnai recipe runs a full half-cup of sliced ginger to 500 grams of chicken — pounded garlic and ginger go into the hot oil first, fried until the wok smells of both, and only then does the chicken join. That order is the whole technique: the ginger infuses the oil before anything else lands.

The Kapook version frames the dish as the health order — "a menu with Thai herbs and protein, plus vegetables — hot and peppery, but genuinely good for you" — and allows either young or mature ginger, the young kind softer and milder. The supporting cast is fixed across both sources: black wood-ear mushrooms for their crunch, sliced onion, and a finish of spring onion and red spur chilli.

Where they part is the sauce. Wongnai glosses the chicken with tao jiao, the fermented soybean paste, white soy and a spoon of sugar; Kapook dresses it with oyster sauce and fish sauce instead. Same dish, two shops — the tao jiao version is the deeper, funkier one.

The Kapook version swaps the tao jiao for oyster sauce and fish sauce, and allows chicken breast in place of thigh. Young ginger if you want it milder. Phol Food Mafia's scale: 400 g thigh, 160 g ginger, marinated 10 minutes — the ginger-to-meat ratio is the point.

The Method

  1. Prep. Pound the garlic; slice the ginger into thin strips.
  2. Ginger into the oil first. Garlic and ginger fried until the wok smells of both, then the chicken until cooked.
  3. The crunch. Onion and wood-ear mushrooms in, stirred through.
  4. Gloss and finish. Tao jiao, white soy and sugar, stirred to coat; spring onion and red chilli, one toss, off the heat. Hot rice on the side.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The sauce. Wongnai's tao jiao, white soy and sugar versus Kapook's oyster sauce and fish sauce. Phol Food Mafia adds a third: tao jiao plus white soy and seasoning sauce. Pim blends black tao jiao with oyster sauce. The tao jiao versions are funkier and darker; the oyster-sauce ones rounder.

To marinate or not. Phol marinates the chicken in seasoning sauce and white soy for 10 minutes before the wok; Wongnai and Pim go straight in. The marinade deepens the seasoning on the meat.

Young or mature ginger. Kapook allows both — young ginger is softer and less fiery; Pim and Phol specify mature ginger, sliced generously (150–160 g, the dish's real main ingredient). The shops slice mature.

Breast or thigh. Kapook and Pim permit chicken breast; Wongnai and Phol specify thigh. Pim swaps the protein outright on repeat days — pork liver is her variant. Thigh stays juiciest in a fast wok.

Wood-ear or not. All five recipes include the black wood-ear — it's the dish's crunch. The sources agree on everything except how much.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Ginger as a garnish. The ginger is the dish — fried in first, eaten by the spoonful.
  • Ground ginger. Powdered ginger is a spice; the dish needs sliced fresh root.
  • Chicken in first. The garlic and ginger must fry in the oil before the meat, or the ginger never blooms.
  • Calling it a "ginger chicken curry". There's no sauce, no coconut, no curry paste — it's a dry, glossy stir-fry.
  • Overcooking the wood-ear. It goes in late and stays crisp; stewed wood-ear turns rubbery.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Ginger: standard supermarkets — a fat root, sliced thin against the grain.
  • Black wood-ear (เห็ดหูหนู): dried at Chinese grocers — soak, trim the woody base.
  • Tao jiao (เต้าเจี้ยว): salted soybean paste at Thai grocers.
  • Red spur chillies: the long mild reds at Asian grocers.
  • Serve with jasmine rice — the rice-shop pairing.
