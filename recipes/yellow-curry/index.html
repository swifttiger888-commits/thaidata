Yellow Curry (แกงกะหรี่)

The borrowed word, made Thai. Here the flavour doesn't come from a pounded chilli paste — it comes from curry powder itself. Chicken, potato, bay leaf, coconut milk, and ajad on the side.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 3–4 · ~40 minutes

The Curry That Runs on Curry Powder

The name gives it away: แกงกะหรี่ is the Thai pronunciation of the borrowed word kari, and the dish travelled into the Thai kitchen through the country's Muslim communities — the same lineage that brought satay and massaman. But where แกงเผ็ด is built on pounded dried chillies, this one is built on curry powder. Turmeric, coriander, cumin, warm spice — the yellow paste gets its character from the powder, and Thai cooks say so out loud.

That's why it sits oddly on western Thai menus: it's the mildest of the Thai curries, the one served with ajad — the same cucumber pickle that cuts through satay — and the one with potato and bay leaf in it. It is closer, in spirit, to the kari of the Malay peninsula than to the chilli-led curries around it in the cookbook.

The Japanese curry that shares its name is its descendant, thickened with roux and poured over tonkatsu. The Thai original stays loose, coconut-milky and golden — a curry you can eat daily without a fight.

The Method

  1. Paste, if from scratch. The dried chillies, shallots, lemongrass, garlic, galangal, kaffir lime zest, coriander seed, pepper, cumin, curry powder and shrimp paste pounded smooth.
  2. Fry the paste. In the rice bran oil over medium heat until it smells cooked through; head coconut milk in, stirred through.
  3. Chicken. Fried until sealed, then the bay leaves in.
  4. Season and thin. Fish sauce and palm sugar, then the thin coconut milk.
  5. Potatoes and shallots. At the boil, add them; simmer about 10 minutes until the potatoes are tender.
  6. Taste. Golden, lightly sweet, curry-powder warm. Adjust the fish sauce and sugar against each other.
  7. Serve. Hot over rice, coriander and red spur chilli on top, ajad on the side — the pickle is the dish's counterpoint, not an afterthought.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Potato or sweet potato. Phol's recipe uses potato; the Kapook home version uses sweet potato and swaps the shallots for onion. Both are published; the curry takes either.

The bay leaf. Phol adds two bay leaves and they're in the method above; plenty of home recipes omit them. The bay leaf is the subtle bridge to the dish's Indian ancestry.

Where the curry powder lives. The Kapook paste pounds curry powder straight into the paste; other cooks fry the paste and add powder later. Either way, the powder is the flavour — this is not optional.

Coconut milk or evaporated milk. The classic uses coconut milk; some home versions use evaporated milk for a lighter curry. The coconut version is the standard.

Ajad or not. Phol serves it with ajad and calls it part of the dish; home cooks sometimes skip it. The pickle's sharpness is what the mild, sweet curry needs.

The lineage. Thai food writers agree the dish came through the Muslim communities; whether it arrived with traders from India or via the Malay peninsula is the argument. The dish itself doesn't care.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Confusing it with Japanese curry. The roux-thickened katsu curry is the Japanese descendant. Thai kari stays loose and coconut-milky — no roux, no tonkatsu.
  • Powder only, no paste. The dish starts with a fried curry paste; the powder works inside it. A spoon of powder stirred into coconut milk is not this curry.
  • No ajad. The pickle's sharp-sweet crunch is the counterpoint the mild curry needs. Served alone, the dish is only half balanced.
  • Heat expectations. Western menus list "yellow curry" as hot. It's the mildest of the Thai curries — the warmth comes from spice, not chillies.
  • Over-reduced and thick. The Thai curry stays soupy enough to spoon over rice. A wall-paste consistency belongs to a different country's kitchen.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Yellow curry paste: Thai grocers sell it labelled พริกแกงกะหรี่ (sometimes "kari paste"). It is different from red or green — check the label, not the colour alone.
  • Curry powder: the mild Madras-style blend from the supermarket works for the from-scratch paste; it's the turmeric and cumin doing the work.
  • Bay leaves: dried is fine. Two, no more.
  • Palm sugar: Thai grocers or the world-food aisle; soft brown sugar works in a pinch.
  • Ajad: make the one from the chicken satay recipe — vinegar, sugar, salt, cucumber, chilli and shallots. Same pickle, same job.
  • Coconut milk: full-fat tins; the head cream for frying, the thin milk for simmering.
