Yam Khanom Jeen (ยำขนมจีน)

Fresh rice vermicelli tossed in lime, chilli and the deep funk of pla ra, with fried mackerel and a handful of herbs — the street-stall salad that dresses a noodle like it's dressing a som tam.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 2 · ~25 minutes

The Noodle That Became A Salad

ขนมจีน (khanom jeen) is Thailand's fresh, fermented rice noodle — the same noodle that carries the coconut curries of khanom jeen nam ya. In a ยำ (yam), the kitchen's usual logic inverts: instead of a curry spooned over the noodles, the noodles are the body of the salad itself, dressed like a som tam and piled with whatever protein the stall has fried that morning.

It is street food at heart. Aroifin's writers frame the dish as both home cooking and a stall earner (ทำขาย), and their advice for the dressing is the rule every version follows: the popular yam khanom jeen balances เผ็ด นัว เปรี้ยว หวานนิดหน่อย — hot, funky, sour, and only a hint of sweet. The funk is น้ำปลาร้า (pla ra), fermented anchovy brine, the Isan pantry's signature. Without it the dish is simply dressed noodles; with it, the lime and chilli suddenly have something to push against.

The noodles stay cold or room-temperature — the vendors blanch them fresh each day and the yam is tossed to order. Chef Thanakorn Ployseechomphu's version for Phol Food Mafia, the one used here, is the classic stall build: deep-fried short mackerel, dried squid and dried shrimp for crunch, a handful of herbs, and peanuts and fried shallots folded in at the end.

The fresh-seafood pot (TrueID): 1 cup noodles, 1 pla tu, blanched prawns and squid, moo yor (steamed pork loaf) — dressing of 3 tbsp lime juice, 1 tbsp ready-made pla ra, ½ tbsp sugar, ½ tbsp fish sauce, 1 tbsp chilli flakes, with the pla ra stirred in last. The pla-ra-first pot (Aroifin/Wongnai): 2 bundles noodles, 2 fried pla tu, 1 cup sliced long beans, ½ cup spring onion, ½ cup kratin seeds (เมล็ดกระถิน), 3 tbsp sliced bird's eye chillies — ½ cup boiled pla ra with the palm sugar dissolved into it first. The seafood feast (Kapook): 1 large squid, 500 g prawns, 1 kg mussels, 500 g noodles, plus sliced kaffir lime leaves in the toss. The no-pla-ra pot (Kapook): mussels and crab stick dressed only in fish sauce, lime juice, fresh chillies and palm sugar, with tomato and shallots.

The Method

  1. The fry. Deep-fry the mackerel, dried squid and dried shrimp until golden and crisp, then drain and set aside. This is the only heat in the dish — everything else happens in the bowl.
  2. The dressing. Combine the pla ra, fish sauce, lime juice, sugar, toasted rice powder and chilli flakes in a mixing bowl and stir until the sugar dissolves. Taste here, before the noodles go in: spicy-sour-salty with the funk of pla ra running through it, and only a whisper of sweet. Aroifin's rule for the balance is เผ็ด นัว เปรี้ยว หวานนิดหน่อย.
  3. The herbs. Add the sliced shallots, chillies, long beans, spring onion, sawtooth coriander and mint, and toss them through the dressing so they start to soften.
  4. The fish. Pull the flesh off the mackerel in chunks — bones out — and add it with the crisp squid and dried shrimp, tossing everything together. Fold in the roasted peanuts and fried shallots last so they keep their crunch.
  5. The serve. Pile onto a plate, crown with the fried dried chillies and a scatter of mint, and put sweet basil on the side. Room temperature, no rice required — this is the whole meal, or a snack between som tams.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The protein. Fried pla tu is the standard (Phol Food Mafia, Aroifin, Wongnai, TrueID, Kapook), but TrueID adds blanched prawns, squid and moo yor; Kapook's roundup collects tuna-fritter versions, a mussel-and-crab-stick version, and a mixed pot with fish balls and sausages. Aroifin settles it: the toppings are ตามใจชอบ — whatever the kitchen has. Only the dressing is fixed.

Pla ra or not. Most sources build the dressing on pla ra, and Kapook's own headline splits its six recipes into ใส่ปลาร้า and ไม่ใส่ปลาร้า — the no-pla-ra versions dress with fish sauce and lime alone and read much closer to a plain noodle yam. Kapook's first recipe also marks the pla ra optional ("ใส่หรือไม่ใส่ก็ได้"), the one concession every home cook understands.

When the pla ra enters. Aroifin and Wongnai start with boiled pla ra and dissolve the palm sugar into it — the dressing is built around the funk. TrueID stirs the pla ra in last, after the noodles are already dressed, so it sits on the surface. Phol Food Mafia mixes it with the other liquids at the start. Same ingredient, three different depths.

The sugar. White sugar at Phol Food Mafia, TrueID and Kapook's seafood recipe; palm sugar at Aroifin, Wongnai and Kapook's first recipe. TrueID splits the difference ("ใช้น้ำตาลปี๊บได้"). The point in every version is the same: barely any of it.

Kratin seeds. เมล็ดกระถิน — the tiny, nutty seeds of the lead tree — are a signature garnish in Aroifin and Wongnai's version, optional in Kapook's, and absent from the others. They add a pop that no other ingredient quite replaces.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • The wrong noodle. Khanom jeen is a fresh, soft, thick-ish fermented rice noodle, not thin rice vermicelli. Swap in rice sticks and the salad becomes a different, brittle dish — the noodle is supposed to drink the dressing, not fight it.
  • Skipping the pla ra. Replacing fermented fish with extra fish sauce is the single most common western shortcut, and it deletes the dish. Pla ra is not a stronger fish sauce — it is the นัว, the deep fermented funk the lime and chilli are built against. Thai sources do make no-pla-ra versions, but they call them out as such; the default has pla ra in it.
  • Dressing it into soup. The noodles should glisten, not swim. This is a salad; too much dressing collapses the herbs and turns the plate into a pool. Aroifin's balance — hot, funky, sour, a hint of sweet — holds precisely because the dressing is a coating, not a broth.
  • Oil-and-vinegar instincts. A western "noodle salad" reflex reaches for oil and vinegar. The Thai dressing is lime juice, fish sauce, chilli and a little sugar — no oil at all — with the crunch coming from fried fish, peanuts and fried shallots instead of raw vegetables in vinaigrette.
  • Serving it hot. Yam khanom jeen is a room-temperature or cool dish. Western versions that stir-fry the noodles first end up with a fried noodle dish that happens to have lime on it.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Khanom jeen: vacuum-packed fresh noodles at Thai grocers (some Chinese grocers stock them too). If you must substitute, use the thickest dried rice vermicelli — but know the texture is not the same.
  • Pla ra: bottled ready-made pla ra (ปลาร้าสำเร็จรูป) at Thai grocers and online Thai supermarkets; TrueID's recipe is built on exactly this. Boil it briefly first if the bottle says so — Aroifin and Wongnai specify boiled (ต้มสุก).
  • Short mackerel (pla tu) is hard to find whole in the UK — use supermarket mackerel fillets, grilled or shallow-fried until crisp, then flaked. Smoked mackerel works in a pinch but its smoke fights the lime; TrueID and Kapook's prawn-and-mussel versions sidestep the problem entirely with easy UK seafood.
  • Kratin seeds (เมล็ดกระถิน) are a Thai-market rarity — skip them, as Kapook's recipes do.
  • Sawtooth coriander (ผักชีใบเลื่อย) at Thai grocers; coriander leaves are the accepted stand-in. Toasted rice powder: dry-toast raw glutinous rice in a pan until golden and pound or blitz it — ten minutes, and no UK shop sells it.
