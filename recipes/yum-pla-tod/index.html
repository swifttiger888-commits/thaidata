Yum Pla Tod (ยำปลาทอด)

A crisp whole fried fish, broken over a sharp, bright salad of shallots, chillies, herbs and sour mango — hot crunch against cold, sour dressing.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 2 October 2026 · 2–3 · Researched from four Thai-language sources (Cookpad, Kapook, TrueID, Wongnai)

The restaurant dish that turns one fish into dinner

Yum pla tod is the answer to a problem every Thai restaurant kitchen knows: fried fish is glorious for ten minutes, then the skin softens and nobody orders it. Fix it by tearing the fish apart while it is still crackling and tossing it through a sharp yum dressing — chillies, shallots, fish sauce, lime and a hit of sour mango. The heat of the fish wilts the herbs just slightly, and every bite carries hot crunch against cold, sour dressing.

On UK Thai menus it usually appears as "sea bass yum" or "pla kapong tod yum" — Thai Aroy Dee in Leeds lists it as Sea Bass Yum. Thai cooks argue about the details: whole fish or steaks, mango or lemongrass, salad on top or on the side. The page below covers the version you will actually be served, and where the sources split.

The Recipe For 2–3 servings; about 40 minutes. The fish is fried whole — a 600–800 g sea bass is ideal. Sour (green) mango is the classic garnish; Granny Smith apple is the common UK substitute.

Any firm white fish works: sea bass is the restaurant standard, but the sources also use sea bream, tilapia and small gourami. Skip the mango and build the salad around lemongrass, ginger and cashews (the Cookpad version), or serve the salad on the side with a fish-sauce caramel drizzled over the fish (the Wongnai version).

The Method

  1. Prep the fish. Score the sea bass 2–3 times on each side, rub with a little salt, and leave it to air-dry for 15 minutes — then pat completely dry. Dry skin is what makes the crackling.
  2. Dust and fry. Dust the fish lightly with crisp-fry flour or cornflour and shake off the excess. Deep-fry at 180 °C until deep golden, 7–10 minutes depending on size. The Cookpad source is emphatic: do not touch or turn the fish for the first two minutes or the skin will stick and tear.
  3. Drain it hot. Lift the fish out and rest it upright on a rack so the oil runs out of the cavity — a belly full of oil ruins the salad. Keep it hot while you build the dressing.
  4. Slice everything. Shallots paper-thin, lemongrass heart shaved fine, chillies sliced, mango julienned. Everything must be ready before the fish cools.
  5. Make the dressing. Dissolve palm sugar in the lime juice and fish sauce and taste: it should hit sour first, then salty, then sweet. TrueID's ratio is 4 tbsp lime, 2½ fish sauce, 1⅔ palm sugar; Cookpad goes 3 lime, 2 fish sauce, ⅔ sugar. Adjust to your fruit's sourness.
  6. Dress and serve. Toss the mango, shallots, herbs and nuts through the dressing, pile the salad over the hot fish, and bring it to the table whole — break it with a spoon and fork so the crackling shatters into the dressing.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Whole fish or steaks? Cookpad fries a whole sea bass; Wongnai's restaurant-style recipe uses a thick steak dusted in flour. Whole fish gives you more crackling, steaks cook faster and suit sharing plates.

Mango: essential or optional? Three of the four sources build the salad around sour green mango (Kapook, TrueID, Wongnai). Cookpad skips it entirely and uses a lemongrass–ginger salad with cashews. The mango version is the one UK restaurants mean by "sea bass yum".

Salad on the fish, or beside it? Cookpad and TrueID toss the salad directly over the hot fish. Kapook and Wongnai plate the fried fish with the mango salad alongside — and Wongnai adds a third element: a quick fish-sauce caramel (nam pla lad) fried in the wok and poured over the fish.

Dressing balance. The sources disagree on how sweet the yum should be. TrueID uses almost twice as much sugar relative to fish sauce; Kapook's is sweetest of all (2 tbsp palm sugar to 1½ fish sauce). Northern-style yum leans sour; central-Thai restaurant versions lean sweet.

Peanuts or cashews? TrueID and Wongnai use roasted peanuts (and Wongnai adds fried dried shrimp); Cookpad and Kapook use salted cashews. Both are garnish-crunch, not flavour load-bearers.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Frying wet fish — the skin sticks and tears. Air-dry and pat completely dry before frying.
  • Moving the fish too early in the oil — the first two minutes set the skin; leave it alone.
  • Making the salad too far ahead — dressed shallots and mango go limp in minutes. Dress at the last moment.
  • Drowning the fish — the dressing is sharp and small in volume; too much and the crackling turns to mush.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Sea bass is a UK supermarket staple — a whole 600–800 g fish from the fish counter is perfect, or use two sea bream.
  • Sour green mango comes from Indian and Asian grocers. Out of season, Granny Smith apple cut in matchsticks is the standard substitute.
  • Bird's-eye chillies, lemongrass and kaffir lime leaves are in the world-food aisle of most large supermarkets.
  • Any crisp-fry flour works for the dusting; plain cornflour is the classic Thai choice.
