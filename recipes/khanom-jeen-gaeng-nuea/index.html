Recipe · Thailand · The Noodle Curry You Almost Never See For Sale

Khanom Jeen Gaeng Nuea (ขนมจีนแกงเนื้อ)

Fermented rice vermicelli under a coconut beef curry — curry paste and dry-roasted spices fried until the oil splits, beef simmered soft, pea eggplants and basil dropped in at the last breath.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 3 · ~55 minutes

A Curry That Is Never On The Menu Board

ขนมจีนแกงเนื้อ reads as simply as it eats: ขนมจีน, the soft, faintly sour fermented rice noodles of central Thailand, and แกงเนื้อ, beef curry. The OpenRice cook who shared her recipe opens with the telling detail — she rarely finds the dish for sale, and when she wants it, she makes it herself. It is, like most khanom jeen curries, a home dish first and a stall dish second.

The curry underneath is แกงเนื้อ — beef curry in coconut milk, a central-Thai weekday pot. What lifts it onto khanom jeen is patience with the beef. Every Thai source here insists on the same two slow steps: the paste is fried in thick coconut milk until the oil splits (แตกมัน), and the beef is simmered in thin coconut milk until it softens — fifteen to twenty minutes at the short end, up to forty at the long end. "If you like it properly soft," the Pantip cook advises, "stew the beef with the thin coconut milk for 30–40 minutes first."

The third step is what makes this curry smell like an old kitchen rather than a jar: dry-roasted spices. Coriander seed, cumin and green cardamom are toasted in a dry pan and pounded into the red curry paste — a step Kapook calls "the heart of the rich, fragrant flavour." The finished bowl gets pea eggplants, Thai basil and a hard-boiled egg alongside, and the curry is ladled hot over room-temperature noodles so the sauce soaks into the coils.

The Cookpad pot (Beebirdja): 200 g beef, 50 g shop-bought red paste (Lobo), a cube of chicken blood, 10 pea eggplants, tomatoes, fish sauce, coconut sugar and seasoning powder — fried in rice-bran oil, finished with basil and chillies, five to ten minutes on the heat. The OpenRice pot: 4 ขีด beef, 1 cup pea eggplants, basil, kaffir lime leaves, thick and thin coconut milk, and green curry paste — beef fried in the split milk, seasoned with palm sugar, fish sauce and salt. The classic pot (Kapook's แกงเนื้อ): the same spice-roasting step, dried chillies, lemongrass and galangal in the paste, served with rice — the curry that taught this dish everything it knows.

The Method

  1. The spices. Toast the coriander seeds, cumin and cardamom in a dry pan over medium heat until they smell, then pound them into the red curry paste. Kapook calls this the heart of the dish — skip it and the curry tastes like a jar.
  2. The split. Heat the thick coconut milk until the oil breaks out (แตกมัน), add the paste and fry until it darkens and smells sweet. This is the same กะทิแตกมัน step as every Thai curry worth its name — do not rush it.
  3. The beef. Add the thin coconut milk and the beef, lower the heat and simmer 15–20 minutes. For properly soft, falling-apart beef, the Pantip cook simmers 30–40 minutes; the marbling is what keeps it tender.
  4. The seasoning. Season with fish sauce, palm sugar and oyster sauce, and taste. The oyster sauce is the Pantip cook's touch — it rounds the curry without making it sweet.
  5. The vegetables. Add the pea eggplants and simmer only until just cooked. Then the red chillies and Thai basil go in last — stir once and kill the heat immediately, so the basil stays green and aromatic.
  6. The noodles. Ladle the hot curry over room-temperature khanom jeen so the sauce soaks into the coils. Eat at once, with fresh vegetables and a boiled egg — the Pantip cook says that pairing is 200% better.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The paste. Kapook and the Pantip original pound dry-roasted coriander, cumin and cardamom into red curry paste — the step both call the heart of the dish. OpenRice reaches for green curry paste and skips the roasting entirely. Cookpad's Beebirdja uses shop-bought red paste (Lobo) straight from the packet. Three kitchens, three pastes — all still call it แกงเนื้อ.

How long the beef cooks. Kapook and Pantip simmer the beef 15–20 minutes in thin coconut milk, extending to 30–40 for soft meat. OpenRice fries the beef in the split milk and boils it only until cooked. Cookpad goes shortest of all — five to ten minutes on the heat. The dish will forgive all of them, but only the slow ones melt.

Blood. Cookpad adds a cube of chicken blood (เลือดไก่) to the curry — an old khanom jeen habit the others leave out. If you like it, it sits between the beef and the tomato in the pot.

Kaffir lime. OpenRice finishes with kaffir lime leaves and basil together; Kapook and Pantip finish with basil and red chillies alone. The kaffir version smells sharper on top of the curry; the plain version lets the roasted spices do the talking.

The seasoning set. Pantip and Kapook season with fish sauce, palm sugar and oyster sauce. OpenRice uses palm sugar, fish sauce and plain salt. Cookpad reaches for coconut sugar, seasoning powder and MSG. Every one of them holds the sugar back — the beef and coconut do the sweetening.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Wrong noodles. Khanom jeen are fermented rice noodles, soft and slightly sour, served at room temperature under the hot curry. Dry rice vermicelli (เส้นหมี่), wheat noodles or spaghetti are not the dish — the ferment is the point.
  • Jar paste, no spice step. The dry-roasted coriander, cumin and cardamom pounded into the paste is what Kapook calls the heart of the dish. A jar of "red curry paste" straight into coconut milk tastes like every other jar curry.
  • Skipping the split. The paste must fry in thick coconut milk until the oil breaks (แตกมัน) before any thin milk goes in. Pour everything in at once and the curry stays pale and flat.
  • Quick beef. Western stir-fry habits leave beef tough. Every Thai source here either simmers the beef gently or fries it first in the split milk — never just boiled in a rush.
  • Italian basil, or basil cooked to death. The finish is Thai basil (ใบโหระพา), added last with the heat killed so it stays green. Sweet Mediterranean basil is a different leaf, and basil stewed for ten minutes adds nothing but grey.
  • Soup treatment. This is a curry over noodles, not a noodle soup. The liquid should be thick enough to cling to the coils — the thin coconut milk is for simmering the beef, not for flooding the bowl.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Khanom jeen (เส้นขนมจีน): fresh packs at Thai grocers (often chilled next to the vegetables); some shops sell them frozen. In a pinch, rehydrated rice vermicelli is a cousin, not a substitute.
  • Beef: braising steak, chuck or brisket from any UK supermarket — anything with some marbling, per the Pantip cook's advice, since the fat keeps the meat soft through the simmer.
  • Pea eggplants (มะเขือเปราะ) and clustered pea aubergines (มะเขือพวง): fresh at Thai and Vietnamese grocers; pea aubergines also come frozen. If neither is available, the curry still works with green beans.
  • Red curry paste (พริกแกงเผ็ด): Mae Ploy or Namjai at Thai grocers and online; the spice-roasting step will lift even a jar.
  • Coriander seed, cumin, green cardamom: any UK supermarket spice aisle — the three dry-roasted spices are the dish's signature.
  • Thai basil: fresh at Thai and Vietnamese grocers; a large pot plant from a garden centre also works and keeps for months.
