Look Chup (ลูกชุบ)

Mung bean paste cooked with coconut milk, shaped into miniature fruit no bigger than a thumbnail, painted by hand and dipped in agar jelly until each piece wears a glassy shell — Portuguese marzipan remade in the Ayutthaya palace kitchen.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Makes about 50 pieces · ~2 hours plus drying time

The Marzipan That Became a Mung Bean

The name is the method: ลูก (look) is a small round piece, and ชุบ (chup) means to dip or coat — each shaped sweet is dipped, over and over, into clear agar jelly until it sets in a glassy shell. What is inside that shell is the dish's whole history.

Look chup descends from massapão, the almond marzipan Portuguese traders brought to Siam in the 1600s. In the court of King Narai, the royal kitchen under Maria Guyomar de Pinha — the Portuguese-Japanese cook credited with refining it — swapped almonds, which had to be imported, for the mung bean the country grew everywhere. Almonds became ถั่วเขียว (mung beans), marzipan became look chup, and a European confection turned into one of the most recognisably Thai sweets there is.

It is still a sweet of occasions. The painting is the labour: each miniature mango, chilli, mangosteen and aubergine is coloured by hand with a fine brush, dried, and dipped in the warm agar — Wongnai notes the finished sweets command high prices precisely because the method looks easy and the handwork is not. Made for weddings, ordinations and temple fairs, look chup is the Thai kitchen showing off.

The Kapook pot: 500 g steamed mung beans, 250 g sugar, 250 ml coconut milk and ¼ tsp salt, kneaded and rolled into 10 g balls, dipped in agar up to four times. The Wongnai pot (cherries): 2 cups steamed mung beans, ¾ cup sugar, 1 cup coconut milk and ½ tsp salt; 3 tsp agar and ¼ cup sugar in 2 cups of water, dipped in two or three coats. The TrueID pot: 125 g mung beans with 150 g coconut milk, 125 g sugar and 5 g glutinous rice flour worked into the paste, agar at 10 g to 440 ml of water. All of them, from the first washing of the beans to the last dip, are the same sweet.

The Method

  1. The beans. Wash the hulled split mung beans in three or four changes of water until the water runs clear — every source starts here — then cook them soft. Aroifin boils them in water for 20-25 minutes; Kapook and Wongnai soak them first and steam them. Soft enough to crush is the test, whichever route you take.
  2. The paste. Blend the soft beans with the coconut milk and sugar until completely smooth, then turn the purée into a pan. This is the step that decides the sweet: stir over a medium-low heat, patiently, until the paste dries out and comes away from the pan in one mass. Wongnai's test is the practical one — keep going until nothing sticks to the spoon. Underdone paste will not hold a shape, and the fruit sags while you paint it.
  3. The shaping. Cool the paste completely, knead it smooth, and roll it into small balls — Kapook weighs them at 10 g apiece. Now the craft: pinch, press and score the balls into miniature fruit with your fingers and a skewer. Beginners, Aroifin advises, should start with round shapes — oranges, mangoes, chillies — before attempting durians.
  4. The painting. Dilute the food colourings with water and paint each piece in thin layers, letting every coat dry before the next. Skewer each sweet on a cocktail stick and stand the sticks in a block of foam; the paint must be bone dry before the first dip or it bleeds into the jelly.
  5. The jelly. Stir the water, agar powder, sugar and jasmine essence together, leave the mix to stand ten minutes, then boil it, stirring, and take it off the heat. Let it cool until just warm — too hot, and it slides off the fruit and can melt the paint; too cool, and it sets in the bowl.
  6. The dip. Dip each painted sweet into the warm agar, rest it until the coat sets, then dip again. Aroifin stops at two coats; Wongnai takes three; Kapook goes as far as four. Let the shell set completely, draw out the skewers, trim the excess jelly with a sharp knife, and the fruit is ready to serve in little paper cases.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Boil or steam. Aroifin boils the mung beans in water until soft. Kapook and Wongnai soak the beans for several hours and steam them — Wongnai specifies a vigorous steam for 20-30 minutes. The disagreement is minor, but it changes the texture going into the pan: steamed beans absorb less water, so the paste dries out faster when you stir it.

How many dips. Two (Aroifin), three (Wongnai), four (Kapook). The more coats, the thicker and shinier the shell — and the longer each piece takes. Everyone agrees on the rule behind the number: dip, let the coat set, dip again. One thick coat is never the answer.

Flour in the paste. Most recipes keep the paste to beans, coconut milk and sugar. TrueID's star-shaped version works 5 g of glutinous rice flour into every 125 g of beans, and its mixed-fruit version uses a spoon of tapioca starch — a stiffer paste that holds sharper edges. The classic paste has neither, and Kapook's four-coat version is flourless.

The salt. Wongnai salts the paste (½ tsp), Kapook a pinch (¼ tsp), TrueID the same pinch. Aroifin salts not at all. It is a quiet seasoning that sharpens the sweetness of the beans, and recipes that omit it read a touch flatter.

The sugar. Aroifin uses 200 g to 250 g of beans; Kapook uses half that, 250 g to 500 g — a noticeably less sweet paste. Wongnai sits between. Taste as you stir; the paste should be sweet enough to carry the bean, not jam-sweet.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Calling it marzipan and swapping in almonds. The almond is the ancestor, not the ingredient. Mung bean paste cooked in coconut milk is the whole point of look chup — the Portuguese recipe was adapted because almonds were scarce in Siam. Ground almonds give you marzipan; you already had that.
  • Gelatine instead of agar. Agar is what makes the shell hard, glassy and stable at room temperature — gelatine stays soft, weeps in warm air and clouds in the fridge. Every Thai source specifies agar (ผงวุ้น), and the sweet does not work without it.
  • One thick dip. The shell is built up in thin coats, each set before the next. A single heavy dip leaves a cloudy, uneven skin and floods the painted detail. The gloss people photograph comes from repetition, not volume.
  • Shaping the paste warm. The paste must be completely cold — and, ideally, rested overnight — before shaping. Warm paste is sticky and won't hold a sculpted edge, and the fruit relaxes into blobs while you paint.
  • Whole green mung beans. Look chup wants hulled split beans (ถั่วเขียวเลาะเปลือก): the green skin makes a speckled, grey paste, and the pieces lose the clean ivory canvas the paint needs.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Hulled split mung beans (ถั่วเขียวเลาะเปลือก): sold as "mung dal (skinless)" or "yellow moong dal" in Indian grocers and world-food aisles — the same bean, ready to use.
  • Jasmine essence (กลิ่นมะลิ): Thai grocers stock it in small bottles; a drop of rosewater is a fair stand-in, and plain water is fine.
  • Agar-agar powder: in the baking section of larger supermarkets and any Asian grocer; the Thai brand "นางเงือก" (Nang Nguek / mermaid) that Kapook specifies is the same seaweed gel as any powder.
  • Coconut milk: full-fat canned, one 400 ml tin covers the 250 ml measure with some to spare.
  • Food colouring: gel colours keep the paste from loosening, but liquid colours diluted with water work too — a fine artist's brush and a block of florist's foam (or an upturned egg box) are the only other equipment.
