Kai Palo (ไข่พะโล้)

Pork belly and eggs braised in a sweet five-spice soy stew — palm sugar caramelised until it goes dark, then star anise, cinnamon and a long, quiet simmer. The eggs give the dish its name.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Serves 3–4 · ~1¾ hours (better the next day)

The Stew Named After Its Eggs

พะโล้ is the Thai form of a Chinese braise — the five-spice, sweet-soy technique that came to Bangkok with Teochew and Hokkien cooks and settled into every rice-and-curry shop in the country. The meat can be anything: pork belly, pork leg, chicken, tofu. But the dish is always called ไข่พะโล้, eggs in phalo, because the eggs are the part nobody skips.

It is a home dish before it is a shop dish. Wongnai cook GopOpOp learned it from her mother, who made it for temple merit events — peeled eggs must be flawless when they go to the monks, and she jokes that of a hundred eggs sent, ninety-five came back looking presentable. The same recipe note appears in every source: make it today, eat it tomorrow. It reheats better than it was, and cold phalo sets into a dark jelly you can portion straight from the fridge.

The flavour is sweet-led but deep. Kapook's version describes it as หวานนำ — sweetness first — built on caramelised palm sugar, with the salt of soy and the warmth of star anise and cinnamon behind it. Aroifin's recipe promises the whole point: spoon the dark broth over hot rice.

The TrueID pot: the same build — 600 g pork belly, ten eggs, palm sugar caramel — but with coconut flower sugar (น้ำตาลโตนด) as an option and the note that palm sugar runs sweeter, plus fried firm tofu in the pot. The Wongnai pots: Nickktwf's dorm-room version swaps the whole spices for a ready-made phalo spice packet and 6 tbsp palm sugar, simmering a full hour and a half; GopOpOp's salty temple version works with 10 coriander roots, oyster sauce instead of fish sauce, Sichuan pepper alongside the star anise, and fried tofu at the end. The Kapook quick pot: no powder, no fuss — chicken wings, two cinnamon sticks, star anise to taste, and light soy, ready in half an hour.

The Method

  1. The eggs. Start the eggs in cold water with a spoon of salt and stir constantly until the water comes to the boil — TrueID's trick, which keeps the yolks centred so the peeled eggs look right in the bowl. Boil six minutes, cool in cold water, peel. (Nickktwf's dorm recipe boils a full half hour; the eggs survive it, but they darken through.)
  2. The paste. Pound coriander root, garlic and peppercorns — the สามเกลอ, three friends, that start half the stews in Thai cooking. Fry them in hot oil until the smell fills the kitchen.
  3. The caramel. Add the palm sugar and stir over medium heat until it melts and darkens. This is the flavour of the dish; Aroifin and TrueID both take it to a proper dark brown before the pork goes anywhere near the pot.
  4. The pork. Fry the pork belly pieces in the caramel until the surface firms and colours, then season with fish sauce. The caramel should cling to every piece.
  5. The spices and water. Add the star anise and cinnamon, fry a moment until fragrant, then pour in the water. Bring to the boil and skim the foam that rises.
  6. The eggs. Add the peeled eggs only once the liquid is boiling — Aroifin is specific about this, and it keeps the eggs from breaking apart in the pot. Then turn the heat low.
  7. The wait. Simmer gently for at least half an hour; an hour to an hour and a half if you want the pork to melt rather than chew. Correct the colour with dark soy near the end, scatter coriander over the pot, and serve over hot rice with the broth spooned over everything.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Powder or whole spices. Kapook Creator and Wongnai's Nickktwf reach for a packet of ผงพะโล้, the ready-made five-spice powder. Kapook's quick recipe, GopOpOp, TrueID and Aroifin all use whole spices — star anise and cinnamon toasted in the pot — and GopOpOp says the packet version never smells as good as pounding your own. Nickktwf concedes the same after trying a shop-bought spice sachet.

When the sugar goes in. Aroifin, TrueID and Nickktwf caramelise the palm sugar first, before the meat, so the pork fries in the caramel. GopOpOp holds the palm sugar back until the pork is already tender — her reasoning: sugar added early seals the surface of the meat, the sauce coats but never soaks in, and the pork stays firm instead of melting. Two schools, same ingredient, opposite order.

The salty base. TrueID and Aroifin season with fish sauce. GopOpOp's temple pot uses oyster sauce. Kapook Creator and Nickktwf use the bottled seasoning sauce every Thai kitchen knows (sauce with the green cap), and Kapook's quick recipe relies on light soy with fish sauce as an optional extra. The braise forgives all four.

The sweetener. TrueID offers coconut flower sugar in place of palm sugar, with the warning that palm sugar runs sweeter. Everyone else simply uses palm sugar, in wildly different amounts — one tablespoon (Kapook Creator) to six (Nickktwf) to 150 g for a family pot (Aroifin).

The protein. Pork belly is the default, but Kapook's quick recipe is built on chicken wings and notes the stew works with pork leg or tofu alone, and GopOpOp and TrueID both drop fried tofu into the pot as a second citizen. The only constant in every version: the eggs.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • No caramel. Western braises brown the meat and skip the sugar. Palo's whole character is the palm sugar caramelised before the pork goes in — Aroifin and TrueID both treat it as the first real step, and a version that just stirs sugar into the water tastes thin.
  • Using generic five-spice powder as a shortcut to the same place. The spices here are star anise and cinnamon — whole, toasted in the pot — with coriander root, garlic and pepper carrying the savoury side. Chinese five-spice powder has cloves and fennel too; the Thai pot mostly doesn't.
  • Dropping the eggs in with the raw meat. Aroifin is explicit: eggs go into boiling liquid, or they crumble. Western versions that stew everything together from cold end up with egg gravel in the broth.
  • Serving it the same day as the point. Every source says the dish improves with reheating — Nickktwf cooks one day and serves the next, and cold phalo sets to a jelly that portions cleanly. The Western habit of serving a braise fresh misses the dish's actual peak.
  • Treating it as a curry. No coconut milk, no paste, no chilli heat. Western recipes that "round it out" with coconut or spice paste have invented a different dish.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Pork belly: standard in every UK supermarket; buy the thickest slices or a whole piece and cut it yourself. Streaky bacon is not a substitute — it is cured and will salt the stew.
  • Coriiander root: often still attached to bunches of coriander in Thai and Vietnamese grocers; otherwise use the lower stems of supermarket coriander. Garlic and peppercorns need no introduction.
  • Star anise and cinnamon: the usual supermarket jars are fine. If you want GopOpOp's Sichuan pepper (ซวงเจีย), Thai and Chinese grocers stock it.
  • Palm sugar: blocks or pucks at any Thai grocer and most large supermarkets; light muscovado plus a pinch of white sugar is the accepted stand-in, though the flavour is not quite the same.
  • Fish sauce, dark soy (sweet if you can find it), oyster sauce: Thai grocers. The Thai sweet dark soy (ซีอิ๊วดำหวาน) is thicker and sweeter than Chinese dark soy; use half the amount of Chinese dark soy plus a little sugar if that is all you have.
  • Ready-made phalo powder: Thai brands like Suttharos come in packets with the spice bag included at Thai supermarkets online — Nickktwf's shortcut if you do not keep whole spices.
