Recipe · Thailand · A Name That Means ‘Plain-Boiled’ — And The Fish That Outgrew It

Pla Pae Sa (ปลาแป๊ะซะ)

A whole fish fried until the skin shatters, and a mortar-pounded sauce of garlic, fresh chilli and coriander root — the Isaan way with a name that started life meaning something far plainer.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 2 October 2026 · Serves 2–3 · ~45 minutes

One Name, Three Sauces

Kapook's editors unpack the name: แป๊ะ (pae) means white, or plain, and ซะ (sa) means boiling something in plain water or stock and ladling the liquid over it — a Teochew kitchen technique, not a dish. The plate that kept the name is the old Chinese one: a whole snakehead steamed and laid on cabbage, drowned in a light, sour-sweet broth of pickled plum, pickled garlic and ginger, eaten with a fermented soybean dip on the side.

Then the name ran loose. The version that dominates Thai restaurant menus today is a whole fish deep-fried until the skin crackles, then drowned in a pot of แกงส้ม sour curry — the version Kapook itself describes as the recipe being adapted to Thai tastes. And in Isaan, แป๊ะซะ means something else again: the sauce. Garlic, fresh chilli and coriander root, pounded in a mortar with roasted peanuts — sharp, salty, faintly sweet — served beside a fried or grilled fish. Same words, three dishes, and every one of them worth eating.

The recipe below is the Isaan way — the deep-fried fish with the pounded sauce — with the other two families written out in full, because the Thai sources do not agree, and neither should a page that pretends they do.

The Pantip thread that this sauce comes from gives no quantities — its cooks season by taste, which is how the sauce behaves. The Chinese pot (Kapook): a 700–800 g snakehead steamed 8–10 minutes, laid on cabbage, drowned in a litre of stock boiled with 3–4 pickled plums and 3 tablespoons of their brine, 3 heads of pickled garlic with 1–2 tablespoons of its juice, shredded ginger, a tablespoon of ground white pepper, light soy and a little rock sugar — celery, spring onion and sliced chillies scattered on top. The sour-curry pot (Aroifin): a fried whole fish in a hot pot with 150 g of แกงส้ม paste — Aroifin boils a second, smaller fish and pounds its flesh into the paste — seasoned with 2 tbsp palm sugar, 1 tbsp white sugar, 6 tbsp fish sauce, ½ tsp salt and 12 tbsp tamarind water, over blanched white cabbage, yardlong beans and lotus stems.

The Method

  1. Clean the fish. Scrape the scales, gut it, then rub the skin with coarse salt and a little tapioca starch until the slime lifts, and rinse. Aroifin uses this salt-and-starch scrub specifically to kill the fishy smell — Kapook offers the same trick with salt and tamarind water.
  2. Score it. Cut deep diagonal slashes down to the bone on both sides. The slashes let the heat in, so a thick fish cooks through while the outside crisps.
  3. Fry it. Oil hot and deep enough to cover the fish. Lower it in and leave it alone — Aroifin times 8–10 minutes a side and warns against moving the fish early, or the skin won't fluff. Turn once, fry the second side, lift out and drain.
  4. Pound the aromatics. Fresh chillies, garlic and coriander roots into the mortar first — pound them fine. This is the base of the sauce and it cannot be done in a blender the same way; the pounding draws the oils out of the roots.
  5. Pound the peanuts. Roasted peanuts go in next, pounded until broken but still nubbly. The Pantip cooks are specific: rough, never a paste — the nubs are the texture of the sauce.
  6. Season and thin. Sweet chilli sauce, fish sauce, lime juice — then taste. The thread's rule: the chilli sauce is already sweet, so sugar only if you must, and cooled boiled water if the sauce is too thick. One of the thread's cooks adds MSG; the rest leave it out.
  7. Serve. The hot fish on a platter, the sauce in a bowl beside it, rice and raw vegetables to go with. Spoon the sauce over the flesh or dip as you eat — Wongnai's cook eats his version with rice or as drinking food, and the Isaan sauce works the same way.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

What แป๊ะซะ even is. Kapook traces the name to the Teochew technique — plain-boiled, stock-ladled — and then spends its own intro explaining that the classic steamed fish in pickled-plum soup and the fried fish under แกงส้ม sour curry share the name while being completely different dishes. The Pantip thread opens on the same confusion, because its version is neither: in Isaan, แป๊ะซะ is the dipping sauce.

The sauce. Three camps. Kapook and Cookpad build the Chinese sauce: pickled plum, pickled garlic, ginger, a whisper of sugar, over a steamed fish. Wongnai and Aroifin go Thai: แกงส้ม paste, tamarind water, palm sugar, poured over a fried fish. Pantip goes Isaan: fresh chillies, garlic and coriander root pounded with roasted peanuts, seasoned with sweet chilli sauce, fish sauce and lime. Even inside the Pantip thread there are factions — an old tamarind-and-palm-sugar version bound with pounded roasted peanuts and boiled egg yolk, a pickled-garlic version, and the fresh-chilli one above.

The fish. Kapook, Wongnai and Aroifin all use ปลาช่อน, the snakehead — the classic. Cookpad uses ปลาทับทิม, the red tilapia. Aroifin goes further and boils a second, smaller fish to make the stock for the sauce, then pounds its flesh into the curry paste.

How the fish is cooked. Steamed 8–15 minutes (Kapook, Cookpad), deep-fried until crisp (Wongnai, Aroifin, and Kapook's description of the modern restaurant version), or grilled — the fish the Isaan sauce is most often served beside. The name travels; the cooking doesn't.

The savoury backbone. Wongnai's sour curry is built on a Knorr chicken stock cube. Kapook's broth is soy-based and its editors warn not to salt it early, because the pickled plums give up more salt as they sit. The Pantip sauce uses fish sauce and sweet chilli sauce. Three different answers to the same question.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Treating it as one fixed dish. There is no single "pae sa sauce" that covers all three families. A jar of sweet chilli sauce over steamed fish is nobody's version of this dish — the Chinese pot is pickled plum and garlic, the restaurant pot is sour curry, the Isaan pot is a mortar sauce.
  • Skipping the mortar. The Isaan sauce is pounded — chillies, garlic and coriander root first, then roasted peanuts until nubbly. Blending everything smooth, or opening a bottle of sweet chilli sauce and calling it done, produces something sweet and flat with none of the texture.
  • Filleting the fish. Every Thai source serves the fish whole — head, bones, skin and all. The whole fish holds its shape through the fry, and the skin is the point. A fillet "pae sa" is a different, lesser dish.
  • Moving the fish in the pan. Aroifin's rule is 8–10 minutes untouched, one turn. Fidgeting with the fish tears the skin, and the crisp, fluffy outside never happens.
  • Over-salting the broth early. Kapook warns that the pickled plums give up salt as the soup sits and cools — season lightly, and let people correct at the table. A western habit of seasoning hard at the stove makes the Chinese version inedible by the end of the meal.
  • Over-sweetening. The Isaan sauce takes its sweetness from the sweet chilli sauce; the thread's cooks add sugar only as a last resort. A western "balanced" version with extra sugar reads like bottled dip.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Snakehead (ปลาช่อน) isn't sold here, and the Thai sources themselves swap freely — Cookpad uses tilapia. A whole sea bass, sea bream or tilapia at 700–800 g works; Asian grocers often sell tilapia live.
  • Coriander roots (รากผักชี): buy coriander with the roots still attached at Thai or Vietnamese grocers; if all you can find is trimmed, use the lowest, thickest part of the stems.
  • Roasted peanuts (ถั่วลิสงคั่ว): dry-roast raw peanuts yourself in a pan — one of the Pantip cooks says shop-bought peanut brittle never smells or tastes as good.
  • Sweet chilli sauce (ซอสพริก): the Thai-style bottle used for ไก่ทอด — thin and glossy — not the lumpy Western table condiment.
  • For the Chinese pot: pickled plums (บ๊วยดอง) and pickled garlic (กระเทียมดอง) in jars at Thai grocers; for the sour-curry pot, ready-made แกงส้ม paste (Mae Pranom or similar).
