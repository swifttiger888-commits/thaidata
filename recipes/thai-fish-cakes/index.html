Thai Fish Cakes (ทอดมันปลา)

Fish kneaded with red curry paste and kaffir lime until it turns elastic, shaped flat and fried golden. The bounce is the point — and the classic dip is the ajad pickle, not sweet chilli.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 4 (10–12 cakes) · ~40 minutes

The Fish Cakes That Bounce

ทอดมันปลา means fried fish paste, and the dish lives or dies on texture: the Thai word cooks use is เด้ง — "bounce". The trick is mechanical. The fish is kept ice-cold, worked with iced salted water, then kneaded hard for up to ten minutes until the protein tightens into something springy. A fish cake that doesn't bounce is, by the standard of any Thai kitchen, not finished.

The flavour engine is the same red curry paste that drives แกงเผ็ด — pounded chillies, lemongrass, galangal, kaffir lime zest, shrimp paste — plus hair-thin shavings of kaffir lime leaf and sliced long beans folded through at the end. Egg is the binder in most Thai recipes, one or two of them depending on the cook.

The fish itself is ปลากราย — the featherback, whose flesh is famously elastic. And the dip is its own argument: the classic is ajad, the sharp cucumber pickle, but plenty of market sellers hand over sweet chilli sauce instead. Both exist in Thailand; only one belongs to the dish's older self.

The Method

  1. Keep it cold. The fish stays over ice. Thai cooks often pound it with iced salted water first — cold fish is what turns elastic later.
  2. Mix. Fish, red curry paste, eggs, fish sauce and sugar in a bowl.
  3. Knead. About ten minutes, folding the mix into the centre of the bowl, until it turns springy. This is the bounce, and there is no shortcut for it.
  4. Fold in the crunch. The sliced long beans and the hair-thin kaffir lime leaves, kneaded just enough to distribute.
  5. Shape. Flat cakes about half a centimetre thick — thick cakes cook dark outside before the middle is done.
  6. Fry medium. Deep-fry in medium oil until golden and floating, turning once. Not maximum heat — the outside must not outrun the inside.
  7. Drain. Paper towel, then keep warm while the ajad is made.
  8. Pickle and serve. Vinegar, water, sugar and salt simmered until dissolved, cooled, then cucumber, chilli, shallots and coriander stirred through. Hot cakes, cold pickle.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The dip. The classic ajad pickle (ครัวบ้านพิม calls it the old-fashioned dipping sauce — สูตรโบราณ) against the market habit of sweet chilli (น้ำจิ้มไก่, which is what the Wongnai recipe serves with it). Both are published; the older dish pairs with ajad.

Eggs. Wongnai uses two, ครัวบ้านพิม uses one, and some recipes use none — the binder question. More egg makes a softer, more omelette-like cake.

Basil in the mix. ครัวบ้านพิม folds three cups of holy basil into the paste; most recipes, including Wongnai's, keep it out. Both exist; the basil version leans herbal and green.

Fish sauce. From two tablespoons (Wongnai) down to two-thirds of one (aroifin). The curry paste already carries salt, so the lighter hand is the modern one.

Knead time. Five minutes versus ten. Everyone agrees on the goal — เด้ง, the bounce — and argues about the clock.

The fish. Featherback is the standard, but Thai cooks make ทอดมัน from whatever firm fish is at the market — snakehead, and small river fish in the provinces. The technique travels; the fish does not.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Flour and starch filler. Western fish cakes use flour to bind; Thai fish cakes hold on kneaded fish protein and egg. Flour makes them doughy and kills the bounce.
  • Sweet chilli by default. The classic dip is ajad. Sweet chilli is the market shortcut, not the recipe.
  • No kaffir lime. The hair-thin lime leaf shavings are the defining aroma of the dish. Leave them out and it's just a fried fish burger patty.
  • Maximum-heat frying. Dark outside, raw middle. Medium oil, patience.
  • Over-mixing with a machine. A food processor will overwork the paste into rubber. Knead by hand, or pulse very briefly and finish by hand.
  • Thick patties. Half a centimetre. Thick cakes are how you get burnt-and-raw at once.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • The fish, honestly: featherback isn't sold in the UK. A few Thai grocers carry it frozen; otherwise use a firm white fish — cod, haddock or hake. The result is softer and less springy than the real thing. That's the trade, and it's worth saying rather than hiding.
  • The eggs in the mix are what make UK fish work — this is why the two-egg version (Wongnai) is the better starting point here.
  • Red curry paste: the same paste from our red curry recipe, or a good Thai-grocer paste by weight. A fresh one matters more here than anywhere — it carries the whole flavour.
  • Kaffir lime leaves: frozen in Thai grocers; slice while still partly frozen, as thin as you can. The dish is not the same without them.
  • Long beans: yardlong beans from Thai grocers; French beans work in a pinch but lack the crunch.
  • Frying: 170°C, no crowding. Two or three batches — crowding drops the oil temperature and the cakes go greasy.
  • Ajad: everything's standard supermarket stock. It keeps a week in the fridge.
