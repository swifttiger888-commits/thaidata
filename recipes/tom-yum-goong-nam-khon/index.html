Recipe · Thailand · The Creamy Brother Of The Clear Soup

Tom Yum Goong Nam Khon (ต้มยำกุ้งน้ำข้น)

Roasted chilli paste stirred into the broth until it turns red and heavy, prawns in, milk poured last — off the heat — and the lime squeezed at the very end. The tom yum that eats like a meal.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 3–4 · ~25 minutes

The Soup That Names Its Thickness

น้ำข้น means thick water, and the thickness comes from two things that the clear version never touches: น้ำพริกเผา — the roasted chilli paste — and a pour of milk at the end. Stir the paste into the broth and it dissolves into a red, oily, almost creamy stock; add the milk last, with the heat off, and the soup turns the colour of a Thai sunset.

Every Thai source agrees on the order of play: the paste goes in early, the milk goes in last, and the lime is the final word. Kapook's recipe — borrowed from a Pantip home cook — boils the prawn heads first so the fat inside them (มันในหัวกุ้ง) leaks into the water, giving the soup its colour and aroma before a single chilli has gone in. It is the same instinct as the milk: nam khon is built to be rich.

The dish is the creamy sibling of classic tom yum goong, and Thai cooks argue cheerfully about what the creamer should be. A Pantip topic exists for exactly that question — a cook making the soup for a Japanese guest, torn between coconut milk, evaporated milk and fresh milk. The two big recipe sites split the vote: evaporated milk for richness, fresh milk for lightness. What nobody argues about is that the lime goes in at the end, or the soup is not the soup.

The fresh-milk pot (Kapook, from a Pantip cook): 500 g prawns, heads boiled first for the fat, then galangal, lemongrass, 10 sliced red chillies and a cup of straw mushrooms; ½ tbsp nam prik pao in with the prawns, fresh milk at the end, more paste if the colour stays pale. The Wongnai pot (LunnyToon): 600 g prawns, a bunch of galangal, lemongrass and kaffir lime, 6 small shallots, 10 bird's eye and 7 jinda chillies, 1 tbsp nam prik pao, 7 tbsp fresh milk, 2 tbsp fish sauce, 1 tbsp sugar, 2 tbsp lime juice — serves 4–5. The brand pot (Deksomboon): 200 g prawns, 350 ml water, 150 ml evaporated milk, 3 tbsp nam prik pao, 3 tbsp fish sauce, 4 tbsp lime juice, 2 tbsp chopped chillies — herbs simmered one minute, everything finished off the heat.

The Method

  1. The broth. Bring the water to a boil with the salt, smashed lemongrass, sliced galangal, smashed shallots and torn kaffir lime leaves. Deksomboon times the simmer: one minute is enough to pull the herbs through.
  2. The paste. Stir in the nam prik pao until it dissolves. This is the step that earns the dish its name — the broth thickens, turns red, and the oil from the paste beads on the surface. Kapook's fresh-milk recipe goes light here and tops up at the end if the colour is pale.
  3. The mushrooms and chillies. Add the straw mushrooms and simmer until just cooked, then the smashed chillies. Every source gets the mushrooms in early so they drink the broth.
  4. The prawns. Add the prawns and season with fish sauce. Prawns cook in moments — do not wait around, or they turn rubbery.
  5. The milk, off the heat. Take the pot off the heat before the milk goes in — evaporated milk boiled hard will split. Stir it through, then the lime, which every source saves for last: Kapook's cook squeezes it straight into the serving bowl and ladles the soup over it, Deksomboon adds it to the pot once the heat is off. Garnish with culantro and serve with rice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The creamer. Evaporated milk (Deksomboon, Kapook's พี่ขวดเข้าครัว pot) versus fresh milk (Kapook's Pantip-sourced pot, Wongnai's LunnyToon) versus coconut milk — the third school. A Pantip topic is literally this question: a cook making nam khon for a Japanese guest, unable to choose between กะทิ, นมข้นจืด and นมสด. Another Pantip cook who poured boxed coconut milk in near the end found it split into white flecks and asked what to switch to. Evaporated milk is the sturdier pour; coconut milk wants careful, low heat.

How much nam prik pao. Half a tablespoon (Kapook's fresh-milk pot) to three full tablespoons (Deksomboon). Kapook's cook adds more at the end purely for colour — proof that the paste is doing two jobs, flavour and paint.

When the lime goes in. Squeezed into the serving bowl, with the soup ladled over it (Kapook's พี่ขวดเข้าครัว pot) — versus into the pot after the heat is off (Deksomboon, Kapook's Pantip-sourced pot). Both agree on the rule underneath: never boil the lime.

The heat. Twenty bird's eye chillies plus four jinda (Kapook), ten plus seven (Wongnai), two tablespoons chopped (Deksomboon). Kapook's pot also salts the water first; the others rely on fish sauce alone — 5 tbsp in one pot, 2–3 in the others.

The mushrooms. A fixed cup (Kapook) versus "then as you like" (Wongnai). No source skips them entirely — straw mushrooms are the vegetable that the milk lands on.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Boiling the milk. Every Thai source adds the milk last, with the heat off or dying. Boil it and the milk splits into flecks — the exact complaint the Pantip cook posted about her coconut-milk pot.
  • Skipping the nam prik pao. Without the roasted chilli paste this is just clear tom yum with milk poured in. The paste is the thickener, the colour and the whole point of the น้ำข้น name.
  • Lime early. Western soup habits season at the start. Thai sources squeeze the lime last — into the bowl or the cooling pot. Boiled lime goes flat and bitter.
  • The long simmer. Prawns go in and come out in minutes. Simmer them western-soup-style and they turn to rubber.
  • Cream by default. No Thai source uses cream. The two main camps are evaporated and fresh milk, with coconut milk as a careful third — never dairy cream, which buries the chilli and the lime.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Nam prik pao (น้ำพริกเผา, roasted chilli paste): Thai grocers and online Thai supermarkets; the Pantai brand is the common UK sight. It is sweet, smoky and hot — taste it before you add the fish sauce, since it carries salt of its own.
  • Evaporated milk (นมข้นจืด): the same Carnation tin in any UK supermarket — one small 170 g tin is more than the 140 ml needed.
  • Straw mushrooms: fresh ones are rare in the UK; the canned, brined kind at Asian grocers is the standard substitute — rinse before use.
  • Kaffir lime leaves: fresh at Thai grocers in season, frozen year-round; lemongrass and galangal from the same aisle.
  • Chillies: bird's eye chillies from any Asian grocer; the Thai counts (20+) assume Thai heat tolerance — start with a third and work up.
  • Culantro (ผักชีฝรั่ง, sawtooth coriander): Thai grocers; ordinary coriander does the job if you cannot find it.
