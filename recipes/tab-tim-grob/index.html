Tab Tim Grob (ทับทิมกรอบ)

Water chestnuts dyed ruby-red, rolled in tapioca starch, boiled and shocked until each piece is a translucent jewel with a crisp heart — served cold in scented syrup under salted coconut cream.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 4–6 · ~50 minutes

Rubies in Coconut Milk

ทับทิมกรอบ means "crisp rubies" — the name is literal. Diced water chestnuts are soaked in red syrup until the colour seeps through the flesh, rolled in tapioca starch, and boiled just long enough for the starch to turn into a translucent shell. Then the trick: the rubies go straight from the boil into iced water. The shock sets the shell and keeps the chestnut heart crisp. That one step is the difference between jewels and mush.

The dessert is an old palace and café classic — the same Chinese-Thai café culture that gave Thailand its sock-brewed coffee sold this over shaved ice a hundred years ago. It is still the dessert most Thai restaurants reach for when the menu needs something cold, pretty and not too sweet.

The third element matters as much as the rubies: the coconut cream is salted, and the syrup is scented — pandan in most recipes, jasmine in others, and in the most traditional version the coconut milk is scented with candle smoke, อบควันเทียน. Sweet against salt against the cold. It works.

The Method

  1. Prep the chestnuts. Peel, boil until just cooked, dice into roughly one-centimetre cubes.
  2. Dye. Soak the cubes in the red syrup 15–20 minutes, until the colour soaks through the flesh, not just onto the surface.
  3. Coat. Drain briefly — not bone-dry — and roll in tapioca starch until every piece is coated. For a thicker shell, dampen and roll twice.
  4. Boil and shock. Into boiling water; when they float with translucent shells, lift them straight into iced water. The shock is what keeps the heart crisp.
  5. Syrup. Sugar dissolved in water with the pandan leaves over low heat, simmered briefly, cooled completely.
  6. Coconut cream. Warmed gently — never boiled, or it splits — with the sugar and salt stirred in until dissolved. Cool.
  7. Assemble. Rubies in a bowl, cold syrup to nearly cover, salted coconut cream poured over, over crushed ice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The colour. Red syrup — Hale's Blue Boy, the sweet sala-scented one — against plain water with red food colouring (Wongnai's way). The syrup adds sweetness and a faint fruit note; the colouring adds nothing but red.

The scent. Candle smoke (อบควันเทียน — the old way: coconut milk held under a lit candle's smoke), pandan (Wongnai), or jasmine essence (TrueID). All three are published; the candle is the most traditional and the most effort.

Evaporated milk. aroifin stirs 40 g of evaporated milk into the coconut cream for richness; the other recipes keep the cream plain. Both work — the salt stays regardless.

Single or double coat. One starch coat for a thin shell, two for the thick-chewy version. Sources describe both; the double coat holds better through a long soak.

How the chestnut is cooked. Some recipes boil the chestnuts first, some only par-cook, some start from raw and rely on the final boil. The just-cooked version above keeps the heart at its crispest.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Jelly "rubies". Tinned and jelly-based versions replace the tapioca shell and the crisp chestnut heart — the whole point of the dessert.
  • Skipping the ice shock. Without the cold shock, the shell goes soft and the rubies turn to mush in the syrup.
  • Boiling the coconut milk. It splits. Warm gently, dissolve, cool.
  • Unsalted coconut. The salt is the balance against the sweet syrup. Unsalted coconut cream makes the dessert cloying.
  • Served warm or room temperature. It is an iced dessert — the cold is half the texture.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Water chestnuts: tinned whole water chestnuts (in brine — drain well) work perfectly and are in most supermarkets. Fresh ones occasionally appear in Chinese grocers.
  • Tapioca starch: the Asian-grocery standard or the baking aisle. Not cornflour — cornflour clouds instead of turning clear.
  • The red: Hale's Blue Boy red sala syrup from Thai grocers (the same shelf as the Thai iced tea ingredients). Fallback — water with red food colouring, exactly as the Wongnai source does.
  • Pandan: frozen leaves in Thai grocers; jasmine essence is the easier scent and is what many Thai home cooks reach for.
  • Evaporated milk in the cream is optional — try it once without, once with.
  • Serve over crushed ice, not cubes — the rubies need to sit in the syrup, and crushed ice chills it fast.
