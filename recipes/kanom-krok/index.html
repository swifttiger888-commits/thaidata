Kanom Krok (ขนมครก)

The market's coconut pancakes: rice-flour batter in the wells of a sizzling pan, coconut cream poured on top, spring onion and corn scattered in — crisp shell, soft custard heart, eaten hot.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 4 (~30 pieces) · ~50 minutes

The Pancakes of the Sizzling Pan

The name is the pan: ขนมครก — ครก being the mortar, and the cooking pan looks like one: a round plate of shallow wells over a charcoal or gas fire. Every Thai market has a stall working one, the vendor pouring batter outside-in, topping each well with coconut cream, scattering spring onion and corn, and flipping the finished half-cups together two by two. The whole thing hisses and smells of coconut.

The texture is the entire point: crisp at the rim, custard in the middle. Two quiet tricks earn it — a scoop of cooked jasmine rice blended into the batter for the soft heart, and clear lime water for the crisp edge. The traditional recipe below uses both; the sources argue about everything else.

Fillings are where vendors show off. The plain coconut version is the classic; spring onion is the savoury standard; corn and taro the sweet-loaded ones; and the palace version — ขนมครกชาววัง — piles on golden threads, raisins, pumpkin and more. The pan doesn't judge; it just cooks.

The Method

  1. Fillings. Corn sliced thin, taro diced — both steamed until cooked, about 20 minutes. Spring onions sliced.
  2. Batter. Rice flour, cooked jasmine rice, lime water, both coconut milks, sugar and salt blended smooth. Rest 30 minutes — the flour needs it.
  3. Topping. Head coconut milk, sugar, salt and the rice flour whisked until dissolved.
  4. Jugs. Both mixtures into narrow-spout pouring jugs — the pour is the technique.
  5. The pan. Kanom krok pan over low heat until properly hot, then wipe the wells with an oiled cloth. (A new pan: wash it three times with detergent, then season it with oil over heat for 30 minutes.)
  6. Pour. Batter first, outside-in around the pan — then the coconut topping over each well immediately, so the two layers set as one.
  7. Fill and cover. Spring onion in straight away; corn and taro once the batter starts to set. Lid on, about 5 minutes.
  8. Lift. When the outer edges turn golden-brown, scoop each out with a spoon. Serve warm — two halves pressed together is the street way.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The cooked rice. TrueID blends cooked jasmine rice into the batter for the soft heart; the Pantip recipe keeps the batter plain rice flour. Both publish; the rice version is the custard-soft one.

Lime water. TrueID uses it for the crisp edge and says plain water works in a pinch; the Pantip recipe uses plain water from the start. The crispness is the argument.

The flour. Wongnai's palace version adds plain flour and baking powder for an extra-crisp shell; the traditional batter is rice flour alone. Both exist under one name.

Sweetener. White sugar in the everyday recipes, palm sugar in Wongnai's — the palm version tastes deeper and slightly caramel.

The topping. Thickened with a spoon of rice flour (TrueID, Pantip) or poured plain (Wongnai). The flour keeps the cream from separating in the wells.

Fillings. Plain and proud, or loaded — spring onion, corn, taro, pumpkin, sesame, golden threads. The vendors argue with their pans.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • The wrong pan. A flat pancake pan produces flat pancakes. The wells are the dish — the crisp rim and the custard centre come from the shape.
  • Wheat-flour batter. Kanom krok is rice-flour — the western "coconut pancake" with wheat flour is a different cake wearing the name.
  • High heat. The edges burn before the centre sets. Low heat, hot pan, patience, lid.
  • One-pour shortcuts. The coconut cream must go on immediately after the batter, as a separate layer. Mixed together, the two textures never happen.
  • Skipping the rest. The 30-minute batter rest is not decorative — the rice flour needs it to cook tender.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • The pan: Thai grocers sell the aluminium kanom krok pan. The honest stand-in is a Dutch poffertjes pan or a Danish æbleskiver pan — same wells, same geometry.
  • Rice flour: standard supermarket or Asian grocer stock. The cooked-jasmine-rice trick needs nothing special.
  • Lime water: edible lime paste (ปูนแดง) from Thai grocers, dissolved and settled; use the clear top. Skipping it costs crispness, not edibility.
  • Coconut milk: freshly pressed is the source's preference, but a good full-fat tin works — TrueID says so themselves.
  • Fillings: corn, taro and spring onions — all standard. Taro from Asian grocers; roast the corn briefly if you want the market's smokiness.
  • Heat: the lowest setting that keeps the pan hot. These are slow little custards, not flash-fried pancakes.
