Recipe · Thailand · The Curry Without Coconut

Gaeng Om (แกงอ่อม)

Chicken roasted with pounded chillies, lemongrass and shallots, simmered with pla ra, dill and a full pot of greens, thickened with toasted rice — Isaan's curry, and not a drop of coconut in it.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 29 September 2026 · 4 servings · ~40 minutes

The Isaan Curry With No Coconut

Mahidol University's Institute of Nutrition opens its recipe with the rule: in Isaan, if a dish is called a แกง — a curry — there is no coconut milk in it, and แกงอ่อม is the proof. The name follows whatever went in the pot: แกงอ่อมไก่ with chicken, อ่อมหมู with pork, อ่อมเนื้อ with beef — and the same pot takes fish or frog when the river provides.

The construction never changes. Aromatics are pounded, roasted in the pot, the meat fried into them, then water, pla ra and the greens. In Mahidol's kitchen version the vegetables outnumber the chicken — 344 g of cabbage, mustard leaves, pumpkin flowers, spring onions, dill and lemon basil against 304 g of meat. The dill (ผักชีลาว) is the dish's signature, the smell that announces it from across the market; toasted rice gives the broth its body.

One warning: the north has a namesake. Sanook's comparison lays it out — northern แกงอ่อม is a different curry under the same name, built on dried chilli, turmeric, shrimp paste and roasted coriander seed and cumin, fried with the meat without oil, no pla ra. Order one in Chiang Mai and you get the spice version; in Khon Kaen you get this one.

The quick pork version (Wongnai, 538k reads): 300 g pork neck, 4–5 red chillies pounded with 2 cloves of garlic, roasted with the pork in the pot, 3 tbsp pla ra, water, one head of Chinese cabbage and 2 bunches of dill — no rice powder, 1 tbsp sugar instead. Aroifin's pork-belly pot: a ladle of pla ra, salt, MSG and 2 tbsp toasted rice powder, choy sum instead of cabbage. Kapook's no-pla-ra version: chicken stock instead of water, galangal and fingerroot in the paste, pea aubergines added. Tumkinaeng's beef version: 300 g beef ribs, kaffir lime leaves torn in by hand, and 2–3 tbsp beef blood stirred into the boiling pot to thicken it. The northern namesake (Sanook): a different curry — dried chilli, turmeric, shrimp paste, roasted coriander seed and cumin, fried without oil.

The Method

  1. The paste. Slice the lemongrass thin and pound it with the shallots and red chillies until fine (Mahidol).
  2. The roast. A little water in the pot to stop the paste sticking; roast it until fragrant, add the chicken and roast it into the paste, stir in the fish sauce and keep roasting until the chicken starts to cook (Mahidol).
  3. The broth. Add the water; once it is hot, stir in the toasted pounded rice, then the pla ra and sugar (Mahidol).
  4. The greens. At a proper boil, with the chicken cooked, add the kaffir lime leaves, then the cabbage, leaf mustard, pumpkin flowers and spring onions (Mahidol).
  5. The finish. When the vegetables begin to wilt, add the dill and lemon basil last; cook until the greens collapse and serve hot — sticky rice on the side (Mahidol, Tumkinaeng).

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Pla ra or not. Every source but one cooks with pla ra. Kapook's ครัวตุ๊กตา version deliberately omits it — the family never ate it — and swaps in chicken stock and fish sauce, adding "if you want it, put it in at the seasoning step". The rest treat it as the dish's backbone.

The thickener. Toasted rice powder (Aroifin 2 tbsp, Kapook 3 tbsp) versus toasted pounded rice — ข้าวเบือคั่ว (Mahidol, 9 g) versus raw soaked pounded rice — ข้าวเบือ (ครูกุลชญา, 2 tbsp) versus beef blood stirred in at a rolling boil (Tumkinaeng) versus nothing at all (Wongnai's ครัวตามใจทอม, whose readers comment to add it).

Garlic in the paste. Aroifin pounds in 5–6 cloves, Wongnai's pot is chilli and garlic only, Kapook and ครูกุลชญา include it — but Mahidol's paste and Tumkinaeng's are chilli, shallot and lemongrass, no garlic. The garlicless pots let the dill and pla ra lead.

Coarse or fine. Aroifin, Kapook and ครัวตามใจทอม pound the paste coarse; Mahidol and ครูกุลชญา pound it fine. A coarse paste leaves flecks that catch on the meat; a fine one dissolves into the broth.

The liquid. Water (Mahidol, ครูกุลชญา), chicken stock (Kapook), or the water used to rinse the mortar — the frugal move that wastes none of the paste (Aroifin, Tumkinaeng).

The simmer. Aroifin's pork-belly pot is done in 20 minutes of cooking; ครูกุลชญา's free-range chicken simmers covered for two hours and twenty minutes. Same dish, different bird.

Sugar. Mahidol adds 3 g and ครัวตามใจทอม a full tablespoon to round the pla ra; the other pots add none — salty, hot, herbal, and nothing else.

Salt's hiding place. Mahidol records the village trick: pound salt in with the chillies to make the paste easier to grind and kill the raw green smell — then hold back on fish sauce and pla ra because the salt is already in there. Aroifin salts the pot instead.

North versus Isaan. Sanook's comparison: the northern namesake uses dried chilli, turmeric, shrimp paste, roasted coriander seed and cumin, fried without oil, seasoned with salt or fish sauce — spice-led and milder. The Isaan version is hot, salty and นัว from the pla ra. Same name, different curry.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Adding coconut milk. The Isaan rule is absolute: a curry called แกง has no coconut in it. Coconut milk turns this into a completely different dish.
  • Boiling everything in one pot of water. The dry-roast is the technique — the paste and meat are fried together until fragrant before any water goes in. Skipping it leaves the paste raw and the broth flat.
  • Using a jar of red curry paste. This is a pounded-fresh paste of chillies, shallots and lemongrass — nothing fermented, no spices. Red curry paste carries cumin, coriander and shrimp paste; it reads as a different dish entirely.
  • Swapping the dill. Dill is not garnish — it is the identity of the dish, the note Mahidol calls its signature. Replace it with coriander and the whole point of the pot is gone.
  • Boiling the greens to mush. The vegetables go in by stage — firm ones first, dill and lemon basil last, wilted not boiled to death. Texture is half the dish; a western one-pot simmer destroys it.
  • Skipping the body. Toasted rice — powder, pounded rice or a spoon of blood — is what makes the broth cling instead of sitting thin. A watery version is a soup missing its thickener.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Pla ra: bottled at Thai grocers (look for ปลาร้า — Suan Sawan and similar). It is strong; start with a tablespoon and work up to the recipe's 21 g.
  • Dill: the same fresh bunches in every UK supermarket — no specialist shop needed.
  • Kaffir lime leaves: fresh at Thai grocers, frozen elsewhere; they freeze well.
  • Pumpkin flowers and leaf mustard: seasonal and mostly at Thai grocers; choy sum, pak choi or more Chinese cabbage stand in.
  • Toasted rice: dry-fry raw glutinous (sticky) rice in a pan until golden and pound it, or buy ready ข้าวคั่ว powder at Thai grocers.
  • Chicken drumsticks: any butcher; free-range for the longer-simmering home-style version.
  • Lemon basil (แมงลัก): Thai grocers; Kapook's cook happily substitutes holy basil when it is missing.
  • Lemongrass, shallots, Thai chillies: standard at supermarkets and Thai grocers alike.
  • Northern version spices (turmeric, cumin seed, coriander seed): any supermarket spice aisle, if you want to try the other แกงอ่อม.
