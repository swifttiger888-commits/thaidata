Gyoza (Kiao Sa)

Japanese-style pan-fried gyoza with a juicy pork and cabbage filling, pleated by hand and finished with the crisp flour-water wing. The dumpling on every Thai menu, done properly.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 3 October 2026 · Serves 4 · 45 minutes

เกี๊ยวซ่า

Gyoza came to Thailand with the Japanese restaurant boom of the last generation, and Thai menus now list it alongside kiao tod (fried wontons) and kanom jeeb in the dumpling corner of the starter page. It is the pan-fried one: a thin wrapper, a juicy pork-and-cabbage centre, and a crisp golden base that shatters when you bite it.

The trick is the cooking method — fry the bases, then add flour-thinned water and steam under a lid. The water cooks the tops, and as it boils away the dissolved flour sets into a lacy, crisp wing that glues the whole row of dumplings together. Flip the pan over and the wing arrives face-up like a trophy.

The Method

  1. Make the filling. Mix the pork with the cabbage, chives, ginger, garlic, soy, oyster sauce, sesame oil, pepper and sugar. Work the mixture hard in one direction until it turns sticky — this is what keeps the filling juicy, not dry.
  2. Fold. Lay a wrapper on your palm, add a heaped teaspoon of filling, and wet the edge with a fingertip of water. Fold in half and pleat one side onto the other, pressing out any air pockets so they don't burst in the pan.
  3. Fry and steam. Heat the oil in a non-stick pan and stand the gyoza flat-side down. Fry uncovered until the bases are golden. Pour in the flour water, cover, and steam for 3–4 minutes until the wrappers turn translucent. Uncover and keep cooking until the water evaporates and leaves a crisp, lacy wing. Invert onto a plate so the wing faces up.
  4. Serve. Serve immediately with the soy-vinegar dip, and shredded cabbage if you like.

Filling that stays juicy

Juiciness is not about adding liquid — it is about working the meat until sticky so the proteins bind, and about squeezing every drop of water out of the cabbage so it doesn't leak during cooking. Some Thai recipes add a little chilled stock instead; both work, but sticky mince is the base.

Pleating, briefly

Wet the edge, fold, and pleat the top side onto the flat side — four or five small pleats is enough. Press the air out as you go; trapped air expands in the pan and bursts the wrapper.

Serving

  • Dip: Two parts soy to two parts rice vinegar, plus chilli oil to taste

Cook's Tips

  • Squeeze the cabbage hard after chopping — released water makes the wrappers soggy and the filling sloppy.
  • The flour-water mix is what creates the crispy 'wing'. Plain water steams the dumplings fine but gives no crunch.
  • Freeze uncooked gyoza on a tray, then bag them. Fry from frozen with an extra two minutes of steaming.

Variations

  • Kimchi gyoza. Swap half the cabbage for squeezed, chopped kimchi and add a spoon of gochujang to the filling.
  • Fried wontons (kiao tod). Same filling in square wonton wrappers, deep-fried instead of pan-steamed.
