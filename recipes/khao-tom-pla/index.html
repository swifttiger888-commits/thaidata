Khao Tom Pla (ข้าวต้มปลา)

Whole jasmine grains in a clear, sweet stock, poached fish, fried garlic — and the tao jeow dipping sauce that makes a Pantip thread out of a bowl of rice soup.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 2 · ~40 minutes

The Rice Soup That Refuses To Be Porridge

ข้าวต้ม (khao tom) is Thailand's answer to congee with one unbreakable rule: the rice stays rice. Grains remain whole and separate, the broth stays clear, and the fish sits on top like a guest, not an ingredient boiled into submission. Chef Phol's recipe at Phol Food Mafia opens with the why — good khao tom pla starts with ข้าวสารเก่า, aged rice, because fresh-crop rice falls apart in the pot and turns the soup to glue.

The stock does the talking. Kapook's grouper version — from a Pantip member's home kitchen — simmers cilantro roots, garlic, dried shrimp and dried squid into a broth that is sweet without a pinch of sugar, while Wongnai's version pours in a litre of pork-bone stock. Either way the broth stays pale and clean; the garnish carries the colour: fried garlic, Chinese celery, spring onion, white pepper.

And then the dipping sauce. Thai rice-soup stalls serve ข้าวต้มปลา with a little dish of เต้าเจี้ยว — fermented yellow bean paste — spiked with chilli. Kapook's cook writes of spooning it over the fish and biting through the beans one by one; a Pantip thread exists purely to ask how to make it, because a bowl without it, in the poster's words, is not worth buying. The fish is quiet. The sauce is the point.

Chef Phol's pot: 120 g grouper per bowl, 1 cup rice, 6 cups water, pandan and salt — the rice water is strained off and becomes half the stock (with 2 cups pork stock), and the poaching liquid for the fish. Wongnai's quick pot: 2 ladles cooked rice, 500 g red snapper, 1 litre pork-bone stock, 4 shiitake, sliced galangal and 2 cilantro roots, seasoned with light soy and pepper. TrueID's winter pot: a whole sea bass, 2 litres of fish stock, Chinese garlic, cilantro root — cooked rice goes straight into the stock.

The Method

  1. The rice. Rinse the rice, then boil it in about 6 cups of water with the salt and pandan leaves — about 10 minutes, until the grains open but stay whole. Strain and keep the rice water: Chef Phol uses it for the stock and for poaching the fish. Kapook's cook goes further, rinsing the starch off the cooked grains (ล้างยาง) so the soup stays clear. This step is what separates khao tom from porridge.
  2. The stock. Simmer the cilantro roots, garlic, dried shrimp and dried squid in water — or half rice water, half pork stock, Chef Phol's way — seasoned with light soy and a little salt. Keep it low until the shrimp and squid go tender. The sweetness in the finished bowl comes from these, not from sugar.
  3. The galangal. Grill the young galangal briefly and chop it fine, then add it to the stock with the tang chye. This is the background note of every good khao tom pla — and the one ingredient Thai cooks argue about most (see below).
  4. The fish. Cut thick slices and poach them in the rice water or stock. Wongnai's rule is absolute: do not stir while the fish cooks, or the broth turns fishy. The doneness cue is visual, from Kapook: the skin-side layer just under the flesh turns translucent — then it is done. Lift the fish out, don't leave it in the pot.
  5. The bowl. Rice first, fish on top, stock ladled over, then fried garlic with its oil, Chinese celery, spring onion and a good dusting of white pepper. The tao jeow dip — bean paste loosened and spiked with chopped chilli — goes alongside, to spoon over the fish. Eat it scalding; the whole point of the dish is the first hot spoonful.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The rice. Wongnai, TrueID and the dory recipe at Kapook use cooked rice, dropped straight into the stock. Kapook's grouper recipe boils raw rice first, drains and rinses it. Chef Phol insists on aged raw rice with pandan, and turns the strained rice water into the stock. Everyone agrees on the outcome — whole grains, clear broth — but gets there by three different roads.

The stock. Dried shrimp and dried squid in plain water (Kapook's grouper pot), pork-bone stock (Wongnai), rice water and pork stock half-and-half (Chef Phol), fish stock (TrueID), or just water with aromatics (the dory recipe). The dish absorbs whatever stock the kitchen has — none of the five sources uses the same one.

The galangal. Kapook grills young galangal and stirs it, chopped fine, into the soup. Wongnai floats slices in the stock. Chef Phol dry-roasts mature galangal, pounds it (ข่าแก่คั่วป่น) and puts it on the table as a condiment, not in the pot at all. Young in, mature out — three schools of the same root.

The fish. Grouper (Kapook, Chef Phol), red snapper (Wongnai), sea bass (TrueID), dory (ครัวแม่ผึ้ง's recipe). The constant: firm, white, lean flesh that holds a slice. No Thai source even considers an oily fish.

The tao jeow. Kapook and Chef Phol make the dipping sauce central — crushed bean paste with chilli, spooned over the fish. Wongnai and TrueID never mention it, and the Pantip thread that asks how to make it treats a bowl without it as not worth buying. Street-stall habit against home-kitchen habit.

The stir rule. Wongnai forbids stirring while the fish cooks — it makes the soup fishy. TrueID's rule is only about timing: blanch briefly, never to the point of falling apart. Both point the same way, though: the fish is handled like a poached egg, not a stew ingredient.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Calling it congee, then cooking it like congee. Khao tom is not jok. The rice is simmered just until the grains open, then drained — or added cooked. Boiling raw rice in the stock until it collapses produces glue, and the Thai fix (aging the rice, rinsing the starch) is the first thing Western versions skip.
  • Oily fish. Salmon in rice soup is a Western substitution Thai sources never make. The dish is built around firm white flesh — grouper, snapper, sea bass, dory — that poaches to a clean slice instead of dissolving and oiling the broth.
  • Stirring the fish. Wongnai's rule is explicit: stir while the fish cooks and the broth picks up a fishy smell. Western recipes often have you "gently stir" the fish in — the opposite of the technique.
  • Dumping everything in one pot. The rice gets its own pot (or is pre-cooked), the fish is blanched separately and lifted out, and the bowl is assembled at the end. One-pot "simmer everything together" recipes skip every texture decision the dish is made of.
  • No tao jeow. The fermented-bean dipping sauce is the flavour engine of the dish for Thai cooks — it is why the plain, gentle bowl works. A Western version that omits it has removed the thing Thai sources argue about most.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Fish: European sea bass and seabream are the closest to Thai ปลากะพง — firm, lean and widely available. Red snapper and halibut work; frozen pangasius/dory fillets are the honest stand-in for ปลาดอรี่ (the dory version). Avoid salmon and mackerel.
  • Rice: Thai Hom Mali (jasmine) from any Thai grocer or the world-food aisle. "Aged rice" in Thailand means last year's harvest — in the UK, a bag that has sat in your cupboard works; so does day-old cooked rice for the cooked-rice method.
  • Tao jeow (เต้าเจี้ยว): yellow bean paste at Thai and Chinese grocers, sold as tao jeow or "yellow bean sauce". In a pinch, Korean doenjang or a mild brown miso loosened with water is closer than omitting it — but say it plainly, it is not the same.
  • Tang chye (ตั้งฉ่าย): Chinese pickled mustard greens, tinned, at any Chinese supermarket.
  • Dried shrimp and dried squid: Thai and Chinese grocers stock both; they are what give the stock its sweetness without sugar.
  • Cilantro roots: UK supermarket coriander is usually sold rootless. Thai grocers sell bunches with roots — otherwise use the thickest stems, crushed.
  • Pandan leaves: frozen at Thai grocers, and they freeze well at home.
