Khao Kan Jin (ข้าวกั๊นจิ๊น)

Cooked rice kneaded with minced pork and fresh pig's blood, pressed into a banana leaf and steamed — the North's dark, savoury answer to the rice ball, eaten warm with fried garlic.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 3 · ~50 minutes

The Rice That Names Its Own Kneading

The name is a two-word recipe. กั๊น (kan), in Kham Muang — the northern dialect — means to knead, press or squeeze, and จิ๊น (jin) is meat. Khao kan jin is literally "rice kneaded with meat": cooked rice worked by hand into minced pork and fresh pig's blood until every grain is dark red, then packed into banana leaves and steamed until the parcel is firm enough to hold in one hand.

It is a Shan dish. The Tai Yai people of the Myanmar borderlands eat steamed jasmine rice — a quirk that sets them apart from their sticky-rice neighbours — and khao kan jin is what they do with it at midday. Northerners call it ข้าวเงี้ยว (khao ngiao, "Shan rice"), after the old Lanna word for the Tai Yai; and they call it จิ๊นส้มเงี้ยว, which hints at the wrapping: like naem, the sour northern sausage, the mixture is bundled in banana leaf and tied. Thai Wikipedia records that it was traditionally a lunch dish, the ข้าวตอน (midday rice) of farmers, and that the original meat was beef — pork took over only when beef fell out of northern kitchens.

Blood does the flavouring and the binding. Every Thai source starts the same way: knead the fresh blood with crushed lemongrass to take the smell off it (the same ดับคาว step used for all blood dishes), then work it into the rice with salt, a little sugar, and — the dish's real seasoning — oil that has fried garlic in it. The result is a parcel that tastes of garlic, iron and rice, with a faint sweetness, sold in northern markets with fried garlic, fried chillies and cucumber alongside.

The accompaniments: fried garlic (กระเทียมเจียว), fried dried bird's-eye chillies (พริกขี้หนูแห้งทอด), sliced shallots or onion (หอมแดงซอย), cucumber (แตงกวา), coriander and spring onion. Toke Tong's Chiang Rai cooks eat the parcels beside khanom jeen nam ngiao and pork rind (แคบหมู) — the full Shan table. The encyclopaedia pot (Thai Wikipedia, citing the สารานุกรมวัฒนธรรมไทย ภาคเหนือ): cooked rice, minced pork, blood, plus boiled pork skin (หนังหมูต้ม), sour tomatoes (มะเขือส้ม), salt, fish sauce and fried-shallot oil (น้ำมันหอมเจียว) — wrapped in two banana leaves, squat like a hor mok, pinned with a wooden skewer. The blood-only pot: both Wikipedia and Toke Tong note versions that skip the minced meat and knead blood into the rice alone.

The Method

  1. The blood. Put the fresh blood in a bowl and knead it with bruised lemongrass stalks, squeezing the grass into the blood for about a minute, then strain. This is the ดับคาว step every source insists on — the lemongrass takes the raw smell off the blood, not with heat but with scent.
  2. The garlic oil. Fry the sliced garlic in the oil until golden and crisp. Strain the oil and keep it — this garlic-scented oil is the dish's real seasoning, mixed through the rice. Leave a spoonful of garlic and oil in the pan.
  3. The pork. Fry the minced pork in that pan until cooked. Wongnai's Kru Yo then adds the blood to the pan over medium heat, stirring just until it begins to clot, before anything touches the rice; the Cookpad and Toke Tong cooks mix everything raw into warm rice and let the steam finish it. Both work — the pre-cooked version gives a firmer, more even parcel.
  4. The kneading — the กั๊น. Warm the cooked rice and add the salt, sugar and garlic oil, then the pork and blood. Knead with your hands until every grain is stained and the mixture is even. This step names the dish; a spoon will not do it.
  5. The wrap. Tear banana leaves into wide sheets and stack them in layers — four for a sturdy parcel, two if you follow the encyclopaedia's hor mok-style bundle. Spoon the mixture into the middle, fold the ends together, roll the leaves tight, fold in the head and tail, and tie with bamboo strip (ตอก) or a toothpick.
  6. The steam. Steam the parcels about 30 minutes until the rice is firm. Open one to check — the mixture should hold together like a dark, dense cake. Serve with fried garlic, fried chillies, sliced shallots and cucumber. Central Thais squeeze lime over it; the northern recipe does not call for lime.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The sugar. One teaspoon (Wongnai's Kru Yo), two tablespoons (Cookpad), three tablespoons (Toke Tong). The encyclopaedia version uses none at all, relying on fish sauce and salt. All agree the sweetness is a whisper, not a glaze — this is a savoury parcel.

The extras. The สารานุกรมวัฒนธรรมไทย recipe — the most traditional on record — adds boiled pork skin, sour tomatoes (มะเขือส้ม) and fried-shallot oil to the rice, where the home-cook recipes use only salt, sugar and garlic oil. The skin gives the parcel its chew; the cooks who leave it out leave the dish a little leaner.

The meat. Minced pork is the modern standard, but the dish was made with beef until beef left northern kitchens (Wikipedia, Toke Tong). Some recipes — recorded by both Wikipedia and Toke Tong — use no minced meat at all, kneading blood into the rice alone.

When the blood meets heat. Wongnai fries the pork and lets the blood half-set in the pan before mixing; Cookpad and Toke Tong mix everything raw into warm rice. The difference is texture in the finished parcel — slightly firmer versus softer and looser.

The wrap. Two banana leaves pinned flat like a hor mok (the encyclopaedia) versus four layers rolled and tied with bamboo strip (Toke Tong, Cookpad). Steaming runs 30 minutes in most recipes; Wongnai's total cook time stretches to 40.

The lime. Wongnai's Kru Yo — a central Thai cook, by her own admission — squeezes lime over the parcel. She notes the traditional northern recipe does not use it. The garnish belongs to the plains, not to Lanna.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Sticky rice. English recipes routinely reach for glutinous rice because it is "northern". It is wrong twice over: khao kan jin is a Shan dish, and the Shan are the one Tai group who steam jasmine rice. Sticky rice turns the parcel into a different, sweeter thing.
  • Cooked, cubed blood. The dish needs fresh, liquid blood kneaded into the rice — it is the binder and the seasoning, not a topping. Black-pudding logic, where blood is a chunky filling, misses the point.
  • Skipping the lemongrass knead. Every Thai source works crushed lemongrass through the blood first. Skipping it leaves a raw, metallic smell that no amount of seasoning hides.
  • Ignoring the garlic oil. The oil that fried the garlic is the main seasoning — western shortcuts that season only with salt and sugar make a grey, bland parcel.
  • Frying or grilling it. The parcels are steamed, not seared; the rice is meant to set firm and dense. Western riffs that pan-fry "for texture" are cooking a different dish.
  • Serving it as a plated main. In the North this is street food — a lunch parcel eaten out of hand with fried garlic, chillies and cucumber, often alongside nam ngiao. Plating it with a sauce misses the point of the thing.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Fresh pig's blood (เลือดหมู): ask a Chinese or Southeast Asian butcher and say you want it liquid for cooking — some add a splash of vinegar to keep it from setting. Frozen tubs of pig's blood at larger Asian supermarkets work too; thaw and use only what you need.
  • Banana leaves (ใบตอง): frozen at Thai and other Southeast Asian grocers, sold year-round; soften over a flame or in hot water before folding. If you truly can't find them, parchment plus foil will steam the parcel, minus the leaf scent.
  • Jasmine rice (ข้าวสวย): any supermarket; cook it slightly on the dry side and let it cool to warm before kneading.
  • Everything else — minced pork, garlic, sugar, salt — is standard UK supermarket fare; fried shallots for the richer encyclopaedia version come from Thai grocers or the world-food aisle.
