Recipe · Northern Thailand · The Wedding Curry That Cooks A Fruit Like Meat

Gaeng Khanun (แกงขนุน)

Young jackfruit simmered with pork and sour tomatoes in a clear, chilli-warm broth, finished with a handful of cha-om and cha-phlu leaves — the north's auspicious curry, cooked for weddings and Songkran.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 4–6 · ~65 minutes

The Curry That Blesses A Wedding

The name does the work: ขนุน (khanun, jackfruit) chimes with หนุน — to support, to prop up. That is why gaeng khanun is wedding food in the north, as Wongnai's recipe explains: the couple eat it as a charm, เกื้อกูลค้ำจุน, that they will hold each other up for life. The Toke-Tong kitchen in Chiang Rai adds the older calendar: Lanna cooks make it on วันปากปี, the fourth day of Songkran, the first proper day of the new year.

The cooking is the other half of the story. Unripe jackfruit, still hard and green, is simmered in the broth until it softens into something almost meat-like — Krua.co describes the texture as เหมือนเนื้อสัตว์, like animal flesh — so the pork is a partner, not the point. And unlike the coconut-cream curries visitors expect from the north, the classic pot is a clear broth: paste dissolved in water, sharpened with sour tomatoes and scented, at the very end, with two leaves no western curry uses.

The paste (น้ำพริกแกง): pound 7 dried long chillies (deseeded, soaked soft) with 1 tsp sea salt, then 1 tbsp finely chopped mature galangal, 25 cloves Thai garlic and 5 shallots; last, work in 1 tbsp chopped pla ra (ปลาร้าสับ) — the fermented fish that gives the broth its depth.

The other pots. Wongnai's quick home pot: 300 g jackfruit, 300 g ribs, a pork stock cube, 50 g tomatoes, a paste of garlic, dried chilli, shallots and a spoon of shrimp paste — no galangal, no pla ra. The vegetarian pot (Krua.co's แกงขนุนอ่อน): no pork, straw mushrooms and vegetarian pla ra in the paste, soy sauce to season. The wedding-feast pot (Krua.co's แกงเป็ดขนุนอ่อน): duck, coconut cream and a fried kua paste — the rich cousin, not the everyday bowl.

The Method

  1. The paste. Pound the dried chillies with the salt until fine, then the galangal, garlic and shallots until smooth, and finish with the pla ra. Krua.co's rule: the fermented fish goes in last, so it binds the paste instead of vanishing into it.
  2. The broth. Stir the paste into the water over medium heat until it dissolves, and bring it to the boil. No frying, no coconut milk — the paste cooks in the water, and that is the whole technique.
  3. The pork. Add the ribs and simmer until tender — soft rib takes only a few minutes; wongnai's pot stews them longer first, so read your butcher. Add the sliced pork and the jackfruit and cook until the jackfruit is soft; the young fruit needs a good half hour to go from wood to meat.
  4. The sour and the leaves. When the jackfruit is soft, add the crushed sour tomatoes, then the cha-phlu and cha-om. The moment the pot returns to the boil, season with fish sauce and cut the heat — the leaves keep cooking in the hot broth.
  5. The serve. Ladle into bowls with steamed or sticky rice. Toke-Tong's kitchen rule: don't clap a lid on the pot straight away; let it stand uncovered a moment or the cha-phlu and cha-om over-cook and the broth goes flat.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The fermented fish. Krua.co's pork recipe builds the paste on pla ra (ปลาร้า); Wongnai's pot uses shrimp paste (กะปิ) instead, and drops the galangal. Both agree the paste is dried chillies, garlic and shallots pounded with a fermented-salt punch — but which one changes the broth from northern-funk to central-Thai-mild.

Coconut milk or none. The classic pot (Krua.co, Wongnai, Toke-Tong) is a clear broth — paste and water, nothing creamy. The duck version fries a kua paste in coconut cream until the oil splits, a proper กะทิแตกมัน curry. Same jackfruit, two different soups; the everyday bowl is the clear one.

The meat. Ribs plus sliced rump (Krua.co), ribs alone with a stock cube (Wongnai), no meat at all in the vegetarian pot (Krua.co), duck in the feast version. Jackfruit is the constant — the pork is whatever the kitchen has.

The tomato's name. Krua.co calls it มะเขือส้ม, the sour tomato; Wongnai calls it มะเขือเทศ. Northern Thai kitchens use both words for the same fruit — the point is that it must be sour enough to sharpen the sweet-tasting broth.

The stock cube. Wongnai's quick recipe dissolves a Knorr pork cube in the water. Krua.co and the Chiang Rai kitchen use plain water and let the paste and bones build the broth. The cube is a weeknight shortcut, not the northern way.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Ripe or canned-sweet jackfruit. The dish is built on young, green jackfruit — hard, white-fleshed, tasteless until it simmers. Canned jackfruit in syrup turns the curry into dessert; brine-packed young jackfruit is the one that works.
  • Coconut milk as default. English-language recipes routinely make it creamy. The northern pot is a clear broth; coconut cream only appears in the duck-feast cousin. Adding coconut milk changes the dish, not improves it.
  • Central red curry paste. A shop-bought red curry paste brings shrimp paste, lemongrass and kaffir lime — none of which are in this paste. The northern one is dried chilli, galangal, garlic, shallot and pla ra, pounded coarse. Wongnai's version is as close to "instant" as the dish gets.
  • Cooking the leaves into mush. Cha-om and cha-phlu go in at the very end, after the jackfruit is soft, and the pot comes off the heat as soon as it boils. Recipes that simmer the greens for twenty minutes are cooking the signature aroma out of the dish.
  • Treating the jackfruit like a vegetable garnish. The jackfruit is the body of the curry — simmered until it eats like meat (Krua.co says it outright: รสสัมผัสคล้ายเนื้อสัตว์). Under-cook it and you serve chips in broth.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Young jackfruit: tins of "young green jackfruit in brine" at Thai and South Asian grocers and online Asian supermarkets — check the label, avoid anything in syrup. Half a kilo drained is about right for the recipe above.
  • Cha-om (ชะอม): sold frozen in bundles at Thai grocers; strip the tiny leaves from the stems. It has a distinctive green smell — there is no western equivalent, so don't substitute; find it or make the dish another day.
  • Cha-phlu (ใบชะพลู, wild betel leaves): fresh at well-stocked Thai grocers in London, Birmingham, Manchester; ask for "cha-plu". Unlike cha-om it is optional — the curry still works without it, but it carries half the aroma.
  • Pla ra (ปลาร้า): jarred fermented fish at Thai grocers; the Wongnai-style swap is a teaspoon of shrimp paste (กะปิ). Both give the broth its northern depth.
  • Dried long chillies: Thai dried chillies from the grocer, or guajillo/puya from a supermarket world-foods aisle — deseed and soak them soft before pounding.
  • Sour tomatoes: UK tomatoes run sweet, so pick the tartest on the shelf (or cherry tomatoes on the vine); the north wants the broth sharpened, not sweetened.
