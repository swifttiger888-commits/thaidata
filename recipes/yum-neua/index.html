Yum Neua (ยำเนื้อย่าง)

Grilled sirloin, rested and sliced against the grain, dressed warm with a pounded lime-fish-chilli paste balanced in the bowl before it ever touches the meat.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 26 September 2026 · Serves 2 · ~25 minutes

The Salad That Ruined a Mother's Reputation

On Pantip, a son with leftover grilled beef asked his mother how to make the dressing. She gave him the family method — a little sugar, warm water, some chillies, lime "until you like it," fish sauce. He followed it, found the result inedible, kept adding lime and fish sauce to fix it, and watched it get sloppier. His verdict, posted for the whole forum: "She cooks many dishes well, but her yum is not good."

The thread is the dish's real recipe: the dressing is the entire skill. The balance — sour-hot-salty with sweet rounding — is fixed in a bowl, tasted, and corrected before the meat goes in. The replies name the three ways to ruin it: lime squeezed into hot liquid turns bitter; lime squeezed hard enough to crush the peel turns bitter; and a broken balance can't be fixed by adding more. Phol Tantasathien, the Top Chef Thailand judge, publishes the same logic as his own recipe — a pounded paste, a balanced dressing, beef dressed while warm.

The Method

  1. The beef. Sirloin rubbed with the fish sauce and pepper for two minutes, grilled to your doneness — and then rested. The rest is not optional: juices stay in the beef, not on the plate.
  2. The paste. Garlic, coriander root, spur chilli and bird's eyes pounded fine, then seasoned with lime juice, fish sauce and coconut sugar.
  3. Balance first. Sour-hot-salty, sweet rounding — tasted and corrected in the bowl before anything else touches it. This is the entire skill; a broken balance can't be fixed later by adding.
  4. Dress the beef, warm. Slice against the grain, still warm, dressing first — warm meat drinks the dressing. The vegetables join after: cucumber, tomato, onion, celery, spring onion.
  5. Finish. A light toss, coriander leaves on top, served immediately. The salad waits for no one — the vegetables give up their crunch within minutes.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The cut. Sirloin is the clean pick; rump, flank and ribeye all appear. The rule is a cut that grills pink and slices clean.

The vegetables. Celery in (Phol) or out; mint in or out; some versions run cucumber-tomato-onion only.

The dressing base. A pounded paste (Phol) against sliced garlic and chillies stirred straight into the lime-fish-sugar. The paste distributes better; the sliced version is brighter.

Sugar. Coconut sugar, palm sugar, white sugar — all standard, in small amounts.

Isaan-style. Some add toasted rice powder, which nudges the dish toward its minced cousin, larb. Without it, the salad stays in the yum family.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Grey beef. Overcooked beef cannot be rescued by dressing. Medium-rare, rested, sliced against the grain — or make something else.
  • Bitter dressing. The three documented ways: lime into hot liquid, lime squeezed hard enough to crush the peel, and lime added at the wrong moment. Cold ingredients, gentle squeeze.
  • The adding spiral. Fixing a broken balance by adding more fish sauce, then more lime, then more sugar — the Pantip author's own path to a sloppy, inedible bowl. Balance it in the bowl, once.
  • Vegetables dressed first. The beef is dressed while warm; the veg joins after. Reversed, the vegetables wilt and the beef stays plain.
  • Skipping the rest. Ungrated, unrested beef leaks juice into the salad and thins the dressing.
  • Bottled "Thai dressing." The dressing is four ingredients pounded and balanced. A bottle cannot be balanced to your limes, your chillies, your beef.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Beef: sirloin or rump from any butcher; the thin "frying steaks" work well and cook fast.
  • Thai garlic: the small-cloved kind in Asian grocers — milder and sweeter than supermarket garlic.
  • Coriander root: coriander bunches from Thai grocers come with roots attached; the root is the paste's anchor. In a pinch, the lower stems.
  • Bird's eye chillies: standard in every UK supermarket now.
  • Fish sauce: any good Thai brand (Squid, Tiparos) — it's half the dressing, so it matters.
  • Coconut sugar: health-food aisles and Asian grocers; palm sugar or a half-measure of soft brown sugar substitutes.
