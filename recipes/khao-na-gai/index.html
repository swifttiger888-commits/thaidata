Khao Na Gai (ข้าวหน้าไก่)

Soy-marinated chicken simmered in stock, finished with a glossy, translucent gravy — Bangkok's Chinatown classic that a hundred-year-old shop still ladles over rice every day.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Serves 2–3 · ~50 minutes

The Gravy That Outlived Its Shop

The name splits neatly: ข้าว (khao) is rice, หน้า (na) is the topping, and ไก่ (gai) is chicken. One plate, one topping, one sauce — but the sauce is the whole point. It is not a Thai curry: there is no coconut milk, no curry paste. The gravy is a Chinese-style stock, seasoned with light soy, sweet dark soy and oyster sauce, and finished with a starch slurry until it turns clear, glossy and just thick enough to cling to the chicken.

The dish belongs to Bangkok's ห้าแยกพลับพลาไชย — the Phlapphla Chai five-way junction in Pom Prap district, the old Chinese quarter. Aroifin traces the first plate to the shop เลี่ยวเลี่ยงเซ้ง near Ratchawong pier, and the recipe that spread from there now carries the nickname ข้าวหน้าไก่ 100 ปี — the hundred-year-old khao na gai — at ร้านพูนเลิศ เหลาะงาทิ้น, still trading at the junction today. Maggi's editors remember the same dish as the one from ร้านสีฟ้า, the blue shop: a Hainanese formula, dark and glossy, served with Chinese sausage and a fried egg on the side.

Every source agrees on the finish. The gravy is thickened at the last minute, stirred fast so the starch never lumps, and cooked only until it goes transparent and glossy. Over the rice go sliced green spur chillies — พริกชี้ฟ้าเขียว — and coriander; the chillies are mild, and their job is to cut the richness of the sauce, not to burn.

The stall batch (Phol Food Mafia, ห้าแยก style): 600 g chicken giblets and 1.4 kg sliced breast for 1.6 litres of stock — the giblets are why his gravy simmers a full 25 minutes. The shop-style pot (Maggi, ราชวงศ์): 250 g cubed thigh with straw mushrooms, spring onion, coriander root and garlic, thickened with Hong Kong tapioca starch and a splash of Chinese cooking wine, served with kunchiang and a fried egg. The pantry pot (Pantip): thigh or breast marinated for an hour with garlic, pepper and coriander root, simmered in mushroom-soaking water and thickened with tapioca starch.

The Method

  1. The marinade. Slice the chicken and massage it with the light soy, sweet dark soy, white pepper and cornflour. The cornflour is doing two jobs — velveting the meat and later helping the sauce grip. Wongnai and Phol rest it 15 minutes; Aroifin marinates overnight. Longer is better if you have the time.
  2. The stir-fry. Heat the vegetable oil and sesame oil in a wok over a medium flame and stir-fry the marinated chicken until just cooked. The sesame oil is not a garnish here; it is half the flavour of the finished gravy.
  3. The stock. Pour in the chicken stock, bring it to the boil, then skim the foam. Both Phol and Wongnai insist on this: a clear stock is what lets the gravy turn translucent later.
  4. The seasoning. Add light soy, seasoning sauce, sugar and oyster sauce, then simmer over a medium heat. Fifteen minutes does it for sliced breast or thigh; the Phol stall version goes a full 25 because the giblets need it. Pantip uses the water from soaking shiitake mushrooms instead of stock — free flavour from the cupboard.
  5. The slurry. Dissolve the cornflour in cold water and drizzle it in while stirring quickly, so it cannot lump. Cook until the gravy turns glossy and translucent and just clings to the spoon. Every source stops here — the gravy should coat, not slump.
  6. The plate. Pile hot jasmine rice, ladle the chicken and gravy over it, and finish with sliced green spur chillies and chopped coriander. Maggi's shop-style touch — a couple of slices of fried kunchiang and a fried egg — turns it into the full ร้านสีฟ้า experience.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The cut of chicken. Aroifin and Maggi say thigh — the famous shops use it because it stays tender through the simmer. Phol's stall recipe (and Wongnai's) use breast, and Phol adds a full load of giblets, noting the original used thigh. The two schools make two gravies: thigh gives richer meat, breast and giblets give a lighter, cleaner plate.

The marinade. Wongnai and Phol rest the chicken 15 minutes; Pantip gives it an hour; Aroifin marinates 5–6 hours, or overnight. Everyone agrees on the same marinade — light soy, sweet dark soy, pepper and starch — and only argues about the clock.

The thickener. Phol and Wongnai use cornflour. Pantip uses tapioca starch, Maggi specifies Hong Kong tapioca starch (แป้งมันฮ่องกง), and Aroifin uses both. Cornflour gives the clearest gloss; tapioca sets firmer and stays glossy longer on a stall counter.

The extras. Maggi stirs in straw mushrooms, spring onion, coriander root and garlic; Pantip adds shiitake; Phol's version runs on giblets; Wongnai and Aroifin keep it to chicken alone. Chinese cooking wine appears in Maggi and in Pantip's "original formula" — Pantip's cook cheerfully admits she had only Thai whisky in the house and left it out.

The accompaniments. Green spur chillies and coriander are universal. Maggi alone adds kunchiang and a fried egg — the house style of the blue shop, not the stall standard.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Reaching for coconut milk or curry paste. There is none. Khao na gai is a Chinese-Thai plate — the sauce is stock, soy, oyster sauce and starch. Add coconut milk and it stops being the dish.
  • Swapping in kecap manis for the sweet dark soy. Same family, wrong viscosity and sweetness. The Thai bottle — ซีอิ๊วดำหวาน, Healthy Boy or similar — is thinner and less sweet, and it is what gives the gravy its dark gloss.
  • Boiling the slurry instead of stirring it in. Dump the starch in at a full boil without stirring and you get gravy with dumplings in it. Every Thai source drizzles the slurry in slowly while stirring, and cooks only until the sauce clears.
  • Skipping the foam skim. Western recipes often simmer straight through. Thai sources skim the scum off the stock before seasoning — skip it and the gravy comes out cloudy instead of translucent.
  • Serving it with bird's-eye chillies. The green chillies on a proper plate are พริกชี้ฟ้า — large, mild, sliced — eaten with the gravy to cut its richness. Small hot chillies would bury the sauce.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Sweet dark soy (ซีอิ๊วดำหวาน): Healthy Boy brand at Thai grocers or online Thai supermarkets. If you must, use kecap manis at half the amount — it is sweeter and thicker.
  • Seasoning sauce (ซอสปรุงรส): Golden Mountain or Maggi seasoning, both widely stocked in Asian supermarkets.
  • Tapioca starch: Thai grocers sell it as แป้งมันสำปะหลัง; cornflour is a perfectly good substitute and is what two of the five sources use.
  • Chicken stock: good shop-bought works; Pantip's shortcut of shiitake-soaking water adds depth for free.
  • Kunchiang (กุนเชียง): Chinese dried sausage, sliced and pan-fried, from any Chinese supermarket — the fried egg and sausage side is optional but traditional in the ราชวงศ์ style.
  • Green spur chillies: look for large mild green chillies (long green or banana chillies) rather than hot bird's-eye.
