Pad Krapow (ผัดกะเพรา)

Thailand's favourite thing to eat, any hour of the day. Real holy basil, maximum-heat wok, and a fried egg with crispy edges and a yolk that runs into the rice.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 26 September 2026 · Serves 1 · ~20 minutes

The Dish Thailand Orders

Forget pad thai and tom yum — the dish Thais actually order most is pad krapow, a one-plate stir-fry of minced meat, garlic, chillies and holy basil over rice with a fried egg. It emerged around 1947, in the era of one-plate-meal promotion: Chinese stir-fry technique married to Thai ingredients. In 2020, the Michelin Guide asked Chef Ton (Thitid Tassanakajohn) — of Baan, sibling restaurant to one-star Le Du — for his recipe, and he handed over the lot, including the two spices he calls his secret: roasted coriander seed and cumin, sprinkled at the end.

The oldest documented recipe is from a cremation memorial cookbook printed in 1968. It has no oyster sauce, no vegetables, no egg — a paste of coriander root, pepper, dried ginger, chillies, garlic and shallots fried in lard. Long beans and baby corn in "pad krapow" are shop filler, added to pad out the meat — and Thailand's half-million-member krapow lovers' club does not approve.

The name is the leaf. กะเพรา is holy basil (Ocimum tenuiflorum) — a different plant from the sweet basil (โหระพา) western recipes quietly substitute. Thai cooks are blunt: if it isn't holy basil, it isn't pad krapow.

The Method

  1. Maximum heat. Wok on, oil in. This dish lives or dies on wok hei.
  2. Pound the aromatics. Chillies and garlic pounded coarse in a mortar — not blended. Pounding releases the essential oils; a blender just mangles them. Fry in the oil until lightly coloured. (If the smell stings your nose, you're doing it right.)
  3. Meat in. Stir-fry until about 75% cooked.
  4. Season. Oyster sauce, soy, palm sugar — then splash the fish sauce against the hot wok edge so it hisses. The heat kills the fishy edge and builds the smoky depth.
  5. Basil last, heat off. Holy basil in, two or three tosses, flame off immediately. The leaves stay green and the aroma stays in the pan — cook it any longer and both are gone.
  6. The egg. Plenty of oil, smoking hot. Egg in, spoon the hot oil over the whites so they crisp into lacy edges while the yolk stays runny.
  7. Plate. Rice, krapow over, egg on top.
  8. Finish. A pinch of roasted coriander seed and ground cumin — Chef Ton's signature. Break the yolk and let it run into the rice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The basil. Holy basil is the dish. Pantip cooks allow sweet basil in an emergency — but insist it's then a different dish. No source pretends otherwise.

The meat. Pork mince with some fat is the street #1 (the fat renders into the chilli-garlic). Chef Ton's personal favourite is dry-aged beef. Chicken is common. All three are authentic.

Oyster sauce. Standard in every modern recipe; absent from the 1968 original, which seasoned with fish sauce and sugar alone.

Vegetables. The 1968 original has none. Long beans, baby corn and onion appear in modern shop versions as filler — the krapow community's pet hate.

The cumin-coriander finish. Chef Ton's signature, revealed to Michelin. Not standard; it's the upgrade.

MSG. In the 1968 original. Modern chef recipes — Chef Ton's included — omit it.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Sweet basil instead of holy basil. The defining error. Thai sweet basil (โหระพา) has a completely different aroma — anise-sweet instead of peppery-clove. If the recipe says "Thai basil," it's probably the wrong plant.
  • Low heat or a crowded pan. Without wok hei the meat steams and the sauce goes flat. This is a two-minute high-heat stir-fry, not a braise.
  • Basil cooked in early. Long-cooked basil goes black, slimy and scentless. In the wok last, heat off, two tosses.
  • Vegetable filler. Peppers, onions, beans, baby corn — dilution. The meat and basil are the dish.
  • Sweet glossy sauce. The balance is salty-savoury with a little sweet, not a sugar glaze.
  • No crispy egg. The lacy-edged, runny-yolk fried egg is not optional garnish — the yolk over hot rice is the finish.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Holy basil is the hard one: Thai grocers stock it (ask for ใบกะเพรา / "holy basil"), and it grows easily from seed on a windowsill. "Thai basil" in UK supermarkets is usually sweet basil — the wrong plant. In an emergency, sweet basil works as a stand-in (Pantip-sanctioned), but it's a different dish.
  • Oyster sauce, fish sauce, palm sugar, bird's eye chillies: any Asian grocer. Palm sugar → light muscovado as fallback.
  • Meat: supermarket pork mince (20% fat ideal) or beef mince (10–15%).
  • Jasmine rice. A carbon-steel wok over your hottest burner beats a non-stick on medium every time.
