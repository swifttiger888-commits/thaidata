Khao Man Som Tum (ข้าวมันส้มตำ)

Coconut-milk rice, an old-style papaya salad and sweet shredded pork — a palace set that names only two of its five parts, and has outlived almost every kitchen that still makes all five.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 3 · ~90 minutes

A Name That Names Only Half The Plate

ข้าวมันส้มตำ is not a rice topped with salad. It is a set (สำรับ): the ข้าวมัน — rice cooked in coconut milk, not the chicken-fat rice of khao man gai, as both tukata001 and Pim stop to explain — the ส้มตำ, and at least a third thing, หมูฝอยหวาน, sweet shredded pork, laid over the rice. The full old set adds a chicken curry and a tamarind nam phrik, with fresh leaves for wrapping.

The set is genuinely old. Pim (ครัวบ้านพิม) places it as palace fare popular since the reign of Rama V, and Krua's food writer traces it to a written source: it appears in ตำรับสายเยาวภา, the cookbook of Princess Yaovabha Bongsanid, daughter of King Chulalongkorn — listed there as papaya salad, coconut rice, sweet shredded meat, tamarind nam phrik and chicken curry, all on one tray.

Krua also relays the historian Krits Leelamai's theory about where the tray came from: the Siamese court absorbed Muslim-Persian dining culture in the Ayutthaya era, and the set sits in the same family as nasi lemak — coconut rice with sambal, fried chicken or fish and curry — and the ghee rice and chicken curry eaten with sour achar. Thai today keeps the sour-sweet snap of the old salad, even if it no longer serves the rest of the tray with it.

Pim's rice (the other main version): 2 cups aged jasmine rice, 1½ bottles Amphawa coconut milk plus 1¼ cups water, 1½ tbsp sugar, ½ tbsp salt — and, if you want the tint, dried butterfly-pea flowers (2 tbsp dried or 1 cup fresh), though the traditional rice is plain milky white. The full set (Krua, from the ตำรับสายเยาวภา cookbook): papaya salad, coconut rice, sweet shredded meat, tamarind nam phrik, chicken curry. The shop box today (Wongnai's review of Sarapii Kitchen): coconut rice, som tum, chicken bamboo-shoot curry, shredded pork, a red-eye nam phrik and fresh vegetables.

The Method

  1. The rice. Rinse aged rice twice and drain. Dissolve the sugar and salt in the coconut milk, then put both in a rice cooker with the rice and add the pandan leaves tied in a knot. Cook as usual — but about ten minutes in, open the lid and turn the rice once: coconut milk is thick, and without a turn it sticks and scorches on the bottom. tukata001 warns the same from a pot, and Krua's writer burned her first electric-cooker batch brown before learning to turn the rice three or four times as it cooked.
  2. The salad. Peel the papaya and cut it into thin strands — fine strands, not the fat julienne of a street stall. Pound the soaked chillies, garlic and white pepper in a mortar, then add the papaya and mix, don't pound. Pim and Krua both stress this: the old salad is คลุก (tossed) so the dressing coats the strands, then finished with pounded dried shrimp and peanuts. Dress with fish sauce, palm sugar and tamarind water and fold in the lime, peel on, cut small.
  3. The pork. Make it ahead if you like. Boil the pork about 45 minutes until tender, let it cool, then pound it until the tendons break and tear it into fine strands. Fry the sliced shallots until golden-crisp and lift them out; fry the shredded pork in the same oil until golden. Simmer fish sauce, salt, both sugars, seasoning sauce and water into a light syrup — sticky, not candy — and toss in the pork and crisp shallots.
  4. The set. Rice in the middle, salad beside it, pork laid in a strip over the rice. Serve with fresh leaves — ชะพลู (betel leaves) or ใบทองหลาง — for wrapping bites. If you want the complete old tray, add the chicken curry and the tamarind nam phrik; without them it is still khao man som tum, just a short set.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The curry. tukata001 pairs the set with แกงเขียวหวานไก่, a green chicken curry. Pim cooks แกงเผ็ดไก่ใส่มะเขือ, a red curry with pea aubergines, and Krua reads the palace cookbook the same way — a red curry with cracked coconut milk. Sarapii Kitchen's box ships แกงไก่หน่อไม้, a chicken bamboo-shoot curry. The curry slot is the most elastic part of the set.

The nam phrik. tukata001 calls it น้ำพริกมะขามสด (fresh tamarind); Pim and Krua specify น้ำพริกส้มมะขาม from soaked tamarind pulp, pounded with roasted shallots, garlic, dried shrimp, shrimp paste and palm sugar; Sarapii's box carries น้ำพริกตาแดง. Three relishes, one slot.

The shredded meat. Krua's cookbook and Pim both record the original as beef — เนื้อฝอยผัดหวาน — and both note that pork is the modern stand-in. tukata001's recipe, like most shops today, is pork.

What the salad is. Pim and Krua are firm: the set's som tum is not today's street som tum. No tomatoes, no long beans, no whole dried shrimp, no lime juice squeezed over — fine papaya strands tossed in a dressing, sprinkled with pounded dried shrimp and lime cut with its peel. tukata001's version is close but keeps the salad of her own table: pounded roasted peanuts and a spoon of white pepper in the mortar, the pepper being the note Kapook's headline makes the signature.

The rice liquid. tukata001 uses two cups of coconut milk and nothing else, and stirs once mid-cook. Pim cuts the coconut milk with water (1½ bottles plus 1¼ cups) and offers butterfly-pea flowers for a pale blue tint, though she notes the traditional rice is plain milky white. Both agree on the essentials: coconut milk, sugar, salt — and never chicken fat.

How many parts make the set. tukata001 serves a short set of three. Pim and Krua list five. Sarapii's box is five plus vegetables. Nobody sells it as rice and salad alone.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Cooking the rice in oil, or in chicken fat. The ข้าวมัน of this set is rice cooked in coconut milk (กะทิ) with sugar, salt and pandan. Western takes that borrow the chicken-fat khao man gai rice, or that fry the rice in oil first, are making a different rice.
  • Swapping in modern som tum. Tomatoes, long beans, whole dried shrimp and a lime-juice punch are today's street salad. The set's salad is fine strands, mixed not pounded, dressed with fish sauce, tamarind and palm sugar, and finished with pounded dried shrimp. Pim says so explicitly, and so does the cookbook Krua consulted.
  • Bottled dressing. One widely shared western version calls for "salad dressing, your choice". The Thai sources build the dressing from fish sauce, tamarind water and palm sugar — nothing out of a bottle.
  • Sweet-soy braised pork instead of หมูฝอย. The Thai pork is boiled, pounded, torn into strands, fried crisp and glazed — a dry, fibrous, lacquered thing. Braised sweet-soy pork slices are a different dish wearing the same colour.
  • Stopping at two parts. Rice plus salad is half a khao man som tum. The pork is not optional garnish, and the full set is five parts. Tukata001's whole thread exists because the dish is rare enough that people no longer know it has more parts.
  • Dessert-sweet rice. The rice should be lightly sweet, lightly salted, rich from coconut — not coconut pudding. If it reads as dessert next to the salad, the sugar balance is wrong.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Coconut milk: a 400 ml tin of full-fat coconut milk stands in for the fresh-pressed กะทิ (2 cups ≈ 400 ml). Frozen fresh coconut milk at Thai grocers if you want to be strict.
  • Aged jasmine rice: any Thai jasmine from the supermarket; "old rice" just means well-dried, long-stored grains, which take up more liquid — standard shop rice behaves the same way.
  • Green papaya: at Thai grocers, whole or pre-shredded bags. Shred fine — the strand size is part of the dish.
  • Dried shrimp (กุ้งแห้ง): Thai and Chinese grocers; pound to a rough powder at home.
  • Palm sugar (น้ำตาลปี๊บ): blocks or tubs at Thai grocers; light brown sugar with a spoon of molasses is the fallback.
  • Seasoning sauce (ซอสปรุงรส): Golden Mountain or Healthy Boy, both standard at Thai grocers.
  • Pandan (ใบเตย): frozen at Thai grocers, keeps for months.
  • หมูฝอย shortcut: jars of Thai pork floss (หมูหยอง) at Thai and Chinese grocers are drier than homemade but sit in the same slot; betel leaves (ชะพลู) for the side are sold fresh at Thai grocers when in season.
