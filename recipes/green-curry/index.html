Thai Green Curry (แกงเขียวหวาน)

The curry Thais eat more than pad thai — and the one western kitchens mangle most. Fresh pounded paste, cracked coconut cream, and the green colour it's named for.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 26 September 2026 · Serves 3–4 · ~1 hour

The Curry Everyone Gets Wrong

Green curry (gaeng keow wan, "sweet green curry") is the dish Thailand itself eats more than pad thai — and the one the internet loves to police. In 2021 a Malaysian internet star's takedown of a British celebrity chef's version went viral with 8 million views: "You only used three green chillies? This isn't a curry, this is a smoothie." The Michelin Guide Thailand documents the incident in its own article on the dish.

The name is a promise: a proper green curry is green — the colour of fresh green chillies pounded into the paste, not of coconut milk tinted pale yellow by a jar. Chef Bee Satongun of Paste (1 Michelin star, Bangkok) learned to pound the paste from her mother as a child: "Back then I didn't yet know how to avoid the chillies splashing into my eyes — green curry chillies are fresh and full of water, so I always wanted to avoid making it." Her rule for a curry that passes: "a good green curry must be green, as its name says."

This page is the from-scratch version: Chef Bee's paste, and the everyday chicken curry that Thai home cooks make with it.

The Method

  1. Pound the paste. All the wet aromatics into the mortar — chillies, lemongrass, galangal, makrut zest, shallots, shrimp paste, garlic, coriander root, red turmeric — pounded very fine.
  2. Roast the dry spices. Peppercorns, coriander seeds, cumin and cardamom in a dry pan until fragrant, pounded to powder, then pounded into the paste. The finer the paste, the smoother the curry — no fibres to chew.
  3. Crack the cream. Coconut cream over medium-low, heated slowly until the oil visibly separates on the surface. This is the flavour base — don't rush it, don't use oil as a shortcut.
  4. Fry the paste in the cracked cream, ~5 minutes, stirring, until deeply fragrant.
  5. Chicken before liquid. Meat in, coated in the paste, and the torn makrut leaves go in now to cut any chicken smell — before any coconut milk.
  6. Milk in, boil, season. Coconut milk, heat up, then palm sugar and fish sauce. Hot, salty, sweet in balance.
  7. Vegetables last, briefly. Only at a full boil — pea aubergines and turkey berries, 2 minutes. Longer and the aubergines go black and mushy.
  8. Basil in, heat off. Sweet basil and red chilli slices, pressed under the surface, served immediately with jasmine rice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Sweet or salty first? One Pantip tradition insists real green curry is sweet-leading with light salt (หวานนำเค็มตาม); another insists salty-leading, sweet following (เค็มนำหวานตาม). Both are authentic — this is genuinely regional taste, and the balance is yours.

The dry spices. Chef Bee's royal-cuisine paste roasts and adds coriander seed, cumin and cardamom. Most home pastes stop at the wet aromatics. The dry layer is the refinement.

Protein. Chicken thighs (everyday), Chef Bee's grilled prawns, beef, fish balls — all standard. Chicken blood cubes are an old-school optional extra.

Red turmeric appears in Chef Bee's paste; most home versions skip it or use a pinch of ordinary turmeric.

Turkey berries: 10–15 (Chef Bee), a handful, or half a cup (home cooks) — to taste.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Jar paste + three green chillies. The "this is a smoothie" problem, as immortalised by an 8-million-view viral takedown. A jar of paste is a start, but it is not the recipe.
  • Pale yellow colour. If the curry isn't green, the green chillies were missing. The dish is named for its colour.
  • No cracked coconut cream. Western versions pour everything in at once. The cracked cream is where the paste's aromatics bloom.
  • Broccoli instead of pea aubergines and turkey berries. The defining vegetable swap — pea aubergines are small, crunchy and bitter-sweet; broccoli is a different dish.
  • Cream added for "richness." The richness comes from the cracked coconut cream and the oil the paste releases. Adding dairy cream is a fix for a curry that was never properly started.
  • White sugar. Palm sugar's round, floral sweetness is part of the dish; granulated sugar is sharp and thin.
  • Basil cooked in early — it goes black and scentless. Last, pressed under, heat off.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Green bird's eye chillies and Thai sweet basil: Asian grocers. UK "Thai basil" labelling is unreliable — for this dish you want sweet basil (โหระพา), the anise-scented one, not holy basil.
  • Pea aubergines and turkey berries: Thai grocers (fresh, sometimes frozen). Quartered and salted water prevents blackening.
  • Makrut lime leaves and zest, shrimp paste, palm sugar: Asian grocers. Palm sugar → light muscovado as fallback. Red turmeric → ordinary fresh turmeric or a pinch of powder.
  • The paste freezes well — make a double batch, portion and freeze, and the curry becomes a 20-minute weeknight dish.
  • Chicken thighs over breast — the long simmer stays tender; breast goes dry.
