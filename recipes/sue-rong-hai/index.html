Recipe · Isaan · The Grilled Brisket That Makes The Tiger Cry

Suea Rong Hai (เสือร้องไห้)

Marbled beef brisket grilled until the fat weeps between the layers of meat, sliced thin and eaten hot with jaew — tamarind, fish sauce, toasted rice — sticky rice and a fistful of herbs.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 2–3 · ~45 minutes, plus 3 hours marinating

The Cut That Weeps

เสือร้องไห้ is both a dish and a cut of beef. The cut is the marbled brisket — เนื้อวัวส่วนอก, what butchers call the tiger-cry cut — where ribbons of fat run between layers of lean meat. Grill it and the fat melts and drips; Pholfoodmafia puts the name's origin story plainly: the sight of fat dripping between the layers of succulent meat will leave the tiger weeping. Every restaurant in Isaan that serves grilled beef has it, and the whole dish rests on two things: the cut, and the jaew.

Celebrity chef Pam (พิชญา อุทารธรรม), ambassador for True Aussie Beef, argues the cut carries the dish alone: brisket has a deeper beef flavour than cuts that need long cooking like neck, and it suits patient, low heat. Her rule is the opposite of steak cookery — with good brisket, salt and pepper are the whole marinade, and the work is in the resting and the slow fire. Most home cooks, as the TrueID and Wongnai recipes show, do give the brisket a marinade — but nobody treats the meat as the flavour-carrier of a heavy sauce. The meat speaks, and the jaew answers.

The jaew is the other half of the dish's soul: fish sauce, tamarind, lime, dried chilli and — never optional — ข้าวคั่ว, raw rice dry-toasted and pounded to a gritty powder that clings to the meat. PTKss, a Thai food-trade supplier whose blog teaches the sauce in two versions, puts it the way Isaan does: แม้แต่ราชสีห์ยังร้องว้าว — even a lion would say wow.

The other pots. Wongnai's version (กิน&GO, 500 g brisket) marinates in 150 g evaporated milk, 30 g sweetened condensed milk and 3 tbsp fish sauce for half an hour, then grills over low heat — the condensed-milk trick giving the surface its sweet caramel edge. Chef Pam's pot is the purist one: 500 g of good brisket, half a teaspoon of salt and black pepper, pan-grilled 3–4 minutes a side, rested 2 minutes — with a jaew of 2 tbsp fish sauce, 1 tsp each of lime juice, tamarind and rice powder. ครัวพิศพิไล's pot (via TrueID) works a baking-soda marinade — garlic, pepper, half a teaspoon of soda, oyster sauce, seasoning sauce and light soy, rested an hour — for the tenderest home version on a pan or charcoal.

The Method

  1. The paste. Pound the coriander roots, skin-on garlic and white peppercorns to a paste, then work in the palm sugar, oyster sauce, fish sauce and rice bran oil. This is the old Thai grill marinade: roots and garlic, not bottles. Massage it into the brisket and refrigerate 3 hours to overnight — the long soak is the point of brisket.
  2. The warm-up. Bring the meat to room temperature before it meets the fire. Cold brisket grills unevenly, and brisket already needs patience.
  3. The jaew. Stir the palm sugar into the tamarind paste, fish sauce and lime juice until it dissolves, then stir in the chilli flakes, toasted rice, sliced shallots, kaffir lime leaves and spring onion. The rice powder thickens as it sits — make the sauce first and let it settle.
  4. The grill. Over low-to-medium heat — charcoal if you have it, ครัวพิศพิไล says it's worth lighting — until the fat renders and the meat is cooked through but still juicy. Thai sources agree on the patient fire; none of them sear brisket like a steak.
  5. The rest and the slice. Rest the meat a few minutes so the juices settle, then slice thin, against the grain. Sliced with the grain, brisket becomes chewing practice.
  6. The table. Serve hot — TrueID insists the dish must be eaten hot — with the jaew for dipping, sticky rice, and the full herb-and-veg plate: dill, Thai basil, pennywort, cucumber and snake beans.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The marinade — four schools. Chef Pam salts and peppers only, on the logic that good beef should taste of beef. Pholfoodmafia's chef uses the pounded coriander-root, garlic and white-pepper paste. ครัวพิศพิไล marinates in soy, oyster sauce and a pinch of baking soda — soda being the home cook's tenderiser. Wongnai, the mookata-style shortcut, soaks the beef in evaporated and sweetened condensed milk with fish sauce, so the sugars caramelise on the grill. All four still call the same dish เสือร้องไห้ — the marinade is negotiable, the cut is not.

How done is done. Wongnai and ครัวพิศพิไล grill the brisket over low heat until well cooked — brisket is tough, and the Thai answer is a patient fire. Pholfoodmafia's English recipe says "to your desired doneness", and Chef Pam pan-grills her brisket just 3–4 minutes a side, resting it before slicing. The split is really about how thin the brisket is cut before it hits the pan; the shared rule is rest the meat and slice it thin.

Cooked jaew vs raw jaew. Wongnai is the outlier: it simmers the fish sauce, tamarind water and palm sugar together until the sugar dissolves, then cools the pot before adding lime, chilli and rice powder. Every other source mixes the sauce cold. The cooked base keeps longer and rounds the tamarind; the raw sauce is brighter and sharper.

The sour. Most jaew recipes split the sour between tamarind and lime — Pholfoodmafia, Chef Pam and PTKss all use both. Wongnai goes almost entirely tamarind: 100 g of tamarind water against a spoonful of lime. TrueID's ครัวพิศพิไล version drops tamarind altogether and sours with lime alone. The tamarind-led sauce is the older Isaan form; lime-only is the lighter Bangkok-style reading.

What gets pounded. PTKss pounds fresh bird's-eye chillies, garlic, shallots and coriander root into the sauce — a proper nam jim. Pholfoodmafia keeps everything sliced or stirred — chilli flakes, not fresh chillies — and is alone in adding kaffir lime leaves. Wongnai scatters its sliced shallots and herbs on top of the sauce as a finish rather than stirring them through.

The sugar. Palm sugar in Pholfoodmafia's, Wongnai's and PTKss's second jaew; plain white sugar in ครัวพิศพิไล's and PTKss's first. PTKss's first recipe is explicit that the sweetness is optional: if you don't want it sweet, leave the sugar out — jaew leads with sour and heat, not sugar.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • The wrong cut, cooked like a steak. English-language "crying tiger" recipes default to sirloin or flank seared to medium-rare. That is a fine steak with a Thai sauce — it is not the dish. Every Thai source here uses brisket, the marbled tiger-cry cut, and cooks it through over patient heat. Rare steak was never the point; melting fat was.
  • Dropping the toasted rice. ข้าวคั่ว is the one ingredient every Thai source agrees on, and the one western recipes most often skip. It is not a garnish: the gritty roasted-rice powder thickens the jaew and carries the smoky-nutty note that makes the sauce Isaan rather than merely spicy.
  • Sweet chilli sauce by another name. The western habit is to round the jaew into a sweet, glossy dip. Thai versions lead with sour and salt — PTKss even says the sugar is optional. A jaew you'd drizzle on spring rolls is a jaew nobody in Isaan would recognise.
  • Lime-only, no tamarind. Western recipes sour the sauce with lime and stop there. Most Thai sources pair lime with tamarind, and Wongnai's is mostly tamarind — the deeper, fruitier sour that stands up to grilled beef fat.
  • Marinades that bury the beef. Long soaks in heavy soy-and-brown-sugar marinades make every steak taste the same. The Thai spread runs the other way — from salt alone (Chef Pam) to a simple root paste — because the brisket is the flavour and the jaew is the seasoning.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Brisket: a butcher's flat-cut brisket, untrimmed and marbled, is the one you want; rolled brisket works for a slower roast-style cook. For the quick pan version, the thin-sliced brisket sold at Asian supermarkets for hot-pot and Korean grilling is ideal.
  • Tamarind (น้ำมะขามเปียก): block tamarind at Thai grocers, soaked and sieved, or jarred tamarind paste/concentrate — the paste from Thai grocers is milder than Indian-style concentrate, which needs diluting.
  • Toasted rice powder (ข้าวคั่ว): dry-toast sticky or jasmine rice in a pan until deep golden, pound to a gritty powder. Ready-made packets (Lobo and similar) sit next to the larb kits in Thai grocers.
  • Chilli flakes: Thai พริกป่น — flakes of dried chilli with the seeds, coarser and hotter than UK supermarket flakes. Asian grocers stock it; don't substitute sweet paprika-heavy blends.
  • Coriander roots and Thai garlic: roots come on the bunches at Thai grocers (most UK supermarkets sell coriander rootless); Thai garlic — small cloves, thin skin — is the one used here, skin and all, in the paste.
  • Serving plate: sticky rice (soak and steam), and the fresh herbs — dill, Thai basil, pennywort (ใบใบบัวบก, at Thai grocers), cucumber and snake beans.
