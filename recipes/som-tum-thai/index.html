Som Tum Thai (ส้มตำไทย)

The green papaya salad done properly — mortar-pounded, four-flavour balanced, from a Michelin-recommended som tum chef. "Papaya salad" doesn't begin to cover it.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 26 September 2026 · Serves 1 (doubles easily) · ~15 minutes

The Som Tum Family

"Som tum" is a whole category, not one salad. The Michelin Guide Thailand divides it like this: tam thai — the classic, with roasted peanuts and dried shrimp; tam lao / tam pla ra — the Isaan version with fermented fish; tam pu — with salted field crab; tam pu pla ra — both crab and fish; tam korat — tam thai with pla ra added. This page is tam thai: the version without the funk.

The dish grew out of the Isaan word tam som — "pounding something sour." Isaan cooks have long pounded whatever sour fruit or vegetable was in season; papaya, a Central American import grown in Siam since before King Narai's reign, became the standard over time and spread across Thailand with Isaan migration. An early Thai cookbook from 1908 records a tamarind-based cousin called pu tam, but not som tam itself — the papaya version is younger than most people think.

This recipe comes from Chef Keng (เก่ง-จิรเดช เนตรวงค์) of Tum Kratoei Sagate (ตำกระเทยสาเกต) — a som tum institution that started in Sakat Nakhon, Roi Et province, and now runs 10 branches. His words on why tam thai endures: "Many people don't like the nua funk of som tum lao — that's why tam thai remains the timeless hit for people who don't eat pla ra."

The Recipe (for 1 — double everything to share)

น้ำตาลปี๊บPalm sugar1 tbsp
น้ำตาลทรายแดงBrown sugar½ tbsp
น้ำปลาFish sauce2 tbsp
มะนาวฝานLime segments4
เส้นมะละกอGreen papaya strips80 g
แครอทซอยJulienned carrot10 g
กุ้งแห้งเนื้อDried shrimp, meaty kind15 g

The Method

  1. Pound the aromatics. Chillies and garlic into the mortar, pounded just rough.
  2. Break the peanuts. Roasted peanuts in, pounded just until broken — this thickens the dressing and gives it the nutty perfume.
  3. Build the dressing. Long beans, tomatoes, palm sugar, brown sugar, fish sauce, lime juice. Mix until combined.
  4. Taste and adjust. The balance is sour, sweet, salty, hot — in that order of importance.
  5. Papaya last. Strips in, then carrot. Pound lightly, mix well — the papaya should soften slightly but stay crisp.
  6. Plate. Top with the dried shrimp and peanuts. Serve immediately, sticky rice on the side.

Order matters. The chillies and garlic go in first because the pestle bruises them into the mortar's surface, releasing oils the dressing builds on. The papaya goes in last because it should bruise, not break.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Chillies: jinda (milder, fruitier — Michelin chef) vs bird's eye (hotter — Kapook and most street stalls).

Sugar: palm sugar + brown sugar for layered sweetness (Michelin) vs palm sugar alone (most home recipes).

Peanuts and shrimp: pounded into the dressing so it clouds and thickens (Chef Tuck's technique) vs kept as a topping (Michelin plating). Both are authentic.

Carrot: optional colour and crunch — purists skip it.

Papaya: hand-chopped with a knife vs grated. Pantip home cooks are firm: chopping is an art, and chopped papaya eats better than grated.

MSG: standard in street stalls; chef recipes skip it. Chef Tuck publishes an explicitly MSG-free technique.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • No mortar. Tossing shredded papaya in dressing makes a salad. The pounding — chillies and garlic bruised into the stone, fibres softened, flavours driven in — is the technique the dish is named after (tam = to pound).
  • Sugar-forward. Western versions drift sweet. Tam thai is a four-way balance: sour, sweet, salty, hot. The sour (lime) and salty (fish sauce) are load-bearing.
  • Skipping dried shrimp and peanuts. The Michelin taxonomy defines tam thai by these two ingredients. Without them it's not tam thai.
  • Ripe or substituted fruit. Green, unripe papaya is the point — crisp, neutral, a vehicle for the dressing. Mango or ripe papaya makes a different dish.
  • Served solo. In Thailand it comes with sticky rice (and usually grilled meat) as a meal. The rice is what the dressing-soaked plate is for.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Green (unripe) papaya: Asian supermarkets — whole, or pre-shredded packs in the fridge section.
  • Dried shrimp: Asian grocers; buy the meaty kind if you can. Fine shrimp works, just less nua.
  • Sida tomatoes → ripe plum or roma tomatoes. Jinda chillies are scarce in the UK — bird's eye chillies are an honest swap (Thai sources disagree anyway).
  • Palm sugar → light muscovado as fallback. Thai garlic: small-cloved, stronger bulbs.
  • Equipment matters: a granite or stone mortar and pestle, not a blender. A blender purées; the recipe needs bruising.
  • Sticky rice: glutinous rice from Asian grocers, steamed — not boiled.
