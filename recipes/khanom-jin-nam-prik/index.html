Khanom Jin Nam Phrik (ขนมจีนน้ำพริก)

Fermented rice noodles under an old-style prawn nam phrik — pounded prawns, mung beans and coconut, sweet-sour-salty and finished with a sizzle of chilli oil, eaten with a plate of raw and barely blanched vegetables.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Serves 5–6 · ~50 minutes

The Noodles That Wear The Sauce

The name splits cleanly: ขนมจีน (khanom jin) — the skeins of fermented rice noodles, made by pushing a rice-flour dough through a sieve into boiling water — and น้ำพริก, the chilli-pounded sauce poured over them. Krua.co's food story puts the dish in its place: khanom jeen is eaten in every region of Thailand, and the sauce is the star that tells you where you are. This one is the old-style prawn nam phrik, what Krua.co calls a ตำรับโบราณ — a recipe handed down, not invented this decade. Wongnai's home cook writes the same word under hers: อาหารไทยโบราณ, ancient Thai food.

The balance rule every source states the same way: หวานนำ เปรี้ยวตาม — sweet leads, sour follows, and only a whisper of salt. Pim, whose post is literally titled "an inherited recipe" (สูตรมรดกตกทอด), makes the point that the sweetness is palm sugar dissolved first, the sour is fresh lime and makrut juice squeezed at the last minute, and the coconut must be rich enough to coat without turning to cream.

And half the dish is the vegetables — ผักเหมือด, the side plate of four or five kinds eaten between mouthfuls of noodles: Pim lists Thai morning glory, water mimosa, winged beans, leadtree tips and leadtree pods, raw or barely blanched. That crunch against the sweet-sour sauce is the whole rhythm of the meal.

The south eats it too, its own way. Mahidol's southern cookbook documents ขนมจีนน้ำพริกน้ำยาปักษ์ใต้ — a southern khanom jeen table set with both a nam phrik and the fish-curry nam ya — and its nam phrik swaps the beans for a shrimp-paste base and a float of lime. Further south still, Aroifin's Phuket-style น้ำพริกกุ้งเสียบ folds whole smoked sun-dried shrimp into a dry pounded dip, no coconut at all, served with the same plate of vegetables.

The Wongnai pot (serves 5–10): 500 g prawns — half pounded into the sauce, half boiled and served on top — plus 200 g dried shrimp, 500 g toddy-palm sugar, 3 cups concentrated tamarind, 1 kg fresh coconut milk, 200 g ground roasted peanuts and 300 g boiled mung beans, crowned with chilli-oil colouring, fried shallots and a makrut lime floated on only after the sauce has gone completely cold. Pim's inherited pot: coconut milk pressed from 2 kg of grated coconut, mung beans and peanuts pounded with boiled prawns, a roasted paste of shallots, garlic and coriander root, salted and sweetened with pre-melted palm sugar, soured with fresh lime and makrut juice plus tamarind — she keeps both, the two acids together taste rounder. The southern pot (Mahidol): seven soaked dried chillies pounded and fried with shrimp paste, loosened with a bowl of coconut milk, 300 g chopped prawns, crushed peanuts, palm sugar and tamarind, half a lime dropped in for the aroma.

The Method

  1. The beans. Boil the mung beans until they begin to puff, then rub the skins off and pound them with the roasted shallots, roasted garlic and roasted galangal. Roasted, not raw — the char is what makes the sauce smell old-fashioned.
  2. The prawns. Boil the prawns briefly in the thin coconut milk, lift them out and pound them into the bean mixture. They are the body of the sauce, not a topping — except in the Wongnai pot, which keeps half back to serve whole on top.
  3. The sauce. Cook the pounded mixture in the coconut milk over medium heat, stirring, then add the coconut cream and drop in the halved makrut lime. Season with fish sauce, palm sugar and tamarind water: sweet leads, sour follows, only lightly salted. Taste, adjust, and take it off the heat — this sauce is assembled, not stewed.
  4. The sizzle. Fry the sliced shallots until golden, then — Pim is explicit — switch the fire off and add the dried bird's eye chillies to the hot oil. The chillies perfume the oil without burning, and the whole lot, oil and all, goes hissing into the sauce. That red sheen is the finish, not the recipe's heat engine.
  5. The serve. Twirl the khanom jeen onto a plate, pour the nam phrik over or serve it in a separate bowl, and set out the vegetables — banana blossom, blanched morning glory, bitter gourd, fried chillies. Eat a mouthful of noodles, a mouthful of vegetables, repeat.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The protein. Fresh prawns pounded into the sauce in every source, but Wongnai also works in 200 g of dried shrimp, and Mahidol's southern pot replaces them with a shrimp-paste (กะปิ) base. Aroifin's Phuket-style dip is a different creature again: whole smoked sun-dried shrimp (กุ้งเสียบ) folded in, never pounded fine.

The sour. Tamarind water everywhere, but Pim insists on fresh lime and makrut juice squeezed in alongside it — lime alone works, the two acids together are rounder. Mahidol drops half a lime into the simmering pot. Wongnai uses tamarind concentrate only, three cups of it.

The sweetener. Palm sugar in Cheewajit, Pim and the southern pot — but Wongnai's classic uses half a kilo of toddy-palm sugar (น้ำตาลโตนด), the darker, smokier southern-style sugar, which pushes the sauce markedly sweeter.

The body. Mung beans are the constant. Pim adds peanuts pounded with the beans; Wongnai adds 200 g of ground roasted peanuts on top of 300 g of beans; Mahidol's southern version drops the beans entirely and thickens with only 3 tablespoons of crushed peanuts and the shrimp paste itself.

The makrut lime. Cheewajit simmers a halved makrut in the sauce until seasoned, then the heat goes off. Wongnai floats the makrut halves on the finished sauce — and warns the sauce must be completely cold first, or the lime turns it bitter. Pim skips the fruit and squeezes makrut juice fresh into the mix.

The region. Krua.co frames the dish pan-regionally — every region eats khanom jeen, the sauce is the regional signature — and calls the prawn nam phrik an old-style central Thai recipe. Mahidol documents the southern table, where the nam phrik shares the plate with nam ya fish curry, and Aroifin's southern dip has no coconut milk at all. Same noodles, three sauces.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Treating it as a curry. There is no curry paste here — the sauce is pounded prawns, beans and coconut, seasoned sweet-sour-salty. A western version that starts from a red curry paste has made khanom jeen nam ya, not nam phrik.
  • Calling it peanut sauce. Ground peanuts and mung beans thicken and round the prawn sauce; they are not the point. Double the peanuts and you have satay, not this.
  • Fixing the balance. Western palates want savoury first; this dish wants sweet first, sour right behind, lightly salted. "Correcting" it into a savoury-lead sauce loses the dish's entire shape — every Thai source states the order.
  • Long-cooking the prawns. The prawns are boiled briefly, pounded, and returned to a sauce that is assembled in minutes. Simmer it like a ragu and the prawns go mealy and the makrut goes bitter.
  • Skipping the chilli-oil finish. The sizzled dried chillies poured over at the end are the colour and the aroma — and Pim's fire-off trick is what keeps them fragrant instead of acrid. Finishing with a spoon of shop-bought chilli oil is not the same thing.
  • Cooking the vegetables. The side plate is raw or barely blanched on purpose — the crunch against the soft noodles and rich sauce is the eating rhythm of the dish. Stir-frying the ผักเหมือด into the sauce collapses the texture the meal is built on.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Khanom jeen noodles (เส้นขนมจีน): fresh skeins at Thai grocers, usually weekend stock; dried packets are fine — soak and drain per the packet.
  • Prawns: small raw prawns from any supermarket fish counter; the smaller the better here, since they are pounded.
  • Mung beans (ถั่วเขียว): whole green mung beans from supermarket dry-goods aisles or South Asian grocers; split yellow mung cooks faster and works too.
  • Makrut lime (มะกรูด): the bumpy Thai lime at Thai grocers; if you cannot find one, simmer a torn kaffir lime leaf in the sauce instead and use fresh lime juice for the sour.
  • Dried bird's eye chillies, palm sugar, tamarind pulp: standard Thai-grocer stock — buy the palm sugar in a tub, it keeps for months.
  • Smoked shrimp (กุ้งเสียบ) for the southern version: Thai grocers sell it flat-packed; check the smell — Aroifin warns rancid shrimp ruins the dip.
  • Side vegetables: winged beans (ถั่วพู) at Thai grocers, morning glory at Asian grocers; banana blossom is rare in the UK — a little raw white cabbage or beansprouts carries the crunch.
