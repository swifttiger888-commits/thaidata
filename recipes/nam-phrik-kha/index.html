Nam Phrik Kha (น้ำพริกข่า)

The north's galangal dip — roasted rhizome and dried chillies pounded fine, dry-fried into a hot, herbal paste that keeps for a month.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · one jar · ~30 minutes

The Dip Built on Galangal

น้ำพริกข่า puts galangal where most Thai dips put fish or shrimp — as the body of the paste itself. Mature galangal, woody and fragrant, is sliced thin and dry-roasted in a pan over low heat until dry and aromatic, then pounded fine with roasted dried chillies, garlic and shallots. The finished paste is fried again until dry, which is what lets it sit in a jar for a month — longer refrigerated, as Kapook notes.

It is a northern Thai dip — Aroifin labels it สไตล์ล้านนา, Lanna style — and the classic pairings are northern too: Aroifin serves it with a plate of raw vegetables; TrueID's batch-cook version goes with หมูทอดแดดเดียว, sun-dried fried pork, and rice; another TrueID ratio recipe pairs it with จิ้นนิ่ง, steamed pork or beef, "the classic northern pairing". KRUA.CO's version, unique among the sources, adds lemongrass and ma-khwaen — the northern prickly ash — and serves it with boiled vegetables and boiled pig's ear.

The heat is deliberate. TrueID's batch recipe specifies large dried chillies, not bird's-eye — the burn should be middling and rounded, not sharp — and warns the paste is strong: a little goes a long way. Wongnai's น้องแปะก๊วย pairs it with steamed earthstar mushrooms, the seasonal northern delicacy. Some versions carry meat — minced chicken stir-fried in (Aroifin), minced pork (TrueID, Kapook's note) — and the meatless base stands on its own.

The pork version (TrueID): 30 large dried chillies, 3–5 heads mature galangal, 5–10 heads garlic, 1 tbsp salt, 2 tsp sugar, 2 tbsp MSG and 300 g minced pork — everything roasted, pounded, then fried in oil with the pork until cooked; made in batches that keep for weeks. The ratio recipe (TrueID): galangal, salt, shallots, dried chillies and garlic 1:1, shrimp paste half that — roasted, pounded, served with steamed pork. The lemongrass version (KRUA.CO): 5 large grilled dried chillies, 3 pieces mature galangal, 15 cloves garlic, 1 stalk lemongrass, 1 tsp ma-khwaen, ½ tsp sea salt.

The Method

  1. The fry. Fry the garlic and shallots in oil until golden and fragrant, then lift out and set aside. Kapook is the only source that deep-fries before pounding; the other versions dry-roast everything.
  2. The roast. Dry-roast the galangal in a pan over low heat until dry and fragrant — this drives out the raw heat and concentrates the herb.
  3. The pound. Pound the galangal fine, then add the dried chillies, then the fried garlic and shallots, season with salt and pound everything together. Wongnai's trick: salt goes in with the chillies first, because it makes them easier to crush.
  4. The second fry. Heat oil in the pan over low heat and fry the paste until dry. This second cooking is what makes the paste keep — Aroifin's version roasts everything, pounds it, and dry-fries the paste again.
  5. The serve. Taste and adjust, then serve with raw or boiled vegetables, steamed pork, or sun-dried fried pork and rice. A little goes a long way.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The meat. None (Kapook's base, KRUA.CO's vegetarian paste, Wongnai's mushroom version), minced chicken stir-fried in before pounding (Aroifin), or minced pork fried into the paste (TrueID's batch recipe). All agree the meat is optional.

The roasting. Everything dry-roasted before pounding (Aroifin, TrueID, Wongnai), garlic and shallots deep-fried in oil instead (Kapook), or only the chillies grilled with the galangal left raw — the paste is dry-fried afterwards instead (KRUA.CO).

The extras. Lemongrass and ma-khwaen prickly ash (KRUA.CO alone), shrimp paste at half ratio (one TrueID recipe — "she adds it for the aroma", and notes some recipes skip it), sugar and MSG (TrueID's batch version), seasoning powder (Kapook, optional).

The double fry. Aroifin dry-fries the finished paste a second time; TrueID's batch version fries it with the pork. Kapook and KRUA.CO fry once. Every version that aims for shelf life fries the paste dry at the end.

The serve. Raw vegetables (Aroifin), boiled or steamed vegetables with boiled pig's ear (KRUA.CO), steamed earthstar mushrooms (Wongnai), sun-dried fried pork and rice (TrueID), steamed pork or beef จิ้นนิ่ง (the other TrueID).

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Fresh galangal straight into the paste. The roast is the point — raw galangal pounded in stays woody, sharp and damp. The herb must be dried in the pan until fragrant first.
  • Bird's-eye chillies. TrueID is explicit: large dried chillies, so the heat is middling and rounded, not the sharp stab of bird's-eye. The dip should be hot but herbal, not searing.
  • Skipping the final fry. The paste fried until dry is what makes it keep for a month. Left moist and raw, it sours within days.
  • Serving it as a curry paste. Nam phrik kha is a finished dip, served with vegetables and steamed or fried pork — not a base to be fried off and built into a curry.
  • Over-serving. Every source repeats the warning: the paste is strong, a little goes a long way. It is a condiment, not a side dish.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Galangal: fresh in Thai and East Asian grocers, increasingly in UK supermarkets. Buy mature (woody) galangal for this dip — young galangal is milder but works, per Aroifin.
  • Large dried chillies (พริกแห้งเม็ดใหญ่): Thai grocers; the wide, mild-to-medium ones, not bird's-eye.
  • Ma-khwaen (northern prickly ash): at Thai grocers or online — skip it and the dip loses a northern note but stays authentic.
  • Sun-dried pork (หมูแดดเดียว) and จิ้นนิ่ง steamed pork: make at home or serve with any steamed pork, or keep the dip vegetarian with vegetables alone.
  • The paste keeps a month in a jar, longer refrigerated — scale the batch up if the galangal is plentiful.
