Pad See Ew (ผัดซีอิ๊ว)

Fresh wide rice noodles charred in a very hot wok, dark soy caramelised on the metal, pork marinated in ice water, kale at the end. The burnt edges are the point.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 26 September 2026 · Serves 2 · ~25 minutes + marinating

The Dish That Looks Burnt

The name is Cantonese, literally "stir-fried with soy sauce" — a dish Thailand borrowed along with the wok. Its Malaysian and Singaporean cousin is char kway teow; its ancestor is chow fun. In Thailand it lives in every street kitchen and every "made-to-order" shop, always next to its sibling, rad na.

San Yod — the Bib Gourmand noodle institution with ten-plus branches across Bangkok — calls it one of the chef-killer dishes: "Both the noodles and the egg must carry the wok's aroma, with good marinated pork and quality kale — cooked plate by plate on a high flame in an iron wok." And then the line that explains the dish: the noodles may look burnt. That's the secret — dark soy hits hot metal, caramelises, and turns the whole plate fragrant.

The Method

  1. Marinate. Pork, sugar, light soy, oyster sauce, sesame oil, pepper and the ice-cold water, mixed and rested 1 hour — or overnight in the fridge for silkier pork. A pinch of baking powder pushes it to restaurant softness.
  2. Coat the noodles. Tease the fresh rice noodles into individual strands and toss with the dark soy. This is what colours them and what caramelises on the metal.
  3. Garlic first. Wok over a high flame, oil in, garlic in — golden and fragrant before anything else.
  4. Pork, then egg. Pork to about 80% done, then the eggs cracked straight in and scrambled until just set.
  5. Noodles and season. Sugar, light soy, seasoning sauce, oyster sauce, pepper. Stir hard, heat high, until the noodles smell toasted and the edges catch. They should look slightly burnt — that's the dish working.
  6. Kale last. Stems first if you want them softer, leaves a moment later, tossed just to wilt. A splash of stock only if the wok dries out.
  7. Serve now. Pickled chillies in vinegar, chilli flakes, sugar and a lime wedge on the side — the four table condiments, for each mouthful to taste.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Order of play. San Yod scrambles the egg first, then the noodles, then folds in the blanched pork. Pim's home order: garlic, pork, egg, noodles. Both end at the same place.

Kale. The restaurant blanches it separately; home cooks stir-fry it in last. Stems first is the compromise.

Tenderising. Pim's ice-water trick vs the baking-powder route used by rad-na shops — she gives both.

Balance. Pim cooks salt-leaning with a sweet finish; some shops run sweeter. The table condiments exist because everyone adjusts anyway.

The char. San Yod pushes the burnt edge as the signature; home woks chase a lighter version of the same idea.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Thin noodles. Pad see ew is made with fresh wide rice noodles (sen yai). Dried pad-thai-style sticks are a different dish.
  • No char. Grey steamed noodles mean the wok was never hot enough. The burnt edges are the signature, not a mistake.
  • Missing oyster sauce. Soy alone doesn't build the glaze — oyster sauce and the seasoning sauce are half the flavour.
  • Filler vegetables. Peppers, carrots and bean sprouts are pad-thai thinking. Kale is the vegetable; it goes in last.
  • Unmarinated pork. Dry, hard pork is the tell of a skipped marinade. The ice-water step matters.
  • Dark soy as a drizzle. It belongs ON the noodles before the wok, where it can caramelise — not squirted over a finished plate.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Fresh wide rice noodles: chiller section of Asian grocers ("fresh rice noodle sheets" or ho fun), or the shelf-stable soft packs. Cut the sheets into 2cm strips yourself if needed.
  • คะน้า (Chinese kale / gai lan): standard in Asian grocers and bigger supermarkets. Tenderstem or spring greens work in a pinch — regular kale is too coarse.
  • Dark sweet soy: kecap manis or Thai/Chinese "dark soy sauce" — the sweet kind, not the salty black one.
  • Seasoning sauce (ซอสปรุงรส): the green-capped bottle (Golden Mountain or Healthy Boy brand) in Asian grocers. Light soy is an acceptable fallback.
  • Pork collar = pork neck; most UK butchers sell it. Thin slices, against the grain.
  • A proper wok on your highest burner beats any pan — the dish is the heat.
