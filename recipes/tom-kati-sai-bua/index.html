Recipe · Thailand · The Ancient Soup Of The Lotus Pond

Tom Kati Sai Bua (ต้มกะทิสายบัว)

A peppercorn, shallot and shrimp paste stirred into coconut milk that must never split, tamarind and palm sugar in balance, then crisp lotus stems and steamed mackerel — the old home soup that restaurants rarely bother to make.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 1 October 2026 · Serves 4 · ~30 minutes

The Soup That Grows In The Pond

สายบัว are lotus stems — the long, hollow tubes that run from the pond floor up to the flower, pulled out, peeled and eaten for their crispness. The soup takes its name from them: ต้ม, boiled; กะทิ, coconut milk; สายบัว, the stems. White stems are the ones you cook with; the pink ones (สายบัวหลวง) are young and tender enough for the table.

It is old food. The Pantip cook who wrote the fullest recipe calls it อาหารสมัยโบราณ — an ancient dish — and notes you will not find it on restaurant menus; it survives in home kitchens, the way his mother made it for him as a child. The heat is old too: white peppercorns pounded into the paste, not the chillies of modern Thai cooking. Most recipes stop there. One floats a few bird's eye chillies on top at the end, what he calls แอบแซ่บ — a secret kick.

The rule every cook repeats is about the coconut milk: it must not split. The Pantip cook and Kapook's editors both warn that once the milk breaks (แตกมัน) the soup is ruined — so the paste is fried in a little coconut milk over a low flame, the rest added gradually, and the pot is never boiled hard. The taste they aim for has a name: หวานซ่อนเปรี้ยว — sweet that hides a sour underneath. And the stems must stay crisp: bite one and the coconut broth squirts through the hollow centre.

The cookbook pot (Krua.co, from สำนักพิมพ์แสงแดด): ¼ tsp white peppercorns, 5 shallots and 1 tsp shrimp paste pounded to a paste; 3 cups coconut milk, 3 × 150 g steamed mackerel, 180 g stems cut into 2-inch pieces, seasoned with 2 tbsp + 1 tsp each of palm sugar, fish sauce and tamarind water — stems added raw, a ¼ cup of coconut cream drizzled on top before serving. The Kapook pot: no paste at all — 2 cups coconut milk, 6 whole shallots, 3 steamed mackerel, 500 g pre-blanched stems, seasoned with tamarind water, fish sauce and palm sugar, simmered gently five minutes; the page lists the family swaps: gourami, salted fish, salted crab, fresh prawns. The Wongnai pot (SUKANPHITHCHA): 300 g stems, 4 flaked steamed mackerel, a paste of 5–6 shallots, 1 tbsp peppercorns and ½ tbsp shrimp paste, thin coconut milk first then 1 cup of the cream, seasoned with 2–3 tbsp tamarind water, ½ tbsp each of sugar and salt. The Aroifin pot: 200 g stems, 1 mackerel, a blender paste of 1 tbsp white peppercorns, 5 shallots and 1 tbsp shrimp paste, 1 cup thin coconut milk plus 2 ladles of the cream, seasoned with 1 ladle of tamarind water, 1 tsp salt and 1 tbsp palm sugar.

The Method

  1. The stems. Peel the lotus stems and strip every fibre, then soak them in salted water. Blanch them in fiercely boiling water with a squeeze of lime — the acid keeps the stems green — for about a minute, then lift them into clean water. The Pantip cook does all three; Aroifin's tips add that young, pink stems are the tender ones, never astringent.
  2. The paste. Pound the white peppercorns with five of the shallots until fine, then work in the shrimp paste and pound smooth. The Pantip cook goes further: he wraps the shrimp paste in banana leaf and toasts it in a dry pan first, for the fragrance.
  3. The coconut milk. Over a low flame, add a ladle of the coconut milk and fry the paste in it until it smells — then gradually pour in the remaining coconut milk with the rest of the shallots, smashed. Keep the heat low: both the Pantip cook and Kapook say a coconut milk that splits has ruined the soup.
  4. The season. Add the tamarind water, palm sugar and salt and stir until dissolved. Taste — the target is หวานซ่อนเปรี้ยว, sweet on the tongue with the sour hiding behind it.
  5. The finish. Squeeze the water from the blanched stems and add them with the flaked mackerel. When the coconut milk comes back to a boil, everything is done. Float the whole bird's eye chillies on top at the very last second — the Pantip cook's secret kick — and serve with hot rice.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The paste — or none at all. Four of the five sources pound a paste of white peppercorns, shallots and shrimp paste (Pantip, Aroifin, Wongnai, and Krua.co's cookbook pot). Kapook's version skips it entirely and relies on whole shallots. The paste is the older road; the Kapook pot is the weeknight shortcut, and both call the result ต้มกะทิสายบัว.

Blanch or raw. Pantip, Aroifin and Kapook blanch the stems first — Pantip in lime water, expressly so the stems stay green and appetising. The Sangdad cookbook pot and Wongnai's add the stems raw to the coconut milk. Aroifin and Wongnai let the stems finish cooking in the soup either way; nobody wants a soft stem.

Salt or fish sauce. The Sangdad cookbook and Kapook season with fish sauce; Pantip, Aroifin and Wongnai use plain salt. Same salinity, two schools — the paste-heavy pots tend to salt, the paste-free pots to fish sauce.

The heat. Peppercorns carry the spice in every version, and three of the five use nothing else — the heat is pepper, not chilli. Pantip floats 10–15 whole bird's eye chillies on top at the end, what he calls แอบแซ่บ, and warns the dish is not one for children.

The coconut milk. Pantip and Kapook insist the milk must never split — once it breaks, the dish is spoiled. Wongnai and Aroifin stage the milk, thin (หางกะทิ) first, cream (หัวกะทิ) after; the Sangdad pot drizzles ¼ cup of cream over the finished soup. Different wording, same instinct: the milk is added in stages and never boiled hard.

The fish. Steamed mackerel everywhere — two (Pantip), three (Sangdad, Kapook), four flaked (Wongnai), one (Aroifin). Kapook's page lists the household swaps when mackerel is not to hand: gourami, salted fish, salted crab, fresh prawns. The soup takes whatever swims.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Boiling the coconut milk hard. Thai sources repeat it like a mantra: the milk must not split, or the soup is ruined. A western cook who treats this like a curry and boils it into submission gets grainy, oily milk.
  • Swapping the lotus stems. Bamboo shoots, broccoli, courgette — no. The stem is the dish: its hollow crunch, and the way the coconut broth sits inside it. Substituting the vegetable substitutes the name.
  • A chilli-forward paste. The heat here is white peppercorns. A modern Thai-style paste of chillies and garlic turns it into a different soup entirely.
  • Cooking the stems to softness. Western soup habits say cook until tender. Thai sources blanch briefly, add near the end, and want them crisp — Pantip describes the crunch (กรุบกรอบ) as the point.
  • Balancing to sweet. The Thai target is หวานซ่อนเปรี้ยว — sweet with sour hiding under it. Crank the sugar and the tamarind's job is gone.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Lotus stems (สายบัว): rarely fresh in the UK; the canned, brined kind at Asian grocers is the practical route — they are already cooked, so skip the blanch, drain well and add them only at the end so they keep their bite.
  • Steamed mackerel (ปลาทูนึ่ง): UK mackerel is the same fish — steam or grill fillets yourself and flake them in. Smoked mackerel works too, though it will deepen the flavour.
  • Shrimp paste (กะปิ): Thai grocers sell it in tubs; a little goes a long way, and the Pantip banana-leaf toast trick works in any dry pan.
  • Coconut milk: full-fat canned, one 400 ml tin plus a bit more for the three-cup measures; pour the thick cream from the top of the tin over the finished soup, Sangdad-style.
  • Tamarind: the block form at Asian grocers — soak a chunk in warm water and squeeze; ready tamarind paste in jars is the shortcut.
  • White peppercorns and palm sugar: any large supermarket; dark brown sugar stands in for palm sugar if needed.
