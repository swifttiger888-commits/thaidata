Yam Khao Tod (ยำข้าวทอด)

Crispy curry rice balls tossed hot with fermented sour pork, pork skin, ginger, shallots and a lime-fish-sauce dressing — a salad that crunches.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · 6 servings · ~45 minutes

The Salad Built on Rice Balls

ยำข้าวทอด flips the yam formula on its head: the "salad" is built not on meat or noodles but on khao tod — cooked rice kneaded with red curry paste, egg and slivered kaffir lime leaves, rolled into balls and deep-fried until the outside shatters and the middle stays soft. Those hot shards are tossed, while still crisp, with the dish's real engine: naem, the fermented sour pork sausage that brings its own lactic tang to the bowl. Ginger, shallots, boiled pork skin, roasted peanuts and a lime–fish-sauce–chilli dressing do the rest.

Thai sources are tellingly reluctant to settle on one name. Wongnai calls it ยำแหนมข้าวทอด — the naem named first, the rice balls second. Kapook serves the same bowl as แหนมคลุก, "tossed naem". Matichon splits the difference with ยำข้าวทอดแหนมคลุก. Whatever the label, the anatomy is constant: two things made separately — the rice balls and the sour pork — combined at the last second, so every spoonful carries crunch against chew against heat.

The technique has its picky bits. Aroifin insists the rice must be hard rice, never jasmine — jasmine turns the balls soggy and soft, so the crisp dies in the bowl. Matichon wants the batter water ice-cold for a shattering crust. Kapook's cooks pound the rice-and-paste mix until it turns sticky before shaping, so the balls hold their form in the oil. And if you are serious, Aroifin will have you make the naem yourself: minced pork, boiled skin, garlic and salt packed airtight and left at room temperature for one to two days — the shortest fermentation in Thai cooking, and the one this dish leans on entirely.

Kapook's แหนมคลุก: 200 g naem, 200 g boiled pork skin, shallot, young ginger and sawtooth coriander, served on betel leaves — and its rice balls are only dusted with rice flour, no batter. TrueID's selling batch (ครัวพิศพิไล): 100 g minced pork and 150 g pork skin kneaded inside the rice balls, with an egg-and-batter double coat. Aroifin's from-scratch version: homemade naem (500 g pork, 2 days' ferment) plus a hard-rice rule for the balls — 150 g rice, 50 g grated coconut, 1 tbsp curry paste, 6 tbsp crispy flour with 6 tbsp cold water. Matichon: ice-cold batter water and a heavier dressing of 2 tbsp each of chilli flakes, fish sauce and lime juice.

The Method

  1. The rice balls. Mix the cooked rice with the curry paste and egg, season with the fish sauce and sugar, then knead in the finely sliced kaffir lime leaves and shape into tight balls. Kapook's cooks pound the mix until sticky first so the balls hold; Aroifin's rule: hard rice, never jasmine, or the crisp dies.
  2. The coating. Whisk the crispy frying flour with the water — Matichon wants it ice-cold — dip each ball and deep-fry until golden and crunchy, then drain. Kapook's shortcut skips the batter entirely: a dusting of rice flour and straight into the oil.
  3. The naem. Crumble the fresh naem into the bowl with three of the fried rice balls and crush them roughly — break them into shards, don't mash them to crumbs. TrueID's version kneads the minced pork and pork skin into the balls themselves before frying.
  4. The dressing. Add the sliced pork skin and minced pork, season with the lime juice, fish sauce and chilli flakes, and toss. Kapook's home cook swaps the pork skin for blanched minced pork and lightly steams the naem first.
  5. The finish. Fold in the shallots, ginger, spring onion, coriander, fried peanuts and fried chillies, and serve immediately with fresh vegetables — lettuce, betel leaves, mint. The rice must still crunch when it reaches the table; wait, and the bowl goes soft.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Coconut in the rice. Wongnai kneads ½ cup of fresh grated coconut into the rice mix, and Aroifin adds 50 g of grated coconut; Kapook, TrueID and Matichon use none. Wongnai offers breadcrumbs as a substitute; the others simply leave it out.

Where the pork goes. TrueID kneads 100 g of minced pork and 150 g of sliced pork skin into the rice balls before frying — and then adds more skin to the salad. Wongnai, Kapook and Matichon keep all the pork in the bowl, and Kapook's minced-pork version drops the skin entirely in favour of blanched minced pork.

How the balls are coated. Wongnai, TrueID, Aroifin and Matichon all dip in a crispy-flour batter (Aroifin: 6 tbsp flour to 6 tbsp cold water; Matichon insists the water be ice-cold). TrueID goes further, alternating flour batter with beaten egg in layers. Kapook alone skips the batter — a rice-flour dusting and straight into the oil.

The naem: bought, cooked, or homemade. Every recipe but one buys ready-made แหนมสด. Kapook's home-cook version lightly steams it first, and the cook's daughter stir-fries it in a little water until cooked through. Aroifin makes the naem from scratch — 500 g pork with garlic, salt and sticky rice, fermented one to two days — and uses the leftovers for khao pad naem.

The rice itself. Aroifin is alone in specifying the variety, and emphatic: hard rice, never jasmine — jasmine goes soggy and the balls lose their crisp. The other sources just say "cooked rice", leaving the choice to the cook.

The name. The same bowl is ยำแหนมข้าวทอด (Wongnai), แหนมคลุก (Kapook, Aroifin) and ยำข้าวทอดแหนมคลุก (Matichon) — the naem is the constant, the rice balls the variable worth arguing over.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Omitting the naem. Substituting bacon, ham or "sour sausage" from the supermarket for naem removes the dish's engine. The fermentation is the sourness — the lime in the dressing only sharpens what the naem already carries. No naem, no yam khao tod.
  • Curry powder in the rice. The balls take red curry paste — lemongrass, galangal, dried chilli, shrimp paste — not curry powder. Powder gives dusty heat and none of the paste's depth; the rice balls taste of nothing but turmeric.
  • Not frying the rice. Western "rice salads" serve the rice soft. Here the rice is deep-fried to a shatter-crisp shell and tossed while hot. Serve soft rice and the dish collapses into cold, dressed rice.
  • Mashing the balls to crumbs. Every Thai source says break the fried balls roughly — shards, not powder. Crumbs absorb the dressing and turn to paste; shards stay crisp between the teeth.
  • Sweetening the dressing. The Thai balance is hot, sour, salty with a teaspoon of sugar to round it. Bottled sweet chilli sauce or a honey-heavy dressing buries the naem's sourness, which is the whole point.
  • Dressing ahead of time. The salad is tossed at the last second and eaten immediately. Dressing it in advance — even ten minutes — turns the crisp rice soft and the dish into leftovers.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Naem (แหนม): sold refrigerated at Thai grocers as "naem" or "fermented pork sausage" — usually in banana-leaf or plastic wraps, sometimes with raw garlic and bird's eye chillies packed inside. It is eaten raw in this dish; check the pack date.
  • Red curry paste: Mae Ploy, Mae Sri and Cock brands are standard in UK supermarkets and Thai grocers — the same paste Thai home cooks reach for.
  • Kaffir lime leaves: frozen year-round at Thai grocers; slice them as finely as you can — they are eaten, not just perfumed.
  • Crispy frying flour (แป้งทอดกรอบ): Thai brands at grocers, or any tempura mix; plain flour whisked with ice-cold water works nearly as well.
  • Pork skin: buy raw pork rind from a butcher and boil until tender, then slice into thin strips; or take the minced-pork route (Kapook's version) and skip it.
  • Young ginger (ขิงอ่อน): Thai grocers carry it in season; regular ginger, julienned thin, is the everyday stand-in.
  • Sawtooth coriander (ผักชีฝรั่ง) and betel leaves (ใบชะพลู): Thai grocers; sawtooth coriander can be swapped for fresh coriander, betel leaves for lettuce cups.
  • Fresh grated coconut: frozen grated coconut at Asian grocers, or rehydrated unsweetened desiccated coconut.
