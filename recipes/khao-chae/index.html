Khao Chae (ข้าวแช่)

Parboiled jasmine rice chilled in candle-smoked flower water, eaten with a ring of tiny side dishes — shrimp-paste fritters, stuffed peppers, sweet fried turnip — the court's answer to April heat.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 30 September 2026 · Serves 2–4 · ~2¼ hours + overnight scenting

The Rice That Sleeps In Flowers

ข้าวแช่ means rice soaked in water, and the name understates the dish. The rice is parboiled, steamed, cooled and scented with jasmine; the water is flower-floated and candle-smoked for up to two nights; and around the bowl sits a court of side dishes, each one sweet-savoury, each one a small project of its own.

Every Thai source traces the dish to the Mon people, who called it เปิงด้าจก์ — rice water — and cooked it at Songkran as an offering to the gods and to their elders, a food considered pure and cooling. The palace kitchens refined it into the elaborate set-piece TrueID's recipe describes: luuk kapi fritters that keep for weeks, shallots stuffed with the same paste, sweet peppers stuffed with pork and covered in a lattice of fried egg, sweet fried turnip, and carved vegetables — krachai shaped like champak blossom, chillies like cucumber, sour mango like leaves.

It remains a hot-season dish. Sriwangying notes it is eaten only around Songkran in the Mon communities of Ratchaburi and Pathum Thani, and the restaurants that sell it in Bangkok open their khao chae seasons in April and close them by May.

The Wongnai pot: the same core — 2 cups rice, 1 litre jasmine water, peppers stuffed with pork and prawn, luuk kapi with grilled catfish, sweet fried turnip — and it adds the practical touch that a little water in the pan stops the palm sugar seizing. The Krua.co pot (Sangdad): aged jasmine rice soaked overnight in diluted alum for clear grains, then parboiled, rubbed free of its starch coat, steamed and sprinkled with jasmine water. The Mon pot (Sriwangying): no fixed list at all — sweet fried fish, sweet fried pork, coconut-milk turnip, fried noodles, mango and jackfruit salads, and watermelon on the side.

The Method

  1. The water. Start this a day ahead. Float jasmine flowers — petals only, calyx removed, TrueID insists — on clean water, float a lit candle on top, cover and leave until the candle burns out. Two nights makes a noticeably deeper scent; the rice and the water both borrow it.
  2. The rice. Parboil rinsed jasmine rice for 3–5 minutes until the grains are nearly cooked but still firm at the centre — Thai cooks call this stage ตากบ, frog's eye, and Krua.co treats it as the test of the whole dish. Drain and steam in muslin for 10 minutes.
  3. The scent. Sprinkle the hot rice with the flower water, spread it to cool, then seal it with fresh jasmine flowers. The rice must stay whole and firm — TrueID's teacher is blunt: steamed rice that has bloomed open is wrong for khao chae.
  4. The fritters (ลูกกะปิ). Pound lemongrass, fingerroot and shallots with the shrimp paste and grilled catfish flesh until it becomes one paste. Fry it in a little coconut milk with coconut sugar until dry and glossy, leave it to cool, then roll into small balls. Kapook and Wongnai both coat: dip in beaten duck egg, roll in glutinous rice flour (Wongnai uses rice flour), and deep-fry.
  5. The stuffed peppers. Pound coriander root, garlic and white pepper; mix with minced pork and prawn meat until sticky. Stuff the deseeded peppers, steam for 10 minutes, cool completely, then egg-dip and fry. TrueID's court version finishes them with a lattice of fried egg, and the same filling can stuff shallots instead.
  6. The turnip. Fry shredded salted turnip with crushed garlic over low heat, add palm sugar and keep frying until the strands turn glossy and sticky. This one is the sweet end of the table.
  7. The table. Chill everything. Put a spoonful of rice in each bowl, pour over ice-cold flower water, scatter a few fresh petals, and eat the rice with small pieces of the sides — a bite of fritter, a bite of pepper, a bite of turnip — between spoonfuls.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The rice treatment. Kapook and Wongnai simply steam the rice. Krua.co's Sangdad recipe soaks it overnight in diluted alum, parboils it, rubs the soft outer starch off under running water, then steams — the rice comes out glassy, with every grain separate. Both camps agree on the frog's-eye test: stop the boil while the grain is still firm at the centre.

The scenting method. Kapook and TrueID smoke the water with a candle floated on the surface. Sriwangying reports Mon households where no candle is used — flowers are simply floated, and the fragrance does the work. TrueID adds flowers Kapook doesn't: Mon rose and chommanard, with the scent of ylang-ylang as the court standard.

How many side dishes. TrueID's teacher counts the palace set — fritters, stuffed shallots, stuffed peppers under egg lattice, sweet turnip, sometimes shredded sour mango. Wongnai's home set is three dishes and openly says add sweet pork floss (หมูฝอย) if you like. The Mon original, per Sriwangying, has no fixed list at all: whatever the river and the garden give that week.

The fritter binder. Kapook rolls the luuk kapi in glutinous rice flour and duck egg; Wongnai uses plain rice flour and duck egg; both add lime water (น้ำปูนใส) to the mix. The paste itself differs too — Kapook allows either grilled catfish or gourami flesh, Wongnai specifies catfish.

The water itself. TrueID's recipe begins with rainwater, or tap water left out overnight to lose its chlorine — no ice if you can manage a clay pot, which cools the water on its own. Kapook doesn't fuss over the source; it wants the candle smoke. Modern sources quietly allow ice.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Calling it "rice in iced water" and stopping there. The rice and the water are the frame, not the painting. Without the side dishes — especially the luuk kapi fritters — it is cold rice soup, and every Thai source treats the sides as the dish's whole point.
  • Hot rice. Western habits say rice should be hot. Khao chae must be cold — rice chilled, water ice-cold, sides at room temperature. Warm rice in the bowl is the one sure way to ruin it.
  • Mushy grains. Normal steamed rice, cooked through, is wrong here. The rice is deliberately under-boiled and finished in steam so the grains stay firm and separate in the water — TrueID and Krua.co both say bloomed rice is a failed khao chae.
  • Jasmine essence or syrup in the water. The scent comes from flowers and candle smoke, not from flavourings. Scented-water shortcuts taste of perfume, and any sugar in the water is an invention — the sweetness lives in the side dishes.
  • Skipping the time. The water wants a night or two; the rice wants to cool slowly with flowers on it. Western quick versions that scent and serve the same afternoon taste like cold rice, because that is what they are.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Jasmine flowers: grow your own or buy pesticide-free blooms — they must be edible-grade since they sit in the water you drink. In winter, food-grade dried jasmine will scent the water, though less deeply.
  • Kapi (shrimp paste): any Thai grocer, the same jar you would use for a nam phrik. Kapook's fish — grilled catfish or gourami — can be swapped for grilled mackerel or smoked trout flesh.
  • Fingerroot (krachai): fresh is rare in the UK; frozen bags are common at Thai supermarkets and fine here. Lemongrass and shallots are everywhere.
  • Salted turnip (หัวไชโป๊): vacuum-packed at Chinese and Thai grocers; rinse the salt off before frying, or the sweet fry turns salty.
  • Lime water (น้ำปูนใส): food-grade pickling lime from a bakery supplier; it keeps the fritters crisp. A pinch of bicarbonate in the frying batter is not a true substitute, but it is the accepted shortcut.
  • Duck eggs: Waitrose, farm shops and Chinese supermarkets. If you can't find them, large hen's eggs work — the coating is thinner, so double-dip the fritters.
