Recipe · Thailand · The Sour Curry With No Orange

Gaeng Som (แกงส้ม)

Prawn meat pounded into the chilli paste, tamarind and lime in the same pot, prawns curled to a C and no further. The name means "sour curry" — there is no orange in it.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 26 September 2026 · Serves 3–4 · ~40 minutes

The Curry Named for Its Sourness

แกงส้ม means "sour curry" — and the joke writes itself, because there is no orange anywhere near it. A Lemon8 recipe is literally titled "gaeng som with no orange in it." What the name promises is exactly what the dish delivers: sour leads, salt follows, sweet only rounds the edges.

Gaeng som is also two curries under one name. The south's version is built on a yellow paste — dried chillies, fresh turmeric, garlic, shrimp paste — and comes out fierce and hot-sour. The central version swaps the turmeric for lemongrass and krachai, runs redder, and usually rides with a cha-om omelette simmered in the broth. Both share the trick that makes the dish: protein pounded into the paste. As one home cook puts it, "pounding the meat into the paste is the heart of the dish" — it's the thickener, and the flavour.

The Southern Yellow Paste (พริกแกงเหลือง — from a Nakhon Si Thammarat teacher)

Dried jinda chillies 30 (or fresh bird's eye), Thai garlic 30 cloves, fresh turmeric 1 piece (about 2 inches), shrimp paste 1 tbsp, salt 1 tsp.

Pound the chillies with salt until broken, add the turmeric and garlic, pound fine, then work in the shrimp paste until smooth. No lemongrass, no krachai — turmeric is the whole character. The southern curry runs hotter and sharper; fish and vegetables go in by season and by fridge.

Both pastes keep in the fridge for days — and the southern one is worth making double of.

The Method

  1. Pound the paste. Dried chillies with the salt until broken; lemongrass, krachai and shallots in; shrimp paste in; then the blanched prawn meat, pounded until smooth. The protein is the thickener and the heart of the dish.
  2. The cha-om omelette. Eggs beaten with the cha-om tips, fried over medium heat into a thick, fluffy omelette, cut into bite-size squares. Medium heat — high heat makes it tough.
  3. Build the broth. Water to the boil, paste dissolved in. Tamarind water, fish sauce, palm sugar — sour first, salty second, sweet rounding the end.
  4. Soak the omelette. Squares in, gentle simmer 3–5 minutes so the broth soaks into the egg.
  5. Prawns, briefly. Rolling boil, prawns in. The moment they curl into a C and change colour, off the heat. A tight O is overcooked — the C is the whole point.
  6. Lime last. Squeezed in at the very end over the tamarind sourness — fresh sharp on top of deep soft. Taste once more, serve hot with fried salted fish, or over khanom jeen rice noodles.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

South vs centre. The south's turmeric-yellow paste against the centre's lemongrass-krachai red one. Both are gaeng som; neither is the "wrong" one — the recipe changes at the border.

The sour. Tamarind alone, or tamarind plus a lime squeeze at the end. The two-sour finish is the modern standard; purists stay with tamarind.

The protein. Prawns, fish, or both — and whether the omelette belongs in the pot at all. Fish versions skip the cha-om entirely.

Heat. Southern versions run genuinely hot; central versions are friendlier. Adjust by your chillies, not by apologising.

What it's eaten with. Jasmine rice is standard; over khanom jeen rice noodles is the southern habit; fried salted fish on the side is non-negotiable everywhere.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Sweet-lead. Gaeng som is a sour curry. If the first thing you taste is sugar, it isn't this dish.
  • Thin, watery soup. The prawn meat pounded into the paste is the thickener. Skip it and the broth stays flat and thin.
  • Overcooked prawns. A tight O-shaped prawn is dead prawn. In at the boil, out at the C.
  • Orange juice. The literal reading of the name — the documented joke. The sour comes from tamarind and lime, never fruit.
  • One sour only. Tamarind gives depth, the end-of-pot lime gives the sharp top note. Both belong.
  • No salt fish. The fried salted fish on the side is the counterpoint — the dish is served with it, not optionally.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Krachai (fingerroot): fresh or frozen in Thai grocers. It has no real substitute — use more lemongrass if you can't find it, but get it if you can.
  • Cha-om: the hard one — fresh in Thai grocers when in season, frozen sometimes. If it's unavailable, make a fish version instead of faking the omelette.
  • Fresh turmeric: Asian grocers and better supermarkets. The southern paste depends on it — don't swap for powder.
  • Dried chillies: the long mild-red ones plus a few small hot ones for balance. Soak them properly or they won't pound.
  • Tamarind: block or paste from Asian grocers; the block soaked in hot water gives the cleanest sour.
  • Fried salted fish: any Asian grocer's salted mackerel or dried fish, fried crisp — it's the side, not a garnish.
