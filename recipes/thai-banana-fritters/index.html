Thai Banana Fritters (กล้วยแขก)

Nam wah bananas sliced lengthwise, a rice-flour batter with coconut and sesame, a spoon of lime water for the crunch — and pandan leaves frying in the oil. The street vendor's art, by the bag.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 4–6 · ~45 minutes

The Fritters of the Street Vendors

The name is the history: กล้วยแขก means "guest bananas" — แขก being the old Thai word for Indian and Muslim visitors. The fritters arrived with those communities' street vendors, and they never left. Every Thai market still has a stall frying them in a wide pan of oil with pandan leaves floating in it, turning the whole street fragrant.

The banana matters more than the batter. Thai cooks use กล้วยน้ำว้า — the nam wah banana, short and firm and barely ripe, "ห่าม" — just past green. Slice a ripe Cavendish lengthwise and it collapses into mush in the oil; the nam wah holds its shape and stays starchy-sweet inside the crisp shell. This is not a banana you can casually swap.

And the shell has one quiet trick: น้ำปูนใส — clear lime water. A spoonful of the clear liquid from settled edible lime paste raises the batter's pH and keeps the crust crisp long after it leaves the oil. Both Thai sources below use it. It's the difference between a crack and a chew.

The Method

  1. Dry mix. Rice flour, corn flour, sugar, salt and baking powder together; evaporated milk whisked in until smooth.
  2. Lime water. Stirred in next — the crispness insurance.
  3. Coconut and sesame. Folded through until evenly distributed.
  4. Slice. Bananas peeled and cut lengthwise, half to one centimetre thick — thick enough to hold, thin enough to cook through.
  5. Oil and pandan. Medium heat, pandan leaves into the oil. They perfume everything that follows.
  6. Fry. Each slice dipped in batter, fried in batches, turned once, until golden. Drain on paper.
  7. Eat warm. The crack is a fresh thing — these do not improve by sitting.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

The flour. Rice flour plus a little corn flour (Wongnai) against the shop shortcut of ready-made crispy fry flour (aroifin). The ready flour works; the two-flour mix is the recipe you can read.

The liquid. Evaporated milk (Wongnai) against head coconut milk (aroifin). The milk version is the street standard; the coconut version is richer and slightly softer-shelled.

Salt. A full tablespoon (Wongnai's salty-sweet street profile) against a quarter teaspoon (aroifin). We've transcribed the source amount and flagged it — start at half if you're not sure.

Lime water. Both sources use it, in different amounts. It is not optional for the texture; only the dose is argued.

Banana ripeness. Everyone says ห่าม — just-ripe. A fully ripe banana fritter is a different, softer thing.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Cavendish bananas. Too soft and too wet — they collapse in the oil. The nam wah is the dish, not an interchangeable fruit.
  • Pancake batter. A thick western batter makes a doughnut around the banana. Gluay khaek is a thin crisp shell with coconut and sesame in it.
  • No lime water. Skip it and the crust goes soft within minutes. The chemistry is the point.
  • No pandan. The leaves in the oil are the street-stall perfume. Without them the fritters smell of nothing but hot oil.
  • Cinnamon sugar dusting. The batter already carries sugar and salt. Cinnamon is a different country's fried banana.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • The banana, honestly: nam wah comes from Thai grocers. If you can only get Cavendish, pick the firmest, barely-yellow ones and slice them thicker — the texture won't match the real thing, and that's the trade.
  • Rice flour and corn flour: standard supermarket stock.
  • Lime water: Thai grocers sell edible lime paste (ปูนแดง); dissolve a piece in water, let it settle, use only the clear top. If you skip it, eat the fritters immediately — the crispness won't wait.
  • Shredded coconut: frozen grated coconut from Asian grocers is closest to the Thai ingredient. Desiccated coconut works in the batter but stays drier and chewier.
  • Pandan: frozen leaves in Thai grocers.
  • Serving: warm, same day, ideally straight from the pan — the street stalls don't keep them either.
