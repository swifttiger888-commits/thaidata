Recipe · Thailand · The Canal Bowl

Guay Tiew Ruea (ก๋วยเตี๋ยวเรือ)

The noodle bowl that was sold from boats — broth dark with spice and blood, pork and liver and crackling, served in bowls small enough to stack.

By Johnny Tiger · Published 27 September 2026 · Serves 4 · ~2½ hours

The Bowl That Rode the Canals

ก๋วยเตี๋ยวเรือ — "boat noodles" — is named for its origin: vendors who cooked and sold from narrow boats on Bangkok's canals, serving in small bowls so nothing spilled when the boat rocked. The bowls stayed small even after the boats moved onto land, and the dish kept its identity: a broth so dark and dense it's practically a sauce.

The darkness is the recipe. The Wongnai formula runs pork spine under a muslin bag of star anise, cinnamon, Sichuan pepper, coriander seed and cumin, then the defining Thai additions: เต้าหู้ยี้, fermented tofu with its brine, dark soy, seasoning sauce, pickled garlic brine — and a splash of coconut milk. Simmered one to two hours with the scum skimmed, the broth comes out mahogany-dark and clings to the noodles.

The service is the other signature. Pork blood goes into the bowl raw and the hot broth sets it — the Wongnai method — adding body and iron depth, then the noodles, bean sprouts, water spinach, sliced pork and liver, with fried garlic and pork crackling on top. The Kapook Ayutthaya version is the regional cousin: same herb bag, but a proud splash of coconut milk and either pork spine or beef bones depending on the shop.

To serve: rice noodles, sliced pork, liver, bean sprouts, water spinach, pork blood, fried garlic, pork crackling.

The Method

  1. The spice bag. Cinnamon, star anise, Sichuan pepper, coriander seeds and cumin tied into muslin.
  2. The long simmer. Water to a hard boil, spine in; when it boils again, the spice bag, seasoning sauce, coconut milk, dark soy, fermented tofu with brine, pickled garlic brine and pandan. Gentle simmer, skimming, 1–2 hours until deep and dark.
  3. The toppings. Blanch noodles, bean sprouts and water spinach; slice the pork and liver.
  4. Blood first, then broth. Blood into each bowl, hot broth poured over to set it, then noodles, greens, pork, liver. Fried garlic and crackling to finish.

Where Thai Sources Disagree

Coconut milk or not. The Ayutthaya style is known for its coconut milk; the Wongnai recipe also carries 100 ml; the plainer Bangkok versions skip it. The milk rounds the broth's edge; without it the bowl is sharper and blacker.

Pork or beef. The Ayutthaya source builds the broth on pork spine for the pork version and beef bones for the beef version. The classic boat bowl is pork — liver and all.

The blood. Wongnai sets raw blood with the hot broth in the bowl; other shops simmer blood cubes in the pot. Both thicken the broth; the bowl-set version is silkier.

Sugar. The Ayutthaya tips insist on real coconut sugar for the roundness of the broth; the Wongnai recipe sweetens through the seasoning sauce alone. The coconut-sugar note marks the Ayutthaya style.

Bowl size. The Kapook 2025 roundup notes the shops sell bowls in small sizes — the boat habit that never left. A full dinner bowl of this broth is a lot; the real thing comes small and stacked.

How Western Versions Get It Wrong

  • Pho or beef pho seasoning. Boat noodles are darker, sweeter and funkier — fermented tofu and pickled garlic, not fish-sauce-cinnamon pho.
  • Skipping the blood. The blood is the dish's depth and its name-level identity. Without it the broth is a plain dark soup.
  • Quick broth. The 1–2 hour simmer with the spice bag is the minimum. A 20-minute version tastes of raw star anise.
  • Big bowls. The dish was born small. Serving it as one giant bowl loses the rhythm of the original.
  • No crackling. Pork crackling and fried garlic are the finish — texture against the smooth broth.

UK Ingredient Notes

  • Pork spine (เล้ง): Thai/Chinese butchers sell pork neck bones — the gelatinous cut the broth needs.
  • Fermented tofu (เต้าหู้ยี้): jars at Chinese grocers, cubes in brine.
  • Pickled garlic brine: from a jar of Thai pickled garlic.
  • Pork blood: fresh blocks at Thai grocers — or simmer blood cubes into the pot if you prefer it cooked through.
  • Pork crackling and fried garlic: both from Thai grocers; don't skip either.
