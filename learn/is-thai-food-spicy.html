Is Thai Food Spicy? Not Always

A practical guide to Thai heat levels — which dishes are mild, which ones bring the fire, and how to order with confidence at any UK Thai restaurant.

The Short Answer

No, Thai food is not always spicy. In fact, many of the most popular Thai dishes in the UK — Pad Thai, Massaman curry, chicken satay — are mild. Thai cooking is built around balance: sweet, sour, salty, and spicy working together. Heat is one tool in the kit, not the whole kit.

The idea that all Thai food is fiery comes from a few famous dishes (green curry, Tom Yum, papaya salad) and the visual of chillies on a Thai table. But in practice, Thai diners control their own heat. The kitchen provides a balanced base, and every table gets a condiment tray — chillies, fish sauce, vinegar, sugar — so each person adjusts each bite to their own taste.

Mild Thai Dishes — Where to Start

If you want to enjoy Thai food without the burn, these dishes are naturally mild and focus on flavour over heat.

Pad Thai — stir-fried rice noodles with tamarind, peanuts, egg, and bean sprouts. Sweet-tangy, zero chilli heat by default.

Massaman curry — a mild, peanut-influenced curry with Persian origins. Slow-cooked with potatoes, onions, and coconut milk. Warm rather than hot.

Chicken satay — grilled marinated chicken skewers served with peanut dipping sauce and cucumber relish. No chillies involved.

Tom Kha Gai — coconut milk soup with galangal, lemongrass, and chicken. Creamy, aromatic, and mild — the gentler cousin of Tom Yum.

Thai fried rice (khao pad) — jasmine rice stir-fried with egg, onion, and your choice of meat. Served with cucumber and lime. No spice unless you add it.

Stir-fried vegetables in oyster sauce — mixed seasonal greens in a savoury, slightly sweet sauce. A staple side dish with zero heat.

Spring rolls and fish cakes — starters that come with sweet chilli dipping sauce on the side, so you control the heat yourself.

The Spiciest Thai Dishes — What Brings the Heat

These dishes are built around chillies. If you order them, expect noticeable heat — though most UK restaurants can dial it down on request.

Green curry (gaeng keow wan) — the name means "sweet green curry" but don't be fooled. Fresh green chillies in the paste give it a sharp, bright heat. Coconut milk softens it but doesn't neutralise it.

Tom Yum — Thailand's iconic hot and sour soup. Birds-eye chillies, lemongrass, and lime create a clear, punchy broth. The heat hits immediately.

Jungle curry (gaeng pa) — the hottest Thai curry. Unlike green or red curry, it has no coconut milk to cushion the heat. Made with fresh chillies, wild ginger, and peppercorns.

Papaya salad (som tam) — shredded green papaya pounded in a mortar with chillies, garlic, lime, fish sauce, and tomatoes. The heat varies — a proper som tam with 3-5 birds-eye chillies is seriously spicy.

Laab (minced meat salad) — a northeastern Thai salad of minced pork or chicken with roasted rice powder, lime, fish sauce, and ground dried chillies. The heat is dry and builds slowly.

Drunken noodles (pad kee mao) — wide rice noodles stir-fried with fresh chillies, garlic, basil, and vegetables. Named not because it contains alcohol but because it's spicy enough to cure a hangover.

How to Order Thai Food Less Spicy

UK Thai restaurants are used to adjusting heat levels. Here is how to ask — and what to expect.

Say "mild please" or "mai phet" (my pet — "not spicy" in Thai). Most restaurants understand and will reduce or omit fresh chillies from your dish.

Use the 1–5 spice scale. Many UK Thai restaurants let you pick a number. Start at 1 (very mild) or 2 (light warmth). Level 3 is medium — the default for most Thai diners. Level 5 is Thai-hot — what the kitchen staff make for themselves.

Choose the right base dish. Dishes with coconut milk (Massaman, red curry, panang) can be made much milder than clear-soup dishes (Tom Yum) or chilli-paste stir-fries (pad prik). The coconut fat naturally tames the capsaicin.

Eat with rice. Plain jasmine rice softens chilli heat. Thai meals are designed so every bite of spicy food is balanced by rice. This is how Thai diners manage heat — not by drinking water, which spreads the capsaicin, but by eating more rice.

The Condiment Tray — Your Heat Control Panel

Every Thai table has a condiment tray. This is not decoration — it is how Thai diners tune each bite.

When you sit down at a Thai restaurant, you'll often see small jars or dishes containing:

Fresh or pickled chillies — add heat to individual bites. Use sparingly.

Fish sauce — deepens saltiness and umami. Good for soups and rice dishes that taste flat.

Chilli vinegar or plain vinegar — adds sourness and brightness. Cuts through rich, oily dishes.

Sugar — rounds off sharp edges. A tiny pinch can soften a bite that's too salty or sour.

Ground chilli or chilli flakes — pure dry heat. Add to anything you want spicier.

The key idea: the kitchen gives you a balanced base. You finish it at the table. This means you can keep your food mild while your dining partner dials theirs up — no awkward negotiation with the waiter needed.

Does Spiciness Vary by Region?

Yes. Southern Thai food is the spiciest — dishes from Phuket, Krabi, and the Malay-influenced south use fresh turmeric, black pepper, and more birds-eye chillies than other regions. Northeastern (Isaan) food is also fiery — laab, som tam, and grilled meats with chilli dipping sauces pack serious heat.

Central Thai food (Bangkok and the surrounding plains) is the most balanced and what most UK restaurants serve. Northern Thai food (Chiang Mai, Chiang Rai) is generally milder than southern or northeastern cooking — curries are less chilli-heavy and more herb-driven.

The good news: most UK Thai restaurants cook in the central Thai style, which means heat is moderate and adjustable. If a restaurant specialises in Isaan or southern Thai food, expect more heat — but they will usually warn you or offer to adjust.
